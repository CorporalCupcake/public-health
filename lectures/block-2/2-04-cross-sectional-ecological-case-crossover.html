Lecture 2.4

Cross-sectional, ecological and case-crossover designs

Block 2 · Epidemiology
The point

Three designs that each trade something away. A cross-sectional study measures exposure and disease at one moment, which is cheap but blurs which came first. An ecological study compares groups rather than people, which suits group-level questions but can mislead about individuals. A case-crossover study compares each case with themselves at other times, which removes every fixed personal confounder but works only for short-lived exposures and sudden outcomes.

1. Cross-sectional studies: a snapshot

A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome in a sample at one point in time, so it estimates prevalence, not incidence.

What it is
A survey of a defined population at one time: who has the condition now, and what are they like now. National health surveys, screening surveys and most studies of salt intake from 24-hour urine are cross-sectional.
Why it matters
It is the main source of the numbers a health system plans with: how many adults have hypertension, how many smoke, how much salt people eat. For that descriptive job, a well-sampled cross-sectional survey is the right design, not a weak one.
How to read it
First check the sampling: a random, weighted national sample supports “X% of Saudi adults”; a clinic sample does not. Then check measurement: measured blood pressure or HbA1c beats self-report. Associations are reported as prevalence ratios or prevalence odds ratios.
What a change means
A rise in prevalence between two surveys can mean more new cases, or the same number of cases living longer (Lecture 1.4’s bathtub). A snapshot can’t tell you which.
Saudi Arabia

The 2013 Saudi Health Interview Survey, run by the Ministry of Health with the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, interviewed and examined a national multistage sample of about 10,700 people aged 15 and over. It is a classic cross-sectional survey: excellent for prevalence of diabetes, hypertension and smoking, silent on what caused what. The studies you will pool in Project 2, on 24-hour urinary sodium in the GCC, are almost all cross-sectional too, which is exactly what an estimate of average intake needs.

2. Which came first?

When exposure and disease are measured at the same moment, a disease can change the exposure, and the association runs backwards.

What it is
Reverse causation: the outcome causes the exposure, rather than the reverse. In a snapshot, both are present together and you cannot see the order.
Why it matters
Diagnosed people get advice and change their behaviour. People told they have hypertension are advised to cut salt; people with diabetes switch to diet drinks; people with heart disease stop exercising hard. In a cross-sectional survey each of these makes a harmful exposure look protective or a harmless one look harmful.
How to read it
Ask whether the exposure is fixed (genotype, sex, place of birth) or could have changed after disease. Fixed exposures are safe in a snapshot; changeable ones are suspect.
Trap

Reading “people with hypertension eat less salt than people without” as evidence that salt doesn’t matter. The diagnosed group has been told to cut down. Restricting to people who don’t yet know their status, or using a cohort, is how to recover the order.

Analogy

A photo of a car park shows that cars with flat tyres are more often parked near the tyre shop. Without the film, you can’t tell whether the shop causes flats or flats bring cars to the shop.

3. Prevalent cases are survivors

A snapshot over-represents long-lasting cases, so any factor that changes how long people live with a disease distorts a prevalence comparison.

What it is
Prevalence-incidence bias (also called Neyman bias, or survival bias): people who died quickly, or recovered quickly, are missing from the snapshot. The prevalence odds ratio mixes the effect on getting the disease with the effect on how long it lasts.
Why it matters
It affects every study that recruits existing cases, including case-control studies that use prevalent cases. If an exposure makes a disease more often fatal, prevalent cases will under-represent the exposed, and the exposure can look protective.
How to read it
In a stable population the prevalence odds ratio is roughly the incidence rate ratio multiplied by the ratio of average disease durations:
\[ \text{prevalence odds ratio} \approx \text{rate ratio} \times \frac{\text{duration in exposed}}{\text{duration in unexposed}} \]
Example

Suppose smoking doubles the rate of heart attacks and also makes a first attack more often fatal. A survey of people living after a heart attack meets fewer smokers than were struck, because more smokers died. If smokers survive with the condition half as long on average, the prevalence odds ratio comes out near 2 × ½ = 1. Smoking would look harmless. Illustrative numbers.

Trap

Using “cases attending the diabetes clinic” as cases. They are survivors who stayed in care. Use newly diagnosed (incident) cases wherever you can.

4. Ecological studies

An ecological study compares groups (countries, regions, years) using group averages, and answers questions about groups.

What it is
The unit of analysis is a population, not a person. Exposure is a group measure (average salt sales, tax rate, air pollution level); outcome is a group rate (stroke mortality). You never know which individuals were exposed or got ill.
Why it matters
It is cheap, uses routine data, and is sometimes the only option. For group-level exposures it is also the right level: a tax, a law, a reformulation policy or a city’s air quality acts on whole populations.
How to read it
Ask whether the question is about groups (“did countries that taxed sugary drinks see sales fall?”) or about people (“do individuals who drink more get diabetes?”). Ecological data can answer the first. For the second they are only a lead.
What a change means
With few groups, a handful of unusual ones can make the correlation. Six GCC countries give six points; any comparison rests on very little.
Example

In 1975, Armstrong and Doll correlated national cancer rates with national diet across dozens of countries. Countries with higher fat intake had much higher breast cancer rates. The authors presented the correlations as leads to test in individual-level studies, and later individual-level studies gave a far weaker and more mixed picture. Rich countries differ from poor ones in fat intake and in reproductive patterns, screening and much else.

Project 4

Your study of GCC sugary-drink taxes uses country-level sales and trade data. That is ecological, and appropriate: the tax is a country-level exposure and sales are a country-level outcome. The risk is in the next step. Claiming that the tax reduced individual diabetes risk needs individual data, or an explicit model linking the change in intake to disease, of the kind you will build in Project 1.

5. The ecological fallacy

A relationship between group averages need not hold for individuals within the groups; it can even have the opposite sign.

What it is
The ecological fallacy is assuming that a group-level association describes individuals. Its mirror, the atomistic fallacy, is assuming an individual-level association explains differences between groups.
Why it matters
The between-group slope mixes the individual effect with everything else that differs between groups. In 1950 Robinson showed that US states with more immigrants had higher literacy, while immigrants themselves were less often literate than native-born residents: immigrants had settled in states with good schools.
How to read it
Try the widget. The within-group slope is the effect for individuals; the ecological slope uses only the group means; the pooled slope ignores groups. Make them disagree in sign.
Groups vs individuals

Five groups of 40 people. Set the slope for individuals within each group and, separately, how group averages line up. The dashed line through the five group means is what an ecological study sees.

Trap

Adjusting an ecological analysis for group-level confounders and assuming that fixes it. Ecological bias can arise even without confounding, whenever the effect of exposure differs between groups or depends on other factors within groups. Only individual-level data inside groups can rule it out.

From your ML background

This is Simpson’s paradox in regression form. A model trained on country-level aggregates and then used to score individuals has learned the between-country slope, not the within-person one. Mixed models with group means as separate predictors (“within-between” models) estimate both slopes at once.

6. Case-crossover designs

For a short-lived exposure and a sudden outcome, compare each case’s exposure just before the event with their own exposure at other times.

What it is
Only cases are studied. For each case, the hazard window is the short period just before the event, when a trigger would act. One or more control windows of the same length, at other times, show how often that person is usually exposed. Analysis is matched (conditional logistic regression), each person their own stratum.
Why it matters
Each person is their own control, so everything fixed about them (genes, sex, chronic disease, income) cancels out without being measured. It is the design for triggers: heavy exertion and heart attack, a hot day and kidney stones, air pollution and asthma attacks.
When it works
The exposure must vary within a person over time and act quickly; the outcome must be abrupt. It fails for chronic exposures (diet, smoking) and slow diseases.
Example

Maclure introduced the design in 1991 to study triggers of heart attack. Mittleman and colleagues used it in 1993: they asked people who had had a heart attack about heavy physical exertion in the hour before symptoms and compared that with their usual frequency of heavy exertion. The risk was clearly raised in the hour after heavy exertion, and much more so in people who rarely exercised.

Project 6 · heat and outdoor workers

A case-crossover design fits heat and acute outcomes in the Gulf: hospital admissions for heat illness, kidney injury or injury at work among outdoor workers. Each admission is a case day; control days are the same weekday in the same month; exposure is daily heat stress from ERA5 at the worker’s location. Every fixed worker trait cancels, including nationality, job and baseline health, which are hard to measure well in migrant workforces.

Trap

Choosing control windows only before the event when the exposure trends over time. In a warming spell, or across the summer, earlier days are cooler than later ones, so “before” controls make heat look harmful even if it isn’t. Time-stratified control days on both sides of the event, within the same month, avoid this.

From your ML background

This is a person fixed-effects model. Conditional logistic regression with one stratum per person is like demeaning features within each user before fitting: only within-person variation is used, so anything constant per person can’t confound. The price is the same as in ML: features that never vary within a person get no coefficient. Lecture 5.5 extends this to the self-controlled case series.

Check yourself

  1. A national cross-sectional survey finds that adults with diagnosed diabetes drink fewer sugary drinks than adults without diabetes. A newspaper concludes that sugary drinks don’t cause diabetes. What is wrong?

    Show answer

    Reverse causation. Once diagnosed, people are told to cut sugary drinks, so current intake reflects the diagnosis. A snapshot can’t show what they drank before diabetes began. A cohort measuring intake before diagnosis, or restricting to undiagnosed people, would address it.

  2. A study of a fatal cancer recruits cases from people currently receiving treatment. An exposure looks protective. Name the bias and the mechanism.

    Show answer

    Prevalence-incidence (Neyman) bias. If the exposure makes the cancer kill faster, exposed cases die before they can be recruited, so they are under-represented among current patients. The fix is incident cases, recruited at diagnosis.

  3. Across 20 countries, higher average income goes with higher heart disease mortality. Within each country, richer people have lower heart disease mortality. Is one of these wrong?

    Show answer

    No. They answer different questions. Between countries, income goes with diet, ageing populations and other national factors. Within countries, income goes with better access to care and healthier behaviour. Using the country pattern to predict an individual commits the ecological fallacy.

  4. Would a case-crossover design suit the effect of long-term salt intake on stroke? Of a heatwave on emergency kidney admissions?

    Show answer

    Not for salt: intake is fairly stable within a person and works over years, so hazard and control windows would look alike. Yes for heat: daily heat varies within a person, acts within days, and admissions are abrupt.

  5. A case-crossover study of air pollution and asthma uses as controls the same day one, two and three weeks before each attack. Pollution in the region fell steadily over the study years. In which direction is the estimate biased?

    Show answer

    Towards protection. With pollution falling, the earlier control days tend to be more polluted than the case days, so case days look relatively clean and pollution looks less harmful than it is. (If pollution were rising, the bias would run the other way.) Time-stratified control days on both sides of the event, within the same month, remove most of this.
