Lecture 2.11

Screening and diagnostic test evaluation

Block 2 · Epidemiology
The point

A test’s sensitivity and specificity describe the test; its predictive values describe what a result means for the person in front of you, and they depend on how common the disease is. Screening adds a second problem: finding disease earlier can look like success even when nobody lives longer. This lecture gives you the numbers to read any test, and the biases to check before believing any screening claim. Lecture 2.15 takes diagnostic accuracy studies further.

1. Sensitivity and specificity

Sensitivity is how many of the sick a test catches; specificity is how many of the healthy it clears.

What it is
Compare the test with a reference standard (the best available way of knowing who truly has the disease). Sensitivity = true positives ÷ everyone with the disease. Specificity = true negatives ÷ everyone without it.
Why it matters
They are the closest thing a test has to fixed properties, so they are what guidelines, regulators and reviews report. They tell you what a test does to a group whose disease status you already know.
How to read it
A highly sensitive test, when negative, rules disease out (few sick people test negative). A highly specific test, when positive, rules disease in (few healthy people test positive). Screening tests usually favour sensitivity; confirmatory tests favour specificity.
What a change means
Specificity moving from 95% to 99% sounds small. In 100,000 healthy people screened it cuts false positives from 5,000 to 1,000: four thousand fewer people sent for follow-up tests.

Trap

Treating sensitivity and specificity as universal constants. They shift with the spectrum of patients: a test validated on advanced cases versus healthy volunteers looks better than it will in a screening population full of early, subtle disease and look-alike conditions. Ask who was in the validation study.

Analogy

A smoke alarm. Sensitive: it goes off for every real fire. Specific: it stays quiet when you make toast. Make it more sensitive and it screams at toast; make it more specific and it may sleep through a smouldering fire.

2. Predictive values and prevalence

When a disease is rare, most positive results are false, even from a good test.

What it is
The positive predictive value (PPV) is the share of positive results that are true. The negative predictive value (NPV) is the share of negatives that are true. They answer the patient’s question: “I tested positive; do I have it?”
\[ \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens} \times p}{\text{sens} \times p + (1-\text{spec}) \times (1-p)} \]
Why it matters
The same test gives very different PPVs in a screening population and a specialist clinic, because the prevalence \(p\) (the share who have the disease) differs. Rare disease means the large healthy group, times a small false-positive rate, swamps the small sick group.
How to read it
Think in natural frequencies: per 1,000 people, how many are sick, how many of those test positive, and how many of the healthy also test positive? Then divide. It is much harder to get wrong than the formula.
What a change means
With 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity, PPV is about 15% at 1% prevalence and about 89% at 30% prevalence. Same test, same lab; different population.
1,000 people, one test

Each square is a person. Lower the prevalence and watch the false alarms (amber) outnumber the true positives (red). Then see how much specificity you need to rescue the PPV.

Trap

Quoting a PPV from a validation study in a different population. A study that enrolled half cases and half controls has 50% “prevalence” by design; its PPV says nothing about a population where 1 in 200 has the disease. Sensitivity and specificity travel (roughly); predictive values do not.

From your ML background

Sensitivity is recall; PPV is precision; prevalence is class balance. A classifier validated on a balanced dataset will have far lower precision when deployed where positives are rare. An AI-ECG model that flags low ejection fraction with excellent discrimination in a cardiology clinic will still produce mostly false alarms if run on everyone in primary care. This is why precision–recall curves, not just ROC curves, matter for rare outcomes.

3. Thresholds and the ROC curve

Most tests give a number, and the cut-off you choose trades sensitivity against specificity; the ROC curve shows every trade at once.

What it is
Sick and healthy people have overlapping distributions of the test value. Moving the threshold down catches more sick people (sensitivity up) and flags more healthy ones (specificity down). The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every threshold. The area under it (AUC) is the chance that a randomly chosen sick person scores higher than a randomly chosen healthy one.
Why it matters
AUC compares tests independent of threshold. But a programme runs at one threshold, chosen by weighing the harm of a missed case against the cost of a false alarm and the follow-up capacity.
How to read it
AUC 0.5 is a coin toss; 0.7 is modest; 0.8 is good; above 0.9 is excellent for most clinical uses. The curve bowing towards the top-left is what you want.
What a change means
An AUC rising from 0.75 to 0.80 is a real gain in ranking, but whether it changes decisions depends on the part of the curve near the threshold you will actually use.
Move the threshold, trace the ROC curve

People with a test value at or above the threshold test positive. Slide the threshold and watch the point move along the curve. Then increase the separation between groups and see the whole curve improve.

Trap

Choosing a model or test on AUC alone. AUC ignores prevalence and calibration (whether a predicted 20% risk really means 20 in 100). Two tests with the same AUC can perform very differently at the threshold you need, and a well-ranked but miscalibrated risk score will put the wrong people over a treatment threshold.

From your ML background

This is exactly classifier threshold tuning. The epidemiology twist is that the threshold is a value judgement: set it from the relative costs of false negatives and false positives (decision curve analysis formalises this), not by maximising Youden’s index or accuracy. Recalibrate to the target population before choosing it.

4. Lead time, length time, overdiagnosis

Screening always makes survival after diagnosis look better; only fewer deaths in the whole screened population shows it works.

Lead-time bias
Screening moves the date of diagnosis earlier. If death comes at the same age, survival “from diagnosis” grows by exactly the lead time, with no benefit at all.
Length-time bias
Slow-growing disease spends longer in the detectable-but-silent phase, so periodic screening finds more of it. Screen-detected cases are therefore biased towards milder disease and look better than symptom-detected cases, whatever screening does.
Overdiagnosis
The extreme of length time: detecting disease that would never have caused symptoms or death in the person’s lifetime. Each overdiagnosed person is counted as a “cure” while receiving treatment that can only harm them.
How to read it
Rising incidence with flat mortality after screening starts is the fingerprint of overdiagnosis. Better five-year survival among screen-detected cases proves nothing on its own.
Example

In South Korea, thyroid ultrasound became a common add-on to health check-ups. Ahn, Kim and Welch reported that by 2011 the rate of thyroid cancer diagnosis was about 15 times the 1993 rate, while thyroid cancer mortality stayed essentially flat. The extra diagnoses led to surgery for many people whose cancers would probably never have harmed them.

Trap

“Five-year survival for screen-detected cancer is 95% versus 60% for symptom-detected, so screening works.” All three biases inflate that comparison. The valid evidence is a randomised trial (or strong natural experiment) comparing disease-specific and all-cause mortality in everyone invited versus not invited.

5. Should we screen?

A screening programme is a whole pathway, from invitation to treatment, and it is worth running only if the pathway as a whole does more good than harm at reasonable cost.

What it is
Wilson and Jungner’s 1968 principles for WHO remain the starting point. The three that matter most: the condition is an important health problem with a detectable early stage; there is an acceptable, accurate test; and early treatment improves outcomes compared with treatment at symptoms.
Why it matters
Screening invites healthy people, so the ethical bar is higher than for treating the sick. Every programme harms some people: false positives, anxiety, overdiagnosis, complications of follow-up tests.
How to judge it
Look for randomised evidence of a mortality or morbidity benefit; numbers needed to screen and numbers overdiagnosed per death prevented; uptake, follow-up and quality assurance in the real programme; and cost-effectiveness (Lecture 1.10).
What a change means
Extending screening to a lower-risk group lowers prevalence, so PPV falls, false positives per case found rise, and the balance of benefit and harm can flip even with the same test.
Example · Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s premarital screening programme for sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia became mandatory in 2004. Couples must be tested and at-risk couples are offered genetic counselling, but the marriage can go ahead whatever the result. So the programme can only work through couples’ choices, and its evaluation has to measure them. Memish and Saeedi reported that voluntary cancellation of planned marriages among at-risk couples rose from 9.2% in 2004 to 51.9% in 2009. Note the outcome: not test accuracy, but behaviour and, ultimately, affected births.

Trap

Evaluating a programme by its test. A perfectly accurate test inside a programme with low uptake, slow follow-up or no effective treatment achieves nothing. Most real screening failures happen after the test.

Analogy

Airport security. The scanner’s accuracy matters, but so do queue length, what staff do with an alarm, and how many innocent travellers are searched for each real threat found.

Check yourself

  1. A new blood test for pancreatic cancer has 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity. A company proposes offering it to all adults over 50. What is the main problem, without doing exact arithmetic?

    Show answer

    Pancreatic cancer is rare in the general population, so 5% false positives among the huge healthy majority will vastly outnumber true positives. PPV will be very low, and each false positive leads to scans, biopsies and anxiety. The test might be useful in a high-risk group where prevalence is higher.

  2. Five-year survival after breast cancer diagnosis rose sharply after a screening programme started. Name two reasons this could happen with no lives saved.

    Show answer

    Lead time: diagnosis moves earlier, so survival from diagnosis grows even if death comes at the same age. Length time and overdiagnosis: screening preferentially finds slow-growing or never-harmful cancers, which survive well regardless. Only mortality in the whole invited population can show benefit.

  3. Two AI-ECG models have the same AUC. Why might one be far better for a screening programme?

    Show answer

    AUC summarises ranking across all thresholds. The programme runs at one threshold, usually in the high-specificity region because prevalence is low. The ROC curves can cross, so one model can be better exactly where you operate. Calibration in the target population also matters, and AUC ignores it.

  4. A test is validated in a study of 200 confirmed cases and 200 healthy volunteers. Which of its reported numbers would you trust least for primary care, and why?

    Show answer

    The predictive values, because the study fixed prevalence at 50% by design. Sensitivity and specificity are also probably optimistic: confirmed cases tend to be clear-cut and healthy volunteers have none of the look-alike conditions seen in practice (spectrum effect).

  5. Saudi premarital screening is very accurate for carrier status. Why is that not enough to call the programme effective?

    Show answer

    The programme’s goal is fewer affected births, and results don’t bar marriage. Effectiveness depends on counselling, couples’ decisions and access to other options after the test. You judge it by downstream outcomes, such as at-risk marriages and affected births, not by test accuracy.
