Lecture 2.10

Causation: Bradford Hill and modern causal thinking

Block 2 · Epidemiology
The point

“X causes Y” means: if X had been different, Y would have been different. Every causal tool in epidemiology is a way of approximating that unobservable comparison. This lecture gives you the definition (potential outcomes), a model of how causes combine (causal pies), a checklist for judging a body of evidence (Bradford Hill), and a drawing tool for assumptions (DAGs, taken further in Block 4). Projects 1 and 8 rest on a causal chain from salt to blood pressure to cardiovascular events, so you will need to defend it.

1. What “cause” means

An exposure causes an outcome in a person if their outcome would have been different had their exposure been different, everything else held the same.

What it is
Each person has two potential outcomes: \(Y(1)\), what would happen if exposed, and \(Y(0)\), what would happen if not. The individual causal effect is the difference. We only ever see one of the two: the one matching what actually happened. The other is the counterfactual.
Why it matters
It turns “causation” from a philosophical word into a quantity with a definition. It also tells you what any study is trying to estimate, and so what could go wrong.
How to read it
Because individual effects are unobservable, epidemiology targets the average causal effect: the risk if everyone were exposed minus the risk if no one were.
\[ \text{ACE} = \Pr[Y(1)=1] - \Pr[Y(0)=1] \]
What a change means
An ACE of −5 percentage points means that treating everyone would leave 5 fewer people per 100 with the outcome than treating no one. It does not tell you which 5, or whether some people were harmed and more were helped.
Trap

Talking about the effect of something you cannot imagine intervening on. “The effect of obesity” is vague: obesity from diet, from exercise, from surgery, or from genes? Each has a different counterfactual and possibly a different effect. A well-defined causal question names the intervention, even if only hypothetically.

Analogy

The road not taken. You took the motorway and arrived late. Would the back roads have been faster? You can never drive both on the same morning. Causal inference is the science of estimating the other road from other drivers’ mornings.

2. From people to populations

Comparing exposed with unexposed people estimates the causal effect only if the two groups are exchangeable: had they swapped exposures, they would have had each other’s outcomes.

What it is
The unexposed group stands in for the missing counterfactual of the exposed group. That works when the groups are alike in everything that affects the outcome. Randomisation makes this true on average. In observational data you hope adjustment makes it true within strata (conditional exchangeability).
Why it matters
Confounding (Lecture 2.6) is simply a lack of exchangeability. Selection bias (Lecture 2.7) is a lack of exchangeability created by who got into the analysis.
How to read it
Ask: “if the treated had not been treated, would their risk equal the untreated group’s risk?” If doctors chose who to treat on prognosis, the answer is no.
What a change means
Moving from “doctors choose” to “coin flips choose” turns an association into a causal estimate, at the cost of chance error, which shrinks as the sample grows.
Potential outcomes for 16 patients

God’s view shows both outcomes for each patient. Switch to what the study sees, then change who gets treated. Watch the observed difference move while the true effect stays fixed.

Trap

Believing a large sample fixes confounding. It shrinks random error, not bias. If doctors treat the sickest, a million patients give a very precise wrong answer.

From your ML background

A predictive model fits \(\Pr[Y \mid X]\) and is rewarded for exploiting any association, confounded or not. In ICU data, “received a vasopressor” predicts death, because the sickest get vasopressors. A model can be excellent at prediction and useless as a guide to action. Causal questions need \(\Pr[Y(x)]\): what happens if we set X. Shifting the inputs by intervention is a distribution shift that a predictive model was never trained on.

3. Causal pies

Most diseases need several component causes acting together; any complete set is a sufficient cause, and blocking any one component prevents every case that set would cause.

What it is
Rothman’s sufficient-component cause model. Draw each sufficient cause as a pie. Its slices are component causes. Disease occurs when every slice of at least one pie is present. A component found in every pie is a necessary cause (the tubercle bacillus for tuberculosis).
Why it matters
It explains three things that puzzle newcomers. Why most smokers never get lung cancer: the other slices are missing. Why the “strength” of a cause varies between populations: it depends on how common its partner components are. And why attributable fractions add up to more than 100%.
How to read it
A component in pies that cause many cases is a high-leverage target. Each case can be prevented by removing any one slice of its pie, so the same case is “attributable” to several causes at once.
What a change means
If a partner component becomes more common (say, a new co-exposure spreads), the measured RR for a cause rises even though its biology hasn’t changed.
Remove a component, prevent a pie

One hundred cases arise from three sufficient causes. Remove components and see which pies can no longer complete. Then compare the fractions preventable by each component alone with their sum.

Example

Phenylketonuria (PKU) needs two components: an inherited enzyme deficiency and dietary phenylalanine. It is often called “genetic”, yet it is fully preventable by diet, which is why newborns are screened. Both components are 100% attributable: remove either and no case occurs. Asking how much is “due to genes” and how much “due to environment” has no answer.

Trap

Expecting causes to share out the burden. Attributable fractions overlap because one case can need several components; summing them to “100% explained” is a category error (Lecture 1.7 made the same point for GBD risk factors). The pies also connect to Lecture 2.9: two components in the same pie produce additive interaction. A RERI above 1 (or above 0, if neither exposure ever prevents disease) shows that some cases need both exposures together.

4. Bradford Hill’s viewpoints

Hill’s nine viewpoints are prompts for judging whether an association is causal, not a scoring system: only temporality is required.

What it is
In 1965 Austin Bradford Hill listed nine aspects of an association to consider before calling it causal. He wrote that none of them “can bring indisputable evidence for or against the cause-and-effect hypothesis and none can be required as a sine qua non”.
Why it matters
Guideline panels, GBD’s risk-outcome selection and expert reviews still structure causal judgements around them. Used well, they make you look for evidence beyond one study. Used badly, they become a tick-box.
How to read it
Today, read each viewpoint as a question about bias: could confounding, selection or measurement error explain this feature of the evidence?
ViewpointAskModern caveat
TemporalityDoes the cause come before the effect?The only necessary one. Watch reverse causation: illness lowers salt intake (Lecture 1.9)
ExperimentDoes removing the exposure reduce disease?The strongest when available: trials, natural experiments
StrengthIs the association large?Large effects are harder to explain by confounding, but weak effects can be causal (second-hand smoke)
ConsistencySeen across populations and designs?A shared bias replicates too; inconsistency may be real effect modification
Biological gradientMore exposure, more disease?Confounders can have gradients; true curves can be J-shaped or have thresholds
Plausibility, coherenceIs there a mechanism; does it fit what else we know?Limited by current biology
Specificity, analogyOne cause, one disease? Similar causes known?The weakest: smoking causes many diseases; analogies can be found for anything
Trap

Counting criteria met (“seven of nine, so causal”). The viewpoints are not independent and not equally weighted. A strong, consistent, dose-responsive association can still be confounded, as the observational studies of hormone replacement therapy and heart disease were before trials reversed them.

Example · Projects 1 and 8

The salt chain passes Hill’s most demanding tests. Experiment: trials show salt reduction lowers blood pressure, and trials of blood pressure lowering prevent strokes and heart attacks. Gradient: cohorts with repeated 24-hour urines show a steady sodium–risk gradient. Temporality is where spot-urine cohorts fail, because illness lowers intake before death. Write your Project 1 justification in this structure.

5. DAGs: drawing your assumptions

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a diagram of what causes what; it tells you which variables to adjust for and, just as important, which not to.

What it is
Nodes are variables; arrows are direct causal effects; no loops. An association between exposure and outcome can flow along any path between them. A causal path follows arrows forward. A backdoor path starts with an arrow into the exposure and carries confounding.
Why it matters
It replaces “adjust for everything associated with both” (which can introduce bias) with a rule: block every backdoor path, and don’t adjust for mediators or colliders. You met colliders in Lecture 2.7.
How to read it
Missing arrows are the strong claims. Every arrow you leave out asserts that one variable has no direct effect on another. Reviewers should argue with those.
Trap

Drawing the DAG after the analysis to justify the adjustment set you already used. Draw it first, from subject knowledge, and let it choose the covariates. Block 4 covers d-separation and the backdoor criterion properly.

6. Triangulation

The most convincing causal evidence comes from several approaches with different, unrelated biases that point the same way.

What it is
Lawlor, Tilling and Davey Smith’s idea: deliberately combine designs whose main sources of bias differ (cohorts, trials, natural experiments, Mendelian randomisation, negative controls), and state for each the direction its bias would push.
Why it matters
Ten cohorts that share the same confounding agree for the wrong reason. Two designs with opposite biases that agree are much harder to explain away. It is Hill’s “consistency” made rigorous.
How to read it
Agreement across designs with different biases: stronger case. Disagreement: find the design whose bias best explains the split.
What a change means
When a new design with a different bias profile disagrees with established cohorts, the question is not “which is right on average” but “which bias is more plausible here”.
Example

Alcohol and heart disease. Many cohorts found lower risk in moderate drinkers than in abstainers, a J-curve. Mendelian randomisation, using gene variants that make drinking unpleasant (in a European cohort meta-analysis, Holmes and colleagues, 2014; and in the China Kadoorie Biobank, Millwood and colleagues, 2019), found no protection from moderate drinking. The cohorts’ likely bias (former drinkers who quit through illness counted as abstainers, healthier lifestyles among moderate drinkers) does not affect MR. Lecture 2.19 covers MR.

Trap

Calling more of the same design “triangulation”. Five more case-control studies with the same recall bias add precision to a biased estimate, not new evidence.

Check yourself

  1. An observational study finds patients given a new heart-failure drug die more often than those not given it. Before concluding the drug is harmful, what is the first question to ask, in potential-outcomes language?

    Show answer

    Are the treated and untreated exchangeable? Would the treated patients, had they not received the drug, have had the same death risk as the untreated? If doctors gave it to sicker patients, the untreated group is a poor stand-in for the treated group’s counterfactual, and the comparison mixes drug effect with prognosis (confounding by indication).

  2. A colleague says “70% of lung cancer is due to smoking, so at most 30% can be due to radon.” What’s wrong?

    Show answer

    Causes are not shares of a fixed pie. Smoking and radon can be components of the same sufficient cause, so a case can be prevented by removing either. Attributable fractions for different causes overlap and can add to more than 100%.

  3. A cohort shows a strong, consistent, dose-dependent link between a vitamin level and lower mortality, with a plausible mechanism. A large trial of the supplement shows no effect. How do you reconcile them using Hill’s viewpoints?

    Show answer

    Strength, consistency, gradient and plausibility can all be produced by confounding (healthier people have higher levels) or reverse causation (illness lowers levels). “Experiment” is the viewpoint least vulnerable to those biases, so the trial carries more weight, unless it tested the wrong dose, population or duration. Counting viewpoints met would have misled you.

  4. Your Project 1 model adjusts the salt–stroke effect for blood pressure “to control for it”. Why is that a mistake for a policy model?

    Show answer

    Blood pressure is a mediator: salt raises blood pressure, which causes stroke. Adjusting for it removes the main pathway and estimates only the effect not through blood pressure, roughly the opposite of what the policy question needs. For the total effect of a salt policy, don’t condition on the mediator.

  5. Why does a randomised trial with 40 people sometimes show the wrong sign, while a confounded study with 400,000 people reliably shows the wrong sign?

    Show answer

    The small trial has exchangeability on average but large chance imbalances: its error is random and shrinks with size. The big study lacks exchangeability: its error is systematic and doesn’t shrink with size. Bigger samples cure noise, not bias.
