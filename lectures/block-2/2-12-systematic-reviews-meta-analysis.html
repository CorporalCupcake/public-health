Lecture 2.12

Systematic reviews and meta-analysis: PICO, search, screening, risk of bias, pooling, PRISMA

Block 2 · Epidemiology
The point

A systematic review answers a focused question using all the relevant studies, found and judged by methods fixed in advance. A meta-analysis is the optional statistical step that pools their results. This lecture walks the whole pipeline using Project 2 as the running example: a systematic review and meta-analysis of salt intake measured by 24-hour urine in adults in the GCC, pooled by country and sex. Its pooled estimate later replaces the intake inputs in Project 1.

1. The question and the protocol

A review is only as good as its question, and the question must be fixed, with its methods, before you look at the studies.

What it is
For effects, frame the question as PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome) or PECO (E for exposure). For prevalence or average levels, JBI’s CoCoPop fits better: Condition, Context, Population. The protocol states the question, eligibility criteria, search, risk-of-bias tool and analysis plan. Register it on PROSPERO before screening starts.
Why it matters
Every later decision (which study counts, which estimate to extract, which subgroup to report) can be bent, consciously or not, once you have seen the results. A dated, public protocol is your defence, and reviewers and journals check it.
How to read it
A good question is narrow enough to answer and broad enough to matter. Too narrow, and you find two studies; too broad, and you pool things that should never be averaged.
ElementProject 2
ConditionSalt (sodium) intake measured by 24-hour urine collection. Spot-urine estimates excluded (Lecture 1.9)
ContextThe six GCC countries; community or population-based samples
PopulationAdults (18 and over), any nationality resident in the GCC
ResultsMean intake in g/day with its SD and sample size, by country and sex
Trap

Quietly changing eligibility after seeing the studies (“let’s also include spot-urine studies, there are more of them”). Changes are sometimes needed; make them as dated, justified protocol amendments, and report them. Undisclosed changes are how reviews end up confirming what their authors hoped.

2. Searching and screening

Find every eligible study, not just the easy ones, and have two people independently decide which ones count.

What it is
Build the search from concept blocks: synonyms joined with OR within a block, blocks joined with AND (salt terms AND urine terms AND GCC country terms). Run it in several databases, then add citation searching and grey literature. Two reviewers screen titles and abstracts, then full texts, independently, and resolve disagreements by discussion or a third person.
Why it matters
Studies that are hard to find differ from easy ones: smaller, non-English, unpublished, or with less exciting results. Single screening misses eligible studies; dual screening is the PRISMA and Cochrane expectation, which is why Project 2 needs a second screener.
How to read it
Report the agreement between screeners (kappa, Lecture 2.14) and the full PRISMA flow. A flow diagram with suspiciously round numbers or no reasons for exclusion is a warning sign.
Example · Project 2

For GCC salt data, the big databases are not enough. Add Global Index Medicus (which covers the WHO Eastern Mediterranean regional index), Arabic-language reports, ministry of health and national survey reports, and conference abstracts. Search each country by name and by older or alternative names. Contact authors of studies that measured 24-hour urine but did not report the mean by sex.

Trap

Counting reports as studies. One national survey can produce four papers. Pooling all four counts the same people four times and inflates precision. Link reports to studies before extraction; PRISMA 2020 asks for both counts.

3. Extraction and risk of bias

Extract the same numbers from every study using a piloted form, and judge each study’s risk of bias with a tool built for its design.

What it is
Data extraction: two people independently record design, setting, sample, measurement method and results on a form tested on a few studies first. Risk of bias: a structured judgement, domain by domain, of whether a study’s result is likely to be systematically wrong.
Why it matters
Pooling biased studies gives a precise, biased answer. Risk of bias judgements feed sensitivity analyses (does the pooled mean change if you drop high-risk studies?) and the GRADE certainty rating.
How to read it
Use domain-level judgements (low, some concerns, high), not a single quality score. Summary scores add up unrelated items and hide which bias matters.

Example · Project 2

Add salt-specific items to the JBI checklist and pre-specify them: was the 24-hour collection checked for completeness (by urine volume, creatinine or PABA)? Was the sample drawn from the general population or from a clinic or workplace? One collection or several per person? A single 24-hour urine gives an unbiased mean but an inflated SD, because day-to-day variation is added to between-person variation. That matters for Project 1, which needs the spread of usual intake, not just the average. For extraction, convert everything to salt in g/day: sodium in mmol divided by 17.1, or sodium in grams times 2.54.

Trap

Using a tool built for effects (such as the Newcastle–Ottawa scale or ROBINS-E) on prevalence studies. Its questions about comparability of exposed and unexposed groups don’t apply, and the questions that do matter (representative sampling, complete urine collections) are missing.

4. Pooling: fixed and random effects

A meta-analysis is a weighted average; the fixed-effect and random-effects models differ in what they assume is being averaged, and so in the weights.

What it is
Each study gets weight in proportion to its precision. The fixed-effect model assumes every study estimates the same true value, so only sampling error separates them. The random-effects model assumes each study has its own true value, drawn from a distribution with spread \(\tau\) (tau), and estimates the mean of that distribution.
\[ w_i = \frac{1}{SE_i^2 + \tau^2} \]
Why it matters
With \(\tau = 0\) this is the fixed-effect weight. As \(\tau\) grows, it dominates every study’s variance and the weights become more equal: small studies gain influence and the confidence interval widens. For salt intake across six countries, true means surely differ, so random effects is the honest default.
How to read it
Look at the forest plot before the diamond. If the studies’ intervals barely overlap, the pooled mean is an average of different things, and you should say so.
What a change means
If the random-effects mean moves well away from the fixed-effect mean, small studies disagree with large ones. Find out why before reporting either.
A forest plot of 24-hour urine salt intake

Box size is each study’s weight. Switch between fixed-effect and random-effects weights, drop studies, and turn up the true spread between studies. Watch I², τ and the prediction interval respond.

From your ML background

Random effects is a two-level hierarchical model: partial pooling, the same idea as empirical Bayes shrinkage or a mixed model. Fixed effect is complete pooling; analysing each study alone is no pooling. Inverse-variance weighting is also how you would combine independent, unbiased estimators in an ensemble, and like an ensemble it cannot cancel a bias that all members share.

Trap

Choosing the model by a heterogeneity test (“Q was not significant, so fixed effect”). The test has little power with few studies. Choose the model from what you believe about the studies, in the protocol. In practice, report random effects with a modern τ² estimator (REML) and a Hartung–Knapp interval when studies are few; the simple DerSimonian–Laird method in the widget understates uncertainty then.

5. Heterogeneity: I², τ and the prediction interval

I² tells you what share of the spread is real, τ tells you how big the real spread is, and the prediction interval tells you where a new setting’s value is likely to fall.

What it is
I²: the percentage of the variation between study results that is due to real differences rather than chance. τ: the standard deviation of true values across studies, in the outcome’s own units (g/day). Prediction interval: the range in which the true value for a new, similar study or country is expected to lie, about the mean ± 2τ when you have many studies.
Why it matters
The confidence interval for the mean shrinks as studies accumulate; the prediction interval does not, because real spread is not noise. For Project 1, “what is intake in Saudi Arabia?” is closer to a prediction question than to “what is the GCC average?”.
How to read it
I² depends on study size: big, precise studies push it towards 100% even when true differences are small. Reviews of means and prevalences routinely show I² above 90%. So read τ for size, and the prediction interval for meaning.
What a change means
A τ going from 0.5 to 2 g/day means countries’ true averages differ by a few grams rather than a fraction of one: big enough to make a single GCC figure misleading for policy.
Trap

Explaining heterogeneity with meta-regression on study-level averages (“studies with more women had lower intake, so women eat less salt”). That is an ecological inference (Lecture 2.4). Compare sex using within-study, sex-specific estimates, which is why Project 2 extracts them separately.

Analogy

Average house prices across a country. The confidence interval says how well you know the national average. The prediction interval says what a house in a city you haven’t surveyed might cost. Buyers need the second.

6. Small-study effects and publication bias

If small studies with disappointing results stay unpublished, the published ones overstate the effect, and a funnel plot can reveal the gap.

What it is
A funnel plot shows each study’s estimate against its precision. Without bias, small studies scatter widely and symmetrically around the pooled value; large ones cluster tightly. A missing corner (small studies with null or unfavourable results) suggests publication bias, though other causes of small-study effects exist.
Why it matters
The pooled estimate inherits whatever selection the publishing process applied. Lost null results inflate effects, sometimes enough to create one from nothing.
How to read it
Only with about 10 or more studies; fewer cannot show a shape. Asymmetry is a prompt to investigate, not proof: small studies can differ for real reasons (different populations, more intensive interventions) or have more bias.
What goes missing from a funnel plot

Simulated trials of an intervention on systolic blood pressure, all with the same true effect. Send most non-significant trials to the file drawer and watch the funnel lose its lower-right corner and the pooled effect grow.

Example · Project 2

Funnel plots were built for effects, where significance drives publication. A survey that measures mean salt intake has no “significant” result to chase, so selection works differently: surveys that went unpublished, or were reported only in government documents. For Project 2, your protection is the grey-literature search, not a funnel test.

Trap

Reporting “no publication bias (Egger p = 0.4)” for seven studies. The test has almost no power with few studies, and a non-significant result proves nothing. Cochrane advises against such tests with fewer than 10 studies.

7. Certainty and reporting: GRADE and PRISMA

GRADE rates how much to trust the pooled answer; PRISMA makes sure a reader can see exactly how you got it.

GRADE
Rates the certainty of a body of evidence as high, moderate, low or very low. For effects, randomised evidence starts high and observational evidence starts low. You then rate down for five problems: risk of bias, inconsistency (unexplained heterogeneity), indirectness (wrong population or measure), imprecision and publication bias. Observational evidence can be rated up for large effects or a dose–response.
PRISMA 2020
The reporting guideline for systematic reviews: a 27-item checklist plus the flow diagram. Follow it from the protocol stage, as the project rules require, not as a final formatting step. PRISMA-P covers the protocol itself.
How to read it
“Low-certainty evidence” is not “no effect”. It means the true value may be substantially different from the estimate.
Example · Project 2

For a pooled mean like Project 2’s, apply the same five domains, but there is less agreement about where certainty should start. State your choice and reasons in the protocol. Expect to rate down for inconsistency (large τ) and perhaps indirectness (studies from a few cities standing in for whole countries).

Trap

Treating a meta-analysis as more trustworthy than its studies. Pooling reduces random error, not bias. Ten biased studies make one precise biased answer, which is worse than one imprecise one because it looks certain.

Check yourself

  1. Your Project 2 meta-analysis gives a pooled mean of 10 g/day (95% CI 9.4 to 10.6) with I² = 96% and a prediction interval of 7 to 13 g/day. Which number should Project 1 use for Saudi Arabia, and why?

    Show answer

    Neither the pooled mean nor its CI on its own. Large I² and a wide prediction interval mean countries truly differ, so a GCC average may be well off for Saudi Arabia. Use Saudi-specific studies (a country subgroup) if there are enough, and carry the uncertainty, including the between-study spread, into Project 1’s probabilistic sensitivity analysis.

  2. A colleague proposes including spot-urine studies “because 24-hour urine studies are too few”. How do you respond?

    Show answer

    Spot-urine equations are systematically biased for population sodium in ways that vary by population (Lecture 1.9), so adding them would trade a little imprecision for bias of unknown size. If the protocol is changed, it must be a documented amendment. Better: keep 24-hour studies as the primary analysis and report spot-urine studies separately, if at all.

  3. Why does adding a huge, precise study often increase I² even when it agrees roughly with the others?

    Show answer

    I² compares real spread with sampling error. A very precise study has tiny sampling error, so any real difference from the others becomes large relative to chance. I² is a relative measure; τ, in g/day, tells you whether the spread is big enough to matter.

  4. Random-effects and fixed-effect pooled estimates for a set of trials differ markedly, with random effects showing the larger benefit. What might be going on?

    Show answer

    Random effects gives small studies more weight, so the gap means small studies show bigger effects than large ones: a small-study effect. Possible reasons are publication bias, more bias in small trials, or genuinely different interventions or populations. Look at the funnel plot and the risk of bias of the small studies before trusting the random-effects estimate.

  5. Your second screener disagrees with you on 15% of abstracts. Is the review in trouble?

    Show answer

    Not necessarily. Disagreement at title and abstract stage is expected, which is why dual screening exists. Resolve each disagreement by discussion or a third reviewer, report agreement (kappa), and if disagreements cluster on one criterion, clarify its wording in the protocol. Inclusive screening at this stage is fine because full-text review follows.
