Lecture 6.11

Emergency preparedness and the International Health Regulations

Block 6 · Health protection
The point

The International Health Regulations (IHR) are the legally binding rules that tell 196 countries what to detect, when to tell WHO, and what not to do (block travel and trade without evidence). Since September 2025 they include a new top alert level, the “pandemic emergency”. This lecture shows how a country decides whether to notify an event, how WHO escalates, how preparedness is measured, and why preparedness scores failed to predict COVID-19 outcomes. You need to recognise these terms, read them correctly in news and papers, and know where Saudi Arabia and the Gulf fit.

1. What the IHR require

The IHR trade an obligation to report for a promise of proportionate responses: countries tell WHO quickly about risky events, and other countries agree not to punish them with unjustified travel and trade bans.

What it is
A treaty-level instrument adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2005 and in force since June 2007, binding on 196 States Parties (all 194 WHO Member States plus two others). It covers all hazards (infectious, chemical, radiological, food safety), not a fixed disease list. Each country must build core capacities to detect, assess, report and respond, and run a National IHR Focal Point reachable by WHO at all times.
Why it matters
Outbreaks cross borders faster than governments share bad news. The IHR make sharing an obligation, and make WHO the hub that can verify unofficial reports with the country concerned.
How to read it
Three duties carry most of the weight: assess urgent events within 48 hours, notify WHO within 24 hours of that assessment when Annex 2 says so, and give WHO the public health reasons for any extra measures that significantly interfere with travel or trade.
What a change means
When a country imposes a travel ban that WHO advised against, it is not breaking a rule by acting, but it must justify the measure; the IHR rely on transparency and peer pressure, not penalties.
Trap

Thinking WHO can order countries to act. WHO issues recommendations; countries keep sovereignty over their own health measures. The IHR have no enforcement mechanism beyond reporting, review and reputation.

Analogy

A neighbourhood fire-alarm pact: everyone agrees to ring the bell early, and in return the neighbours agree not to board up the house of whoever rang it.

2. Deciding to notify: the Annex 2 instrument

A country notifies WHO when an event answers yes to any two of four questions, or at once for four named diseases; notification is a duty to share, not an accusation.

What it is
Annex 2 is a decision flowchart. Four diseases are always notifiable after a single case: smallpox, wild-type polio, human influenza caused by a new subtype, and SARS. A second list (cholera, pneumonic plague, yellow fever, viral haemorrhagic fevers, West Nile fever, and diseases of special regional concern such as meningococcal disease) must always be put through the four questions. Everything else, infectious or not, goes through them when it might be a public health emergency of international concern.
Why it matters
The instrument replaces a fixed disease list with judgement, so new threats (a novel coronavirus, a chemical spill) are covered from day one.
How to read it
The questions: is the impact serious; is the event unusual or unexpected; is there a significant risk of international spread; is there a significant risk of travel or trade restrictions? Two yes answers mean notify within 24 hours of the assessment.
What a change means
An event can move from “monitor” to “notify” as facts change: a local cluster becomes notifiable when a case boards a flight, which turns question 3 to yes.

Trap

Confusing notification with a declared emergency. Countries notify many events each year; almost none become a PHEIC. A notification starts a confidential conversation with WHO; only the Director-General can determine a PHEIC (section 3).

From your ML background

Annex 2 is a hand-built, high-sensitivity classifier with a 2-of-4 rule. It is designed to over-notify, because a missed emergency costs far more than a false alarm handled privately. Like any screening rule, its PPV depends on prevalence (2.11): most notified events turn out not to be emergencies, and that is by design.

3. PHEIC and the new pandemic emergency

A PHEIC is WHO’s highest routine alarm; since 19 September 2025 a higher tier, the pandemic emergency, exists for PHEICs that are spreading widely and overwhelming health systems.

What it is
A public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) is an extraordinary event that risks spreading internationally and may need a coordinated international response. The Director-General decides, advised by an Emergency Committee of experts, and issues temporary recommendations. The 2024 amendments, adopted on 1 June 2024 and in force from 19 September 2025, add the pandemic emergency: a PHEIC from a communicable disease with wide geographical spread, that exceeds or risks exceeding health system capacity, causes major social or economic disruption, and needs rapid, equitable, coordinated international action.
Why it matters
COVID-19 was declared a PHEIC on 30 January 2020, but many governments reacted only when WHO called it a pandemic in March. The new tier gives that word a legal meaning.
How to read it
A PHEIC says “act together now”; it does not mean the disease is everywhere or that travel should stop. Temporary recommendations usually advise against travel and trade bans.
What a change means
The amendments also require each country to name a National IHR Authority to coordinate implementation, create a coordinating financial mechanism, and set up a States Parties committee. Eleven of 196 States Parties, including the United States, Italy and Argentina, rejected the amendments; the earlier text still applies to them.

Trap

Reading the end of a PHEIC as the end of the disease. Ending the mpox PHEIC in 2025 meant the event no longer needed extraordinary international coordination; mpox still circulated, and Africa CDC kept its own continental emergency in place at the time.

4. The WHO Pandemic Agreement

The Pandemic Agreement, adopted in May 2025, covers prevention and fair sharing between emergencies; it cannot open for signature until its pathogen-sharing annex is agreed, and as of late 2026 it has not been.

What it is
A new treaty adopted by the World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025, after a committee vote of 124 in favour, none against and 11 abstentions. It covers prevention (including a One Health approach), research and manufacturing capacity, technology transfer and supply chains. Its core bargain sits in an annex on pathogen access and benefit sharing (PABS): countries share samples and genetic sequences quickly, and in return get a share of the vaccines, tests and medicines developed from them.
Why it matters
During COVID-19, countries that shared data early often got vaccines last. Without a fair exchange, the incentive is to delay sharing, which slows the detection the IHR depend on.
How to read it
The IHR govern what happens during an event; the Agreement governs readiness and equity before and between events. The Agreement opens for signature only after the PABS annex is adopted, and enters into force after 60 ratifications.
What a change means
In May 2026 the World Health Assembly could not agree the annex and extended negotiations, with the result due at the May 2027 Assembly or a special session. Until then, the treaty exists on paper but binds no one.
Trap

Writing that the Pandemic Agreement “is in force” or “gives WHO powers over lockdowns”. It is not yet open for signature, and its text states that it gives WHO no authority to direct national measures such as lockdowns, vaccine mandates or border closures.

5. Measuring preparedness: SPAR and JEE

Preparedness is measured by a mandatory annual self-assessment and a voluntary external review; both score capacities on paper, not performance in a crisis.

What it is
WHO’s IHR monitoring framework has four parts, below. Scores run from 1 (no capacity) to 5 (sustainable capacity) for each technical area.
Why it matters
Donors, governments and researchers use the scores to target investment and to compare countries. They are also the only global, repeated measure of IHR implementation.
How to read it
Treat a SPAR score as a country’s own claim and a JEE score as a peer-reviewed snapshot from one week. Compare areas within a country (laboratories strong, risk communication weak) rather than ranking countries by a single total.
What a change means
A jump in a SPAR score between years may reflect a new law or plan, a new person filling in the form, or a new edition of the tool (the second edition in 2021 had 15 capacities; a third was revised after the 2024 amendments). Check what changed before reading it as progress.

Trap

Treating a high capacity score as proof a country will respond well. Scores measure whether plans, laws, labs and staff exist. They do not measure whether leaders act on warnings or whether the public follows advice, which section 6 shows mattered more in COVID-19.

6. Why preparedness scores missed COVID-19

Countries ranked most prepared before COVID-19 did not clearly do better: the largest analysis found no link with infections or infection fatality, social trust predicted infection rates better than any index, and the main positive finding depends on how deaths are counted.

What it is
The 2019 Global Health Security (GHS) Index ranked the United States first (83.5 of 100) and the United Kingdom second (77.9). A 2022 Lancet analysis of 177 countries (the COVID-19 National Preparedness Collaborators, led by Bollyky) found that preparedness indices, including the GHS Index and JEE scores, were not meaningfully associated with standardised infection rates or infection-fatality ratios. Trust in government and, more strongly, trust between people were associated with lower infection rates. A 2023 BMJ Global Health study of 183 countries (Ledesma and colleagues) found the reverse for excess mortality standardised for under-reporting and age: higher GHS scores went with fewer excess deaths. That result did not hold with excess-death estimates that lean more on reported COVID-19 deaths (from WHO and The Economist), so the evidence is mixed and turns on the outcome chosen and how it is adjusted.
Why it matters
Billions in preparedness funding are targeted with these scores. If they do not predict outcomes, they are measuring inputs that are necessary but not sufficient.
How to read it
Crude comparisons mislead: high-scoring countries are richer and older, and older populations die more from the same infection. Outcomes must be age-standardised (or use infection-fatality ratios and infection rates separately) before asking whether preparedness helped.
What a change means
A crude positive correlation (higher score, more deaths) can turn negative after age standardisation. The direction of a crude association tells you little here.

A country-level rate model (3.3): deaths with a population offset, the index scaled per 10 points, and age and trust as covariates. quasipoisson allows for overdispersion. This is still an ecological analysis (2.4): it says how countries differ, not what preparedness does for a person, and trust may itself be shaped by the same institutions the index scores.

From your ML background

The GHS Index is a hand-weighted feature score never trained on outcomes, so poor predictive validity is not surprising. It is also exposed to Goodhart’s law: once a score drives funding, countries optimise the score (write the plan) rather than the target (respond well). Validate any index against held-out outcomes, as you would a model.

Trap

Concluding that preparedness does not matter. The finding is that these indices did not measure what mattered, and that the outcomes depended on decisions and behaviour as well as capacity. Laboratories and surveillance were still needed to detect the virus at all.

7. Saudi Arabia and the Gulf

Saudi Arabia runs one of the world’s largest recurring mass-gathering operations and has notified MERS cases to WHO for more than a decade, which has built practical IHR experience.

What it is
Saudi Arabia’s Public Health Authority (Weqaya) began as the National Center for Disease Prevention and Control, created in 2013, and became a public authority in 2021. At Gulf level, the Gulf Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (Gulf CDC), under the Gulf Health Council and based in Riyadh, was launched in 2021 and formally inaugurated in 2022. Saudi Arabia completed a Joint External Evaluation in March 2017.
Why it matters
The Hajj (6.10) is a yearly, full-scale test of surveillance, vaccination requirements, heat response and crowd control, the capacities that the IHR ask for.
How to read it
WHO’s Disease Outbreak News on MERS usually begins with “the IHR National Focal Point of Saudi Arabia notified WHO of …”: that is the IHR working as designed.
What a change means
A regional CDC lets Gulf countries pool laboratory capacity and data, which matters for threats like MERS and AMR that cross Gulf borders easily (6.6).
Gulf example · Reading Saudi Arabia’s JEE

The 2017 JEE report for Saudi Arabia (WHO/WHE/CPI/2017.25) scores 19 technical areas and lists priority actions for each. For a PhD proposal on Gulf preparedness, use it as a baseline, then compare with the latest SPAR submissions and with after-action reviews of COVID-19 and the Hajj. Agreement between paper scores and real performance is the research question, not an assumption.

Trap

Assuming that experience with one hazard transfers to all. Strength in mass-gathering medicine and MERS does not guarantee strength in, say, chemical events or risk communication; the JEE scores each area separately for that reason.

Check yourself

  1. A single confirmed human case of a new influenza subtype is found in a remote village with no travel links. A health official argues it need not be notified because there is no risk of international spread. Is that right?

    Show answer

    No. Human influenza caused by a new subtype is one of four always-notifiable diseases: one case must be notified, whatever the four questions say. Its pandemic potential is exactly why the IHR remove the judgement call.

  2. A news article says “WHO declared a PHEIC, so countries must close their borders.” Correct it.

    Show answer

    A PHEIC triggers temporary recommendations, which usually advise against travel and trade restrictions. Countries can still impose extra measures, but must give WHO the public health rationale. The IHR aim for proportionate responses, and a PHEIC asks for coordinated action, not closure.

  3. Country A scored 80 on a preparedness index and had more COVID-19 deaths per person than Country B, which scored 40. Does this show the index is useless?

    Show answer

    Not on its own. Two countries prove nothing, and a crude comparison is confounded by age (high scorers are usually older), testing and death certification, and timing of waves. The stronger evidence is the 177-country analysis that used standardised infection rates and infection-fatality ratios and still found no meaningful association; a later 183-country study found a protective link only for excess mortality adjusted for under-reporting and age. The lesson is that the index measured capacity inputs, not response, and any verdict depends on the outcome and the adjustment.

  4. Why would WHO prefer that countries notify many events that turn out to be minor, rather than only notifying sure emergencies?

    Show answer

    Early detection is worth more than a clean record. A missed emergency can spread internationally for weeks; a false alarm is handled in a confidential exchange with WHO. The 2-of-4 rule is tuned for sensitivity, accepting a low PPV.

  5. A colleague writes that the 2024 IHR amendments apply to every country from September 2025. What two corrections would you make?

    Show answer

    Eleven States Parties, including the United States, rejected them, so the earlier text still applies to those countries; a few others entered reservations. And for the four states that had rejected the 2022 amendments, the 2024 amendments take effect a year later, in September 2026.
