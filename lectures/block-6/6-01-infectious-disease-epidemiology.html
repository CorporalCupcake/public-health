Lecture 6.1

Infectious disease epidemiology: transmission, R0, incubation, herd immunity

Block 6 · Health protection
The point

Infectious disease breaks the rule the rest of epidemiology relies on: one person’s outcome depends on everyone else’s. My infection is your exposure. That makes cases grow, shrink or fizzle out as chains, and five numbers describe the chain: how it spreads, how fast, how many each case infects, how unevenly, and how many die. This lecture gives you those numbers and how to read them. The rest of Block 6 (models, surveillance, outbreaks, vaccines, the Hajj) is built on them.

1. How infections travel

The route of transmission decides who is exposed, which contacts count, and which control works; get the route wrong and the right effort goes to the wrong place.

What it is
The path a pathogen takes from one host to the next. The main routes are respiratory (droplets and fine airborne particles), direct or indirect contact (hands, surfaces), faecal–oral (food and water), vector-borne (mosquitoes, ticks), blood and sexual, and from mother to child. A zoonosis is an infection that crosses from animals to people; the first human case is a spillover.
Why it matters
Each route has its own contact network and its own lever. Water-borne cholera is stopped by clean water, not masks. Dengue is stopped by controlling Aedes mosquitoes, not by isolating patients. An airborne virus needs ventilation as well as hand hygiene.
How to read it
Ask three questions of any new outbreak: what is the source (an animal, a meal, a person)? Is there person-to-person spread, and how much? Where does it concentrate (households, hospitals, schools, workplaces)?
What a change means
A shift from spillover-only cases to clusters of person-to-person cases is the key warning sign. It means the outbreak can now sustain itself without the animal source.
RouteExampleMain lever
RespiratoryMeasles, influenza, MERS in hospitalsVaccination, isolation, ventilation, infection control
Faecal–oralCholera, hepatitis A, food poisoningSafe water, sanitation, food hygiene
Vector-borneDengue, malariaVector control, bed nets, removing breeding sites
In the Gulf · MERS-CoV

MERS coronavirus shows two routes in one disease. Dromedary camels are the reservoir: people catch it from camels in occasional spillovers. Most reported cases, though, came from person-to-person spread inside hospitals, where coughing patients, aerosol-generating procedures and crowded emergency rooms amplify it. Spread in households and the community is limited. That split is why the control strategy is two-pronged: reduce camel exposure, and enforce infection control in health care.

Trap

Assuming the route from the symptoms. A respiratory illness can come from an animal or a contaminated ventilation system, and diarrhoea can spread person to person in a nursing home. Infer the route from the epidemiology (who got sick, when, where, and with what exposure), which is the subject of outbreak investigation in Lecture 6.4.

2. The clock of an infection

Infectiousness and symptoms run on two separate clocks; whenever people are infectious before they feel ill, isolating the sick cannot stop the epidemic on its own.

What it is
The latent period runs from infection to becoming infectious; the infectious period follows it. The incubation period runs from infection to symptoms. The generation interval is the time from one person’s infection to the infection of someone they infect; the serial interval is the time between their symptom onsets, which is what we can actually observe.
Why it matters
The generation interval sets the speed of the epidemic: the same number of secondary cases per case, delivered in half the time, doubles the growth rate. The gap between latent and incubation periods decides whether symptom-based control (isolation, contact tracing) can work. The incubation period sets quarantine lengths and tells you how far back to look for the source.
How to read it
If the latent period is shorter than the incubation period, there is presymptomatic transmission. Fraser and colleagues (2004) showed that the share of transmission before symptoms is what made SARS controllable by isolation and tracing (little presymptomatic spread) and pandemic influenza much harder (a lot).
What a change means
A serial interval of 5 days instead of 10 means each generation of cases arrives twice as fast, so you have half the time to trace contacts before they become infectious themselves.
Trap

Using “incubation period” to mean “time before you can spread it”. They are different clocks. For MERS the incubation period is typically about 5 days (range roughly 2 to 14), but that alone does not tell you when patients become infectious. Quarantine length comes from the incubation period; tracing windows come from when infectiousness starts.

Analogy

A smoke alarm with a delay. If the fire spreads to the next room before the alarm sounds, evacuating on the alarm is too late for that room. Presymptomatic transmission is a fire that outruns its alarm.

3. R0 and the effective reproduction number

R is the average number of people each case infects; above 1 the epidemic grows, below 1 it shrinks, and almost every control measure works by pushing R below 1.

What it is
The basic reproduction number \(R_0\) is the average number of secondary cases caused by one case in a fully susceptible population, with no control measures. The effective reproduction number \(R_t\) is the same thing at time \(t\), in the population as it is now: some people immune, some behaviour changed, some measures in place.
Why it matters
It is the single threshold that decides growth or decline, and it decomposes into levers. Roughly, \(R_0\) is contacts per day, times the chance each contact transmits, times days infectious. Distancing cuts contacts, masks and hygiene cut the chance per contact, and isolation cuts the days infectious.
How to read it
\(R_0\) is a property of a pathogen in a population, not of the pathogen alone: the same virus has a higher \(R_0\) in a crowded city than in a scattered village. \(R_t\) falls as people become immune: roughly \(R_0\) times the share still susceptible, times the effect of any measures.
What a change means
An \(R_t\) of 1.2 means cases grow by 20% each generation; 0.8 means they shrink by 20%. With a 5-day generation interval, 1.2 doubles cases in about 19 days and 0.8 halves them in about 16. The direction matters more than the decimal.
\[ R_0 \approx \text{contacts per day} \times \text{transmission chance per contact} \times \text{days infectious} \]
PathogenTypical R0 (commonly quoted)Read it as
Measles12–18Explosive; tiny immunity gaps cause outbreaks
Seasonal influenzaabout 1.3 (median of estimates)Modest R, but very fast generations
MERS-CoV, between peoplebelow 1Chains die out, but clusters can still be large
Trap

Comparing pathogens by R alone. Speed depends on R and the generation interval. Influenza, with an R near 1.3 but generations of about 3 days, can outrun a pathogen with R of 2 and 10-day generations. When an outbreak is growing, the doubling time is the more urgent number; R tells you how hard you must push to stop it.

From your ML background

R is the branching factor of a tree, or the gain of a feedback loop: below 1 the loop damps, above 1 it explodes. The growth rate is like compound interest, where R is the multiplier per period and the generation interval is the period length. Converting between R and growth rate needs the whole generation-interval distribution, not just its mean (Wallinga & Lipsitch 2007), which is why two studies with the same growth rate can report different R.

4. Superspreading and chance

The average hides the shape: when a few cases cause most transmission, most introductions die out by luck, and the rest explode.

What it is
The number of secondary cases per case varies. Epidemiologists model it as a negative binomial distribution with mean R and a dispersion parameter \(k\) (the same overdispersion as in Lecture 3.3). Small \(k\) means a long tail: most cases infect nobody, a few infect many. A superspreading event is one case, or one setting, causing far more infections than average.
Why it matters
Low \(k\) changes strategy. Many introductions fizzle, so early case counts are noisy and misleading. Control can be targeted: find and close the settings where superspreading happens (hospital wards, crowded indoor events), and trace backwards to the event that infected a case, not only forwards.
How to read it
\(k\) above about 5 is close to “everyone alike”. \(k\) below 1 is overdispersed. Lloyd-Smith and colleagues (2005) estimated \(k \approx 0.16\) for SARS, which implies the most infectious 20% of cases cause close to 90% of transmission.
What a change means
With R = 2, lowering \(k\) from 5 to 0.2 raises the chance a single introduction dies out from under a third to about four in five. Same R, very different early picture.
Twenty introductions of one case each

Each line is one outbreak started by one case. Keep R at 2 and slide k down: more outbreaks die out, but the survivors grow just as fast. Then shorten the generation interval and watch the doubling time fall with R unchanged.

In the Gulf · MERS clusters

Kucharski and Althaus (2015) fitted this model to MERS cluster data and estimated R at about 0.5 and, in their main dataset, \(k\) at about 0.26 (with wide uncertainty): most chains stop quickly, yet clusters of over 150 cases are not unexpected. That is what happened in South Korea in 2015, when one traveller returning from the Arabian Peninsula started a hospital outbreak of 186 cases. Only 22 of the 186 infected anyone, and 5 superspreading events caused 150 of the transmissions (Kim et al. 2017). The same pattern of hospital amplification drove the large Saudi hospital outbreaks.

Trap

Reading “R below 1” as “no risk of a large outbreak”. With strong overdispersion, a subcritical pathogen can still produce a big cluster in the wrong setting. Conversely, “the first few imported cases led nowhere” is weak evidence that R is low: with small \(k\), most introductions fail even when R is well above 1.

Analogy

Sparks from a campfire. Most land on sand and die. A few land on dry grass. The average spark is harmless; the forest fire comes from the tail.

5. Herd immunity

Once enough people are immune that each case infects fewer than one other on average, chains die out, and the unimmune are protected indirectly.

What it is
The herd-immunity threshold is the share of the population that must be immune for \(R_t\) to fall below 1 in a randomly mixing population. It comes straight from \(R_t \approx R_0 \times\) (share susceptible): set that to 1 and solve.
Why it matters
It sets vaccination coverage targets, and it explains why vaccines protect people who cannot be vaccinated (newborns, the immunocompromised). With an imperfect vaccine, the coverage needed is the threshold divided by vaccine effectiveness.
How to read it
\(R_0 = 2\) needs 50% immune; \(R_0 = 4\) needs 75%; measles, at 12–18, needs about 92–95%. If vaccine effectiveness is lower than the threshold, no coverage reaches it: the vaccine alone cannot interrupt transmission.
What a change means
Because the curve is steep at high \(R_0\), small coverage drops matter most for the most contagious diseases. Measles coverage falling from 95% to 90% can turn a protected population into an outbreak-prone one.
\[ \text{herd-immunity threshold} = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0} \]
National coverage vs local gaps

The teal line assumes coverage is spread evenly; the red line has the same national coverage but one community, 10% of people, sits below it. Raise the coverage gap and the share of contacts within that community: the coverage needed climbs past the textbook threshold, sometimes out of reach.

Trap

Treating the threshold as a finish line: “we are at 95%, we can stop pushing”. The formula assumes random mixing. Real populations cluster by school, neighbourhood, nationality and belief, so a national average can hide pockets well below the threshold where outbreaks still happen. The threshold is also a floor, not a target: each birth cohort arrives susceptible, so coverage must be maintained every year, and an epidemic that runs unchecked overshoots it, infecting far more than the threshold implies (Lecture 6.2).

Example

Measles outbreaks in high-income countries with national coverage above 90% are typically concentrated in close-knit communities with low uptake. The national figure looked safe; the local one was not. Coverage must be measured where people actually mix, which is why Lecture 6.5 and the small-area methods of Lecture 6.8 matter.

6. Severity: case fatality vs infection fatality

How deadly a disease looks depends on whom you counted as a case; the case fatality ratio of a newly found disease is usually wrong, and its errors run in both directions.

What it is
The case fatality ratio (CFR) is deaths divided by diagnosed cases. The infection fatality ratio (IFR) is deaths divided by all infections, including mild and silent ones never tested. The IFR needs serosurveys (blood tests for antibodies) or modelling to estimate the denominator.
Why it matters
CFR drives fear and headlines; IFR drives the expected death toll if the infection spreads widely. Planning for hospital beds, risk communication and the case for vaccination all depend on which one you use.
How to read it
Early CFR is biased upward when only the sickest are tested (ascertainment bias) and downward when recent cases have not yet had time to die (right censoring, the same problem as in survival analysis). Watch whether testing is broadening over time.
What a change means
A CFR falling from 60% to 30% over a year may mean better care, or simply that milder cases are now being found. Check testing policy before celebrating.
In the Gulf · How deadly is MERS?

WHO’s December 2025 global update counted 2,635 laboratory-confirmed MERS cases and 964 deaths since 2012, a CFR of about 37%; 2,224 of the cases were reported by Saudi Arabia. Two studies show why that is not the infection fatality. Cauchemez and colleagues (2014) found a CFR of 74% among primary cases picked up by routine surveillance, against 20% among secondary cases found by tracing contacts, and estimated that at least 62% of symptomatic cases had gone undetected. A nationwide Saudi serosurvey (Müller et al. 2015) found antibodies in 0.15% of over 10,000 adults, with prevalence 15 times higher in camel shepherds and 23 times higher in slaughterhouse workers: evidence of infections that were never diagnosed. MERS is severe, but the 37% describes the cases we find, not the infections that occur.

Trap

Dividing cumulative deaths by cumulative cases during a growing epidemic. Many recent cases have not reached their outcome, so the ratio is too low; meanwhile, testing only the hospitalised makes it too high. Use deaths among cases with known outcomes, adjust for the delay from onset to death, and state clearly whether you report CFR or IFR.

From your ML background

CFR from surveillance is a model trained on a biased sample: the label “case” is only assigned to people who were sick enough to be tested. It is the same selection problem as training a disease classifier on hospital patients and deploying it in the community (Lecture 2.7 on selection bias). The serosurvey is the representative test set.

Check yourself

  1. A new respiratory virus has R0 estimated at 2.5. A colleague says isolating everyone with symptoms will stop it. What do you need to know before agreeing?

    Show answer

    How much transmission happens before symptoms (and from people who never develop them). If the latent period is shorter than the incubation period, a large share of spread has already happened by the time anyone is isolated. Isolation must remove enough transmission to bring R below 1; with R0 = 2.5 it must prevent more than 60% of it, which is impossible if most spread is presymptomatic.

  2. Two countries each import ten cases of the same virus. In country A none leads to onward spread; in country B one sparks a large outbreak. Is the virus less transmissible in country A?

    Show answer

    Not necessarily. With strong overdispersion (small k), most introductions die out by chance even when R is above 1. Ten failed introductions are weak evidence about R. The difference may be luck, or the setting where the introduction landed (a hospital ward vs a home).

  3. A country reports 95% national measles vaccine coverage and yet has repeated outbreaks. Give two explanations consistent with the herd-immunity threshold being “met”.

    Show answer

    First, coverage is not even: pockets of low uptake, where people mostly mix with each other, can sit far below the threshold. Second, coverage is not immunity: vaccine failure, waning, missed second doses and older cohorts never vaccinated all leave more susceptibles than the coverage figure suggests. Imported cases then find those susceptibles.

  4. Early in an outbreak the reported CFR is 50%. Six months later, after testing is extended to contacts of cases, it is 20%. Has the disease become milder?

    Show answer

    Probably not. The denominator changed: testing contacts finds mild and asymptomatic infections that were missed when only severe patients were tested. This is exactly the MERS pattern of primary vs secondary cases. Compare like with like, for example CFR among hospitalised cases over time, before concluding anything about virulence.

  5. Two outbreaks have the same R of 1.5, but one doubles every 4 days and the other every 12. What differs, and which one leaves less time for contact tracing?

    Show answer

    The generation interval: the fast one passes infection on about three times as quickly. It leaves less time to trace because contacts become infectious sooner after exposure. Both need the same proportional cut in transmission to bring R below 1, but the fast one needs it much sooner.
