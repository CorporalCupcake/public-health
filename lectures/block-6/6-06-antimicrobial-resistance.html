Lecture 6.6

Antimicrobial resistance as a public health problem

Block 6 · Health protection
The point

Every antibiotic dose selects for the bacteria that survive it. One patient’s treatment slightly reduces how well antibiotics work for everyone, so resistance is a shared problem that individual prescribing decisions do not solve. Public health handles it the way it handles other population problems: measure the burden, run surveillance, set priorities, change behaviour (stewardship), and treat humans, animals and the environment as one system (One Health).

1. Selection pressure

Antibiotics do not create resistance; they kill the susceptible bacteria and leave the resistant ones to take over.

What it is
In any large bacterial population a few cells carry resistance, from random mutation or from genes picked up from other bacteria on plasmids (small circles of DNA that move between cells, even between species). An antibiotic removes their competitors. The more often a drug is used, the more often that advantage applies.
Why it matters
Selection happens wherever the drug goes: in the patient’s gut and skin flora, not just in the infection being treated, and in farm animals and sewage too. Resistance that emerges in harmless gut bacteria can later be passed to a pathogen.
How to read it
Resistance is reported as the percentage of tested isolates (bacteria grown from patient samples) that are resistant to a drug. Places and years with more antibiotic use generally show higher percentages, though many other factors also differ between them.
What a change means
A rising resistant percentage means the drug will fail more often as first-line treatment. Guidelines usually switch empirical treatment (given before lab results) once resistance passes a local threshold, which pushes use onto newer, broader drugs.
Example · Outpatient use and resistance across Europe

Goossens and colleagues compared outpatient antibiotic use in 26 European countries (1997–2002). Use ranged from 10.0 defined daily doses (DDD) per 1000 inhabitants per day in the Netherlands to 32.2 in France, and countries that used more had more resistance. It is an ecological comparison (Lecture 2.4), so countries differ in many other ways, but it fits the pattern seen within countries and over time.

Trap

Saying patients “become resistant”. People do not become resistant to antibiotics; bacteria do. The difference matters for messaging: a patient who stops a course early is not making their body resistant, and the main driver is how often antibiotics are used at all, including when they were never needed.

Analogy

Spraying a field with weedkiller every week does not teach the weeds anything. It clears away every weed the spray can kill, and the few hardy ones get the whole field.

2. Resistance as a shared resource

Antibiotic effectiveness behaves like a shared resource: each prescription benefits one patient now and costs everyone a little later.

What it is
A tragedy of the commons: a resource open to everyone gets overused because each user gains the full benefit of their use but bears only a small share of its cost. Resistant bacteria spread between people, farms and countries, so the cost of one prescriber’s choices lands mostly on others.
Why it matters
It explains why education alone rarely works and why policy uses rules (prescription-only sale, bans on growth promoters), targets and incentives. It also explains why new antibiotics are commercially unattractive: they are kept in reserve, so they sell little.
How to read it
Resistance rises when the advantage from antibiotic use outweighs the fitness cost of resistance (resistant bacteria often grow a little more slowly without the drug). Use and cost pull in opposite directions every year.
What a change means
Cutting use only reverses resistance if use falls below the level that balances the fitness cost. Costs are often small, and bacteria can evolve to lose them, so declines are usually slow (Andersson and Hughes 2010).

The model in the widget tracks the log-odds of resistance year by year:

\[ \text{logit}(p_{t+1}) = \text{logit}(p_t) + k\,u_t - c \]

Here \(p_t\) is the resistant share in year \(t\), \(u_t\) is antibiotic use, \(k\) is how strongly use favours resistant bacteria, and \(c\) is the fitness cost. Resistance rises when \(k\,u_t\) is bigger than \(c\) and falls when it is smaller.

Selection model: use, cost and stewardship

Start with the defaults: resistance climbs, then stewardship cuts use and resistance falls far more slowly than it rose. Then set use after stewardship to 20%, just above the dashed threshold line, and resistance keeps rising. Try compensatory evolution and co-selection to see why real declines are often small.

Example · Why resistance sticks

Andersson and Hughes reviewed the evidence on reversing resistance. Three things slow it: fitness costs are often small; bacteria acquire compensatory mutations that remove the cost without losing resistance; and resistance genes often sit on plasmids with genes for other drugs or metals, so using one drug keeps selecting for resistance to another (co-selection).

From your ML background

This is an adversarial setting with an adaptive opponent, like spam filtering: a strong filter used everywhere selects for spam that slips through, and the filter decays. In both cases the defence is a shared asset, and heavy use speeds its decay.

Analogy

A village pasture where every family may graze as many sheep as they like. Each extra sheep is pure gain for its owner, while the damage to the grass is shared by all. Antibiotic effectiveness is the grass.

3. How big is the burden?

AMR is linked to millions of deaths a year, but the headline number depends on whether you count deaths caused by resistance or deaths in which resistance was present.

EstimateAttributable to AMRAssociated with AMR
2019 (Murray et al. 2022)1.27 million deaths4.95 million deaths
2021 (GBD 2021 AMR Collaborators 2024)1.14 million deaths4.71 million deaths
Forecast, 2025–2050 combined (same 2024 paper)More than 39 million deaths169 million deaths
What it is
Attributable deaths are those that would not have happened if the resistant infection had been a susceptible one. Associated deaths are those in which a resistant infection was involved, whether or not it changed the outcome. Both come from the Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) project within the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework, covering 204 countries.
Why it matters
The attributable number is the one that compares with other causes and that interventions on resistance could prevent. The associated number includes deaths that would happen anyway from a severe infection, so it is bigger, but it is a sensible upper limit on what preventing the infections themselves could avert.
How to read it
The comparison is a counterfactual, like the population attributable fraction in Lecture 1.7: deaths with resistance minus the deaths expected if the same infections had been susceptible. It rests on modelled data in many countries with little laboratory testing, so the uncertainty intervals are wide (for 2019, 0.91–1.71 million attributable).
What a change means
The 2024 forecast expects attributable deaths to reach 1.91 million a year by 2050, mostly among older adults. Forecasts depend on assumptions about ageing, health care and new drugs, so read them as scenarios.
Trap

Reading the fall from 1.27 million (2019) to 1.14 million (2021) as progress. They come from different analyses with different data and methods, and 2021 was distorted by COVID-19. Compare years only within one consistent series from the same GBD round.

Analogy

Associated deaths are every crash in which a car had worn tyres. Attributable deaths are the crashes that new tyres would have prevented. Both are useful numbers, but they answer different questions.

4. Surveillance: GLASS and laboratory data

AMR surveillance is mostly built from routine laboratory results, which tell you about the patients who got a culture, not about everyone with an infection.

What it is
The WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) collects national data on resistance in priority bacteria from blood, urine, stool and genital samples, and on antimicrobial use. Countries build national systems from laboratory networks (Lecture 6.3 covers surveillance in general).
Why it matters
Surveillance sets empirical treatment guidelines, detects new resistance early, and measures whether stewardship works. WHO’s October 2025 GLASS report found that about 1 in 6 laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections in 2023 were resistant to antibiotics, rising to about 1 in 3 in the South-East Asia and Eastern Mediterranean regions (the GCC sits in the latter).
How to read it
Look at the denominator (number tested), which samples were included, and whether only the first isolate per patient was counted, so that one patient sampled ten times does not count ten times. The same report found resistance rising in over 40% of the bacteria–antibiotic combinations it monitored between 2018 and 2023.
What a change means
A jump in resistance can be real, or it can follow a change in who gets cultured. Coverage was patchy: 104 countries reported data for 2023, and almost half of countries did not report.
library(AMR); library(dplyr)

  summarise(tested = n_sir(CRO),                  # ceftriaxone results

The AMR package (Berends et al. 2022) knows bacterial names and antibiotic codes. Here it keeps the first isolate per patient, picks E. coli and Klebsiella, and works out the share resistant to ceftriaxone (a third-generation cephalosporin) by genus and year. The minimum = 10 returns NA when fewer than 10 isolates were tested, which stops a percentage built on three results from looking like a trend.

Trap

Treating hospital laboratory percentages as population prevalence. Clinicians culture mainly severe cases and cases that failed first treatment, both of which are more often resistant. Where cultures are rare and costly, the bias is larger, and resistance looks higher than in the community.

Analogy

Judging how reliable a car model is from the cars at the repair garage. The garage sees the failures, so its numbers are honest but not typical.

5. Priorities: the pathogen list, AWaRe and global targets

WHO steers effort with three tools: which bacteria matter most, which antibiotics to protect, and targets that countries sign up to.

ToolWhat it doesKey content
Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (2024)Ranks 24 resistant bacteria to guide research and developmentCritical: carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, carbapenem-resistant and third-generation-cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales, rifampicin-resistant M. tuberculosis
AWaRe classificationGroups antibiotics by how much to protect themAccess (first-line, e.g. amoxicillin), Watch (higher resistance risk, e.g. ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone), Reserve (last resort, e.g. colistin)
UN political declaration on AMR (2024)Global targets for 203010% fewer deaths associated with AMR than in 2019 (4.95 million); at least 70% of human antibiotic use from the Access group
What it is
The priority list scores pathogens on mortality, burden, resistance trends, transmissibility, preventability, treatability and how many new drugs are in development. AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) was introduced in 2017 alongside the WHO Essential Medicines List and is updated with it every two years. The 2024 UN declaration also asks countries to reduce antimicrobial use in food systems significantly.
Why it matters
The Access share is a simple, comparable stewardship indicator: you can compute it from sales or prescribing data without any laboratory. The priority list tells funders where new antibiotics are most needed.
How to read it
A country with a low Access share relies heavily on broad-spectrum Watch drugs, which select for the resistance on the critical list. The 2024 list moved carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa from critical to high, reflecting recent declines in resistance worldwide.
What a change means
A rising Access share with stable total use means prescribers are choosing narrower drugs. A rising Access share because Watch drugs are out of stock is not stewardship; check supply data.
Trap

Reading the priority list as a list of the biggest killers. It ranks resistant pathogens by need for new drugs and other measures; a pathogen can rank lower because treatments exist or are coming, even if it causes many deaths.

Analogy

AWaRe works like a fire service’s equipment rules: hoses for everyday fires (Access), foam for chemical fires (Watch), and the specialist kit locked away for the rare disaster (Reserve). Use the specialist kit every day and it will not work when you really need it.

6. Stewardship and what the evidence shows

Antimicrobial stewardship means using antibiotics only when needed, choosing the narrowest effective drug, at the right dose, for the shortest effective time; the evidence shows it cuts use without harming patients.

What it is
Hospital programmes combine enabling measures (guidelines, audit with feedback, education) and restrictive ones (approval needed for Watch and Reserve drugs, automatic stop dates). In the community, the main levers are prescription-only sale, delayed prescribing and rapid tests that rule out bacterial infection.
Why it matters
A Cochrane review of hospital interventions (Davey et al. 2017, 221 studies) found that they shortened antibiotic treatment by about 2 days (from 11.0 to 9.1 days; high-certainty evidence) with no rise in deaths (about 11 per 100 in both groups; moderate certainty), and probably shortened hospital stay by about a day.
How to read it
Most stewardship evaluations are before–after studies, best analysed as interrupted time series (Lecture 4.7). Look for a change in level and slope at the start of the intervention, a control series, and balancing measures such as readmissions and infection-related deaths.
What a change means
Lower use is an early outcome; lower resistance is a slower and less certain one (section 2). Expect use to change in months and resistance, if at all, over years.
Gulf example · Enforcing prescription-only antibiotics in Saudi Arabia

Selling antibiotics without a prescription was already illegal in Saudi Arabia, but was common. In 2018 the Ministry of Health began enforcing the rule, with fines of up to SR 100,000 and possible loss of licence. In a simulated-client study of 116 community pharmacies, antibiotics were dispensed without a prescription for sore throat in 96.6% of visits before enforcement and 12.1% a year after, and for urinary symptoms 87.7% before and 5.2% after (Alrasheedy et al. 2020). The study is a before–after comparison without a control group, but the effect is very large.

Trap

Judging stewardship only by prescriptions. Fewer prescriptions can mean sick patients went untreated, or bought antibiotics elsewhere. Track balancing outcomes (complications, readmissions, deaths) and, in the community, other supply routes.

Analogy

Stewardship is a water-saving campaign in a drought: fix the leaks (unneeded prescriptions), use the right amount (dose and duration), and save bottled water for emergencies (Reserve drugs). The aim is not to stop drinking.

7. One Health and the Gulf

Resistant bacteria and resistance genes move between people, animals, food and the environment, so controlling AMR in hospitals alone is not enough.

What it is
One Health treats human, animal and environmental health as linked. Large amounts of antibiotics are used in livestock and aquaculture, sometimes to promote growth; residues and resistant bacteria reach soil and water through manure, sewage and factory effluent.
Why it matters
A resistance gene that emerges on a farm can reach a hospital through food, travel or water. Surveillance and action are needed in all three sectors, with shared data.
How to read it
Look for the same resistance gene, or the same bacterial lineage by genome sequencing, appearing in animals, food and people. That is stronger evidence of transmission than similar percentages in each sector.
What a change means
A policy in one sector that lowers resistance in another is the best evidence for One Health action, as the colistin story shows.
Example · Colistin, mcr-1 and China’s ban

In 2015, researchers in China found mcr-1, a plasmid gene giving resistance to colistin, a last-resort drug, in bacteria from pigs, retail meat and patients (Liu et al. 2016). Colistin was widely used as a pig growth promoter. China banned that use from April 2017; production of colistin for animal feed fell from 27,170 tonnes in 2015 to 2,497 tonnes in 2018. On national pig farms, mcr-1 prevalence fell from 45% of samples in 2016 to 19% in 2018 (Shen et al. 2020), and falls were also seen in samples from people.

Gulf example · Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in the GCC

Carbapenems are the drugs of last resort for many gut bacteria, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) are on WHO’s critical list. A study of CRE isolates from hospitals in the six GCC countries found that most produced a carbapenemase (an enzyme that breaks down carbapenems), mainly OXA-48 and NDM types (Zowawi et al. 2014); later reviews confirm these remain the most common in the region. Both are carried on plasmids and are linked to South Asia (NDM) and to Turkey, the Middle East and North Africa (OXA-48), so the Gulf’s large migrant workforce, medical travel and the Hajj (Lecture 6.10) shape the mix. A standard control measure is to screen high-risk admissions, such as patients transferred from hospitals abroad, so carriers can be isolated.

Trap

Assuming that because a resistance gene is found in animals, the animals were the source for humans. Genes move in both directions. Only sequencing with dates and links (who, where, when) can suggest direction, and even then it rarely proves it.

Analogy

Three houses sharing one water tank. Cleaning the pipes in one house does little if the neighbours keep pouring dirt into the tank.

Check yourself

  1. A hospital cuts its use of a fluoroquinolone by half. Two years later, fluoroquinolone resistance in E. coli has barely changed. Has stewardship failed?

    Show answer

    Not necessarily. Resistance falls only if use drops below the level that balances the fitness cost, and that cost is often small or compensated. Co-selection by other drugs and community use outside the hospital also keep resistance up. Judge the programme on use, appropriateness and patient outcomes in the short term, and expect resistance to move slowly, if at all.

  2. A report says “AMR kills 4.7 million people a year”. What would you ask?

    Show answer

    Is that associated or attributable? 4.71 million is the associated figure for 2021 (deaths in which a resistant infection was involved). The attributable figure, deaths that would not have happened if the infections had been susceptible, was 1.14 million. Both are modelled with wide uncertainty.

  3. A national laboratory network reports that 60% of Klebsiella isolates are resistant to third-generation cephalosporins. A colleague wants to use this as community prevalence. What is the concern?

    Show answer

    Laboratory isolates come mainly from hospital patients and from cultures taken after treatment failed, both more likely to be resistant. Check sample types, settings, whether duplicates were removed (first isolate per patient), and the number tested. Community resistance is probably lower.

  4. After Saudi Arabia enforced prescription-only sale, simulated-client studies showed a large fall in dispensing without a prescription. How would you design a stronger evaluation of its effect on antibiotic use?

    Show answer

    An interrupted time series (Lecture 4.7) of monthly community antibiotic sales over several years before and after enforcement, looking for a change in level and slope, ideally with a comparison series (a drug class not affected, or a country without the change). Track the Access share too, and check for shifts to other supply routes.

  5. Why does a farm policy appear in a public health lecture on AMR? Give one piece of evidence.

    Show answer

    Resistance genes move between animals, food, the environment and people. After China banned colistin as a growth promoter in 2017, colistin use for animals fell sharply and mcr-1 prevalence on pig farms fell from 45% to 19% in two years, with falls also seen in people.
