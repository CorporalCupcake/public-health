Lecture 6.5

Vaccination programmes: effectiveness, coverage, hesitancy

Block 6 · Health protection
The point

A vaccination programme succeeds when an effective vaccine reaches enough of the right people, keeps working, and keeps their trust. Each link has its own measure: vaccine effectiveness (often from quick observational designs such as the screening method and the test-negative design), coverage, waning, safety signals and hesitancy. The designs are case-control and cohort studies you already know (Block 2), with traps of their own: who gets tested, who gets counted in the denominator, and the fact that vaccines protect the unvaccinated too.

1. Efficacy, effectiveness and impact

Vaccine effectiveness (VE) is the proportional reduction in disease among vaccinated people compared with similar unvaccinated people; efficacy is the same quantity measured in a trial.

\[ \text{VE} = 1 - \text{RR} \]
What it is
Efficacy comes from a randomised trial under ideal conditions (cold chain intact, schedule followed, a defined population). Effectiveness comes from observational studies of routine use, so it includes real-world problems: missed doses, mismatched strains, older or sicker people. Impact is the change in disease in the whole population after a programme, which adds indirect protection (section 2). RR is the risk or rate ratio, vaccinated over unvaccinated; with case-control data the odds ratio stands in for it.
Why it matters
Licensing decisions use efficacy; programme decisions (keep, add a booster, change the schedule) use effectiveness and impact. They often differ, and the reasons are informative.
How to read it
VE of 60% means vaccinated people had 40% of the risk of comparable unvaccinated people. Measles vaccines reach the high 90s; seasonal influenza vaccines typically reduce illness by about 40–60% in seasons when the vaccine matches circulating strains (US CDC). Always ask: VE against what outcome (infection, illness, hospitalisation, death)? VE against severe disease is usually higher than against infection.
What a change means
VE moving from 90% to 80% doubles the risk among the vaccinated (10% to 20% of the unvaccinated risk), so small changes at the top of the scale matter a lot.
Trap

Reading VE of 60% as “40% of vaccinated people will get the disease”. VE is relative: if 5% of unvaccinated people get influenza in a season, 2% of vaccinated people do. A second trap: “most cases were vaccinated, so the vaccine fails”. In a population with 95% coverage, most cases can be vaccinated even with a good vaccine (section 3).

Analogy

A seatbelt’s effectiveness is not the share of crashes it prevents; it is how much it lowers your risk of serious injury when you crash. Most people injured in cars may be belted simply because most people wear belts.

2. Direct and indirect effects

Vaccinating some people protects others by reducing transmission, so the effect of a programme is bigger than the effect of a vaccine on the person who receives it.

What it is
Halloran, Struchiner and Longini named four effects by comparing a population with a programme (A) and one without (B). Direct: vaccinated vs unvaccinated within A. Indirect: unvaccinated in A vs unvaccinated in B, the herd effect (herd immunity thresholds are in Lecture 6.1). Total: vaccinated in A vs unvaccinated in B. Overall: everyone in A vs everyone in B, the public-health payoff.
Why it matters
An individually randomised trial measures something close to the direct effect. It misses the indirect effect, so it understates the value of a programme for vaccines that block transmission. Cluster-randomised trials and before–after population studies capture it.
How to read it
A falling disease rate in age groups that were not vaccinated, starting when a programme began, is the signature of an indirect effect. Expect it for vaccines that reduce carriage or infection (pneumococcal and meningococcal conjugates, measles), and little of it for vaccines that only prevent disease (tetanus, which does not spread person to person).
What a change means
As coverage rises, the direct effect stays about the same but the unvaccinated face less infection, so the overall effect grows faster than coverage. Comparing vaccinated with unvaccinated people inside a high-coverage population can even make the direct effect look smaller, because the unvaccinated comparison group is also protected.
Example · Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine in the United States

After the seven-valent conjugate vaccine was introduced for infants in 2000, invasive pneumococcal disease in children under 2 fell by 69% by 2001, and in adults aged 65 and over, who were not given the vaccine, it fell by 18% (Whitney et al., NEJM 2003). Vaccinated infants carried and passed on vaccine serotypes less, so grandparents were protected too.

Trap

Counting only cases prevented in vaccinated people when estimating a programme’s benefit or cost-effectiveness. For transmissible infections this undercounts, sometimes by a lot. The reverse also happens: indirect effects can shift infection to older ages, where some diseases (rubella in pregnancy, varicella) are more severe. Dynamic transmission models (Lecture 6.2) are built to capture both.

3. The screening method

If you know the coverage in the population and the share of cases who were vaccinated, you can estimate VE in minutes, as a quick check rather than a definitive study.

\[ \text{VE} = 1 - \frac{\text{PCV}}{1-\text{PCV}} \times \frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}} \]
What it is
PCV is the proportion of cases vaccinated; PPV is the proportion of the population vaccinated (coverage). In words: compare the odds that a case was vaccinated with the odds that anyone in the population was. It is a case-control study (Lecture 2.3) whose “controls” are the whole population, summarised by its coverage.
Why it matters
Surveillance gives vaccination status of cases and programme data give coverage, so the method is cheap and fast. It is the natural first look when someone says “lots of the cases were vaccinated”.
How to read it
If the share of cases vaccinated equals coverage, VE is zero. The further below coverage it sits, the higher VE. At high coverage the curves bunch together, so a small error in coverage moves the VE estimate a lot.
What a change means
With coverage at 90%, half of cases vaccinated means VE of about 89%. If true coverage in the cases’ age group and area was 85%, the same data give about 82%.
Screening-method curves

Set coverage to 90% and move the share of cases vaccinated: most cases can be vaccinated while VE is still high. Then drop coverage to 50% with the same share of cases: the vaccine now looks useless. Watch how far the red points move VE at high coverage.

# Farrington's version: logistic regression with coverage as an offset
# cases: one row per case, vacc = 0/1, ppv = coverage for that case's age and area
fit <- glm(vacc ~ 1 + offset(qlogis(ppv)), family = binomial, data = cases)
1 - exp(coef(fit))                   # VE
1 - exp(rev(confint.default(fit)))   # 95% CI (Wald)

Each case is compared with the coverage in its own stratum (age group, birth cohort, area) through the offset, which removes the worst source of error: using one national coverage figure for cases from very different groups. The intercept is the log of one minus VE.

Trap

Using coverage from a different population than the cases. If cases cluster in a district with 70% coverage and you use the national 95%, VE looks much higher than it is. Coverage must match the cases by age, place and time, and cases must be ascertained regardless of vaccination status (if doctors test vaccinated patients less, PCV is too low).

4. The test-negative design

Among people who seek care for the same symptoms and get tested, compare vaccination between those who test positive (cases) and those who test negative (controls).

What it is
A case-control study nested in the testing pipeline. VE is one minus the odds ratio of vaccination, test-positive vs test-negative, usually adjusted for age and calendar time. It is the standard design for seasonal influenza VE networks and was used widely for COVID-19 vaccines.
Why it matters
People who get vaccinated also tend to seek care more. In an ordinary cohort, medically attended cases then overstate disease among the vaccinated and VE is biased down. Restricting everyone to people who sought care and were tested balances care-seeking between cases and controls by design.
How to read it
The design works if three things hold: care-seeking affects testing equally for the target infection and for other illnesses; the vaccine does not change the risk of the other illnesses; and the test is accurate. Check that controls are tested for the same reason as cases, in the same weeks.
What a change means
If the vaccine also prevents some test-negative illness, controls are depleted of vaccinated people and VE is underestimated. Low test specificity lets non-cases into the case group and pulls VE towards zero.
Test-negative bias explorer

Start with “Ideal conditions”: vaccinated people seek care 1.5 times as often, which drags the naive estimate down, but the test-negative estimate is right. Then try an imperfect test, and a vaccine that also prevents other illness.

library(splines)
# tnd: one row per tested patient; pos = 1 if test-positive
fit <- glm(pos ~ vaccinated + ns(age, 3) + ns(week, 4),
           family = binomial, data = tnd)
or <- exp(coef(fit)["vaccinated"])
1 - or                               # VE, adjusted for age and calendar time

Calendar time matters because both vaccination and infection are seasonal: early in the season few are vaccinated and few test positive, so ignoring week confounds VE. Splines let age and week enter smoothly.

From your ML background

The design is selection on a variable (being tested) that sits downstream of both the infection and care-seeking, which you know from Lecture 2.7 as collider territory. Here the selection is deliberate and helps, because it equalises care-seeking. It fails when the vaccine has its own path into the selection, the dashed arrow: then conditioning on testing opens a biased path, just as in collider bias.

Trap

Including test-negative controls who tested for a different reason (pre-operative screening, travel) alongside symptomatic cases. They do not share the care-seeking pathway, so the design’s main protection is lost. Restrict to people tested because of a defined symptom set, and analyse by time since symptom onset to limit false negatives from late testing.

5. Coverage and how it is measured

Coverage is the share of the target population that received the recommended doses on time; the hard part is the denominator.

SourceHowTypical problem
AdministrativeDoses given ÷ estimated target populationWrong denominator; coverage above 100%
Household surveyCard or caregiver recall for sampled childrenRecall error, lost cards, sampling error
RegistryLinked individual recordsIncomplete for private providers or movers
What it is
Standard indicators include coverage of the third dose of diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis vaccine (DTP3), the dropout between DTP1 and DTP3 (people reached once who did not return), and zero-dose children who did not receive even DTP1. WHO and UNICEF publish yearly national estimates (WUENIC) that reconcile administrative and survey data.
Why it matters
National averages hide pockets. Measles returns where local coverage falls below the herd immunity threshold, even when the national figure looks fine.
How to read it
Look at subnational coverage, timeliness (doses given on schedule, not just eventually), and dropout. A high DTP1 with a big drop to DTP3 is an access or follow-up problem; low DTP1 is a reach problem.
What a change means
Administrative coverage jumping after a census update usually reflects a new denominator, not more vaccination. Large mobile and migrant populations make denominators especially unstable.
Gulf example · Meningococcal vaccination for Hajj and Umrah

Coverage for pilgrims is enforced by entry requirements. For the 1447H (2026) season, Saudi health requirements ask pilgrims to show a meningococcal ACYW vaccine given at least 10 days before arrival: a conjugate vaccine (quadrivalent, or pentavalent ACYWX) within the last 5 years, or a polysaccharide vaccine within the last 3 years; a certificate that does not state the type counts as valid for 3 years. Serogroup A vaccination had been required of all pilgrims since an outbreak after the 1987 Hajj; W135 was covered after outbreaks linked to the 2000 Hajj spread to pilgrims’ contacts in several countries. Requirements change each season, so check the current list (Lecture 6.10 covers the Hajj in depth).

Trap

Reading administrative coverage above 100% as a data error to be capped at 100%. It is a signal that the denominator is too small (unregistered births, migrants) or that people from outside the area are being vaccinated there. Capping hides exactly the population you are not counting.

6. Waning and safety after rollout

Protection can fade with time since vaccination, and rare harms only show up once millions are vaccinated; both need continuous monitoring after licensing.

What it is
Waning is VE falling with time since the last dose: estimate VE separately by time since vaccination. Safety monitoring starts with spontaneous reports (Lecture 5.12) that raise signals, then tests them with self-controlled case series and related designs (Lecture 5.5), which suit vaccines because exposure dates are known exactly.
Why it matters
Waning drives booster policy; safety drives public trust. Vaccines are given to healthy people, often children, so tolerance of harm is much lower than for treatments.
How to read it
A real waning curve shows VE falling steadily with time since dose, within the same season or strain. A drop that coincides with a new variant or a drifted influenza strain is escape, not waning.
What a change means
If VE against infection falls but VE against hospitalisation holds, boosters may be needed only for groups at high risk of severe disease.
Trap

Depletion of susceptibles. Early in a season, susceptible unvaccinated people get infected and become immune, so the unvaccinated group becomes less susceptible over time. VE then appears to fall even if protection is constant. Lipsitch and colleagues showed this can create apparent within-season waning, and recommended comparing people vaccinated at different times before the season, at the same calendar time.

Analogy

A sunscreen test that compares burns at 4 pm: by then the unprotected people who burn easily have already gone indoors, so the remaining unprotected group looks tougher, and sunscreen looks as if it is wearing off.

7. Hesitancy and how to measure it

Vaccine hesitancy is delay in accepting, or refusing, vaccination despite available services; it has several causes, and measuring only “trust” misses most of them.

FrameworkComponents
SAGE “3Cs” (MacDonald 2015)Confidence, complacency, convenience
5C scale (Betsch et al. 2018)Confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, collective responsibility
WHO BeSD (2022)Thinking and feeling, social processes, motivation, practical issues
What it is
The WHO SAGE working group defined hesitancy and its 3Cs: confidence (trust in vaccines, providers and the system), complacency (low perceived risk of the disease), convenience (access, cost, opening hours). The 5C scale adds calculation (extensive weighing of information) and collective responsibility (willingness to protect others). WHO’s Behavioural and Social Drivers (BeSD) tools give short validated survey questions for the four domains in the table.
Why it matters
The fix depends on the cause. Low confidence needs trusted messengers; complacency needs risk communication; convenience needs clinics open after work. A campaign that answers the wrong C wastes money.
How to read it
Use validated instruments, report each component separately, and link answers to actual uptake from records where possible. Stated intention overpredicts behaviour.
What a change means
Rising complacency after years of low disease is the paradox of success: a well-controlled disease feels like no threat, which lowers uptake until outbreaks return.
Trap

Blaming low coverage on hesitancy when the problem is access. Unvaccinated people are often willing but face practical barriers (distance, cost, time off work, language). Measure both, and check for constraints before designing persuasion campaigns.

From your ML background

Hesitancy surveys are a measurement problem (Lecture 2.14): latent constructs, item wording, and validity across languages. Social-media sentiment models are tempting but measure a loud, unrepresentative sample; treat them as an early-warning signal and validate against surveys and uptake.

Check yourself

  1. During a measles outbreak in a city with 95% coverage, 40% of cases were vaccinated. A newspaper says the vaccine is failing. What do you say?

    Show answer

    With 95% coverage, vaccinated people vastly outnumber unvaccinated, so even a very effective vaccine leaves many vaccinated cases. The screening method gives VE of about 96% here: 40% of cases vaccinated is far below 95% of the population. The check is whether the coverage figure matches the cases’ age group and area.

  2. A cohort study using insurance records finds influenza VE of 20% against medically attended influenza, while a test-negative study in the same season finds 50%. Give one reason the cohort estimate might be too low.

    Show answer

    Vaccinated people seek care and get tested more, so their influenza is more often recorded. That inflates the count of cases among the vaccinated and biases VE down. The test-negative design restricts to people who sought care, which balances that difference.

  3. An individually randomised trial of a new vaccine finds 50% efficacy against infection. After the national programme starts, disease falls 70% in the whole population, including adults not offered the vaccine. Is something wrong?

    Show answer

    Not necessarily. The trial estimates roughly the direct effect. A programme also reduces transmission, so the unvaccinated are protected (indirect effect), and the overall effect can exceed the direct effect. You would still check for secular trends or changes in testing with a before–after design that has a comparison (Lectures 4.6, 4.7).

  4. VE estimated in a test-negative study falls from 70% in the first month after vaccination to 40% by month five, during one long season. Name a reason this might not be true waning.

    Show answer

    Depletion of susceptibles: as the season goes on, more unvaccinated people have been infected and are now immune, so the unvaccinated comparison group becomes less susceptible and VE appears to fall. Strain change during the season could also lower VE without waning. Comparing people vaccinated at different times, at the same calendar time, avoids the first problem.

  5. A survey finds 90% of parents in a district “trust vaccines”, yet coverage is 70%. What would you measure next?

    Show answer

    Convenience or practical issues: distance to clinics, opening hours, costs, missed follow-up doses (DTP1 to DTP3 dropout), and whether reminder systems exist. High confidence with low coverage points to access, not attitudes. Also check the coverage denominator.
