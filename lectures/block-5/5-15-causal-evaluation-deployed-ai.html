Lecture 5.15

Causal evaluation of deployed AI: accuracy vs impact; cluster and stepped-wedge trials; difference-in-differences on rollouts

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

An AI tool is an intervention, not a test result. Its value is the causal effect of care with the tool versus care without it, and that effect passes through people: someone must see the output, change a decision, and the changed decision must change the outcome. Accuracy is only the first link. This lecture shows why strong accuracy often buys little impact, how to rank the evidence, and which designs measure impact honestly: cluster trials, stepped-wedge trials, and difference-in-differences on staggered rollouts. Project 5B asks how many FDA-cleared cardiovascular AI devices ever climbed past the first rung.

1. Accuracy is not impact

Patients benefit only if every link holds: the model is accurate, a clinician takes up the output, a decision changes, and the new decision improves the outcome.

What it is
Accuracy (discrimination and calibration, Lectures 2.15 and 5.13) compares the model’s output with the truth. Impact is a causal contrast (Lecture 4.1): outcomes when clinicians have the tool versus when they do not. Different estimand, different study.
Why it matters
The links multiply. A model with an AUC of 0.90 that clinicians act on 20% of the time can prevent fewer events than a model with an AUC of 0.75 that slots into a pathway clinicians trust.
How to read it
For any AI paper, ask which link its primary outcome measures: AUC (link 1), alerts opened (uptake), tests ordered or drugs started (decision change), or deaths and admissions (outcome). Most papers stop at link 1.
What a change means
Because the links multiply, doubling adherence from 30% to 60% doubles events prevented. Raising sensitivity from 85% to 90% adds about 6%.
\[ \text{events prevented} \approx \text{cases} \times \text{sensitivity} \times \text{share new to clinicians} \times \text{adherence} \times \text{baseline risk} \times \text{RRR} \]

Read it as a funnel: each term is the share that survives to the next link. RRR is the relative risk reduction from acting on a true alert.

The accuracy-to-impact chain

Start with the default, then load “Excellent model, weak pathway”. Watch the specificity readout stay high while events prevented collapse. Then turn on alert fatigue and lower the PPV.

Example · EAGLE, the AI-ECG for low ejection fraction

EAGLE (Lecture 2.15) gave primary care teams AI-ECG results flagging likely low ejection fraction. New diagnoses rose from 1.6% to 2.1%. The cap was uptake: even with a positive AI result in hand, clinicians ordered an echocardiogram for only about half of those patients (49.6%, against 38.1% in usual care). The model was accurate; the gain was limited by what happened next.

Trap

“It has an AUC of 0.92, so it will help.” AUC says the model ranks patients well. It says nothing about whether the output reaches anyone, whether they act, or whether acting helps. Judge an AI tool on the weakest link, not the strongest.

From your ML background

This is offline metrics versus an online A/B test. Recommender teams learned long ago that a better offline score often loses online. There is a second lesson from uplift modelling: the patients at highest risk are not always the patients who gain most from being flagged. A tool that targets treatable, unrecognised risk can beat one that targets risk alone.

2. Why a good model can change nothing

Most failures happen after the prediction, in the human and organisational links, so they are invisible in a validation study.

FailureWhat happensWhere it shows in the data
Alert fatigueToo many false alerts; clinicians learn to dismiss them all, true ones includedFalling acknowledgement rate over time; low PPV
Workflow misfitOutput arrives at the wrong time, screen or personAlerts opened after the decision was already made
Automation biasClinicians defer to the tool even when it is wrongErrors that track the model’s false negatives
No available actionThe prediction is right but nothing effective can be done, or it was already being doneFlagged patients already treated; no change in orders
Example · The Epic Sepsis Model

A proprietary sepsis model was in use at hundreds of US hospitals. An external validation at Michigan Medicine (27,697 patients, 38,455 hospitalisations) found an AUC of 0.63, against the 0.76–0.83 its developer had reported. At the usual alert threshold it missed two-thirds of sepsis cases, yet alerted on 18% of all hospitalised patients. It flagged only 7% of the sepsis patients whom clinicians had not already treated with timely antibiotics. Weak accuracy, heavy alert burden and little new information: three links broken at once.

Trap

Assuming the worst case is “no effect”. Automation bias means an AI tool can cause harm: a confident false negative can talk a clinician out of a correct suspicion. Impact studies need safety outcomes and an analysis of errors, not just the main effect.

Analogy

A smoke alarm that goes off every time you make toast. It detects smoke perfectly well. Within a month the battery is out, and it detects nothing.

3. The evidence ladder for AI

Each rung answers a different question; only the upper rungs say whether patients are better off, and most AI tools never leave the bottom two.

What it is
A ranking of designs by how directly they estimate the effect of deployment. Silent (shadow) deployment runs the model live on real patients but shows nobody the output: it tests accuracy in real time and real data pipes, not impact.
Why it matters
Regulators clear many devices on accuracy alone (Lecture 5.14). Health systems buying a tool, and HTA bodies paying for it (Lecture 5.11), need the upper rungs.
How to read it
Bottom two rungs: is the output right? Middle rungs: did things change when it went live, and was it the tool? Top rungs: randomisation makes the comparison fair.
What a change means
Moving from retrospective to silent deployment catches data-pipeline and timing problems. Moving from before–after to a randomised design removes secular trends and co-interventions as explanations.
Example · Project 5B

Wu and colleagues reviewed 130 AI devices cleared by the FDA from 2015 to 2020: 126 reported only retrospective studies, and none of the 54 high-risk devices had a prospective study. Han and colleagues found 86 randomised trials of AI in clinical practice published from 2018 to 2023; 81% reported a positive primary endpoint, mostly diagnostic yield rather than patient outcomes, and 63% were single-centre. Project 5B codes each cardiovascular device into one rung, separates process outcomes (diagnoses made, echoes ordered) from patient outcomes, and asks whether recent clearances climb higher.

Example · An AI-ECG trial with a mortality outcome

Lin and colleagues ran a pragmatic randomised trial in two Taiwanese hospitals (39 physicians, 15,965 patients): physicians in the intervention arm received real-time alerts when an AI-ECG predicted high mortality risk. Ninety-day mortality was 3.6% against 4.3% (hazard ratio 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–0.99), with the benefit concentrated in high-risk ECGs (HR 0.69). Alerted high-risk patients received more intensive care, but the exact mechanism was not established. Contrast Mayo’s AI-guided AF screening study: AF yield of 10.6% against 3.6% in usual care, but non-randomised, with propensity-matched real-world controls, and a process outcome; whether screen-detected AF patients then avoid strokes was not tested.

Trap

Reading “prospective” as “impact”. A silent deployment and a prospective accuracy study are both prospective, and both measure accuracy. Impact needs a comparison of care with and without the tool.

4. Randomising AI: clusters and contamination

AI tools act on clinicians, and clinicians learn, so randomising individual patients leaks the intervention into the control arm; randomise the clinic, team or ward instead.

What it is
A cluster randomised trial assigns whole groups (care teams, wards, hospitals) to have the tool or not. Every patient in a cluster gets the same condition.
Why it matters
Contamination: a clinician who sees AI alerts for half their patients starts ordering echoes for the other half too, which pulls the two arms together and biases the effect towards zero. Clusters also match how tools are switched on, site by site.
How to read it
The price is the design effect you met in Lecture 3.8: patients in one cluster are alike, so they carry less information. Check that the paper reports the ICC, the number of clusters, and an analysis that accounts for clustering.
What a change means
With an ICC of 0.02, going from 50 to 200 patients per cluster raises the design effect from about 2 to about 5. Adding clusters buys far more power than adding patients per cluster.
Example · EAGLE’s design

EAGLE randomised 120 primary care teams from 45 clinics and hospitals, with 358 clinicians and 22,641 patients. The team, not the patient, was the unit, because a clinician cannot unsee an AI result. The effect (odds ratio 1.32 for a new low-EF diagnosis) was estimated with clustering taken into account.

In the Gulf

Saudi Arabia’s Ministry of Health now organises public care into regional health clusters under the Health Sector Transformation Program. A tool that is switched on cluster by cluster, or hospital by hospital within a cluster, is a ready-made unit of randomisation. If the rollout order is decided anyway, randomising it costs almost nothing and turns a rollout into a trial (next section).

Trap

Too few clusters. Eight hospitals randomised four-and-four can leave the arms unbalanced on case mix, however many patients they hold. Use restricted or stratified randomisation, and small-sample corrections in the analysis (Lecture 3.8).

5. Stepped-wedge trials

In a stepped-wedge trial every cluster starts without the tool and switches to it at a randomly chosen step, so the effect comes from comparing exposed and unexposed clusters in the same period.

What it is
A cluster trial in which the order of rollout is randomised. At each step one or more clusters cross over; by the end, all have the tool. The standard analysis (Hussey and Hughes) is a mixed model with a fixed effect for each period and a random effect for each cluster.
Why it matters
It fits the way health systems actually deploy AI: the decision to roll out is made, and resources only allow a few sites at a time. Nobody is permanently denied the tool, which makes it easier to approve.
How to read it
Exposure is tied to calendar time: later periods have more exposed clusters. Any secular trend therefore looks like a treatment effect unless period is in the model. Check for period effects, a clustering term, and a CONSORT stepped-wedge flow diagram.
What a change means
If outcomes are improving by 1 point per period anyway, a naive comparison of exposed and unexposed periods credits the tool with roughly the trend times the average time gap between them.
Stepped-wedge simulator: naive before–after vs the proper model

Eight hospitals switch on an AI tool one per period, in random order. Set the true effect to zero and add a downward secular trend: the naive comparison finds a “benefit” that is not there; the model with period effects does not.

library(lme4)
# sw: one row per patient; hospital, period (0-8), ai (0/1 in that hospital-period), death (0/1)
fit <- glmer(death ~ ai + factor(period) + (1 | hospital),
             family = binomial, data = sw)
exp(fixef(fit)["ai"])     # odds ratio for the AI tool, net of calendar time
exp(confint(fit, parm = "ai", method = "Wald"))

factor(period) gives each period its own baseline, which absorbs the secular trend; (1 | hospital) lets hospitals differ and handles clustering. The effect of ai is then a within-period comparison of exposed and not-yet-exposed hospitals.

Trap

Assuming the effect is immediate and constant. Clinicians learn a new tool, and alert fatigue builds. If the effect changes with time since switch-on, the standard model can be badly biased. Model exposure time (time since crossover) or report the effect by months since go-live, and allow a transition period after each switch.

Analogy

To judge new textbooks, compare classes that got them this term with classes still waiting this term, not this year’s exam results with last year’s. Exams may have got easier.

6. Rollouts that were not randomised

Most AI tools go live without randomisation; a staggered rollout can still be analysed as a natural experiment, if you compare sites at the same calendar time and check pre-trends.

What it is
A staggered rollout across sites is the setting for difference-in-differences with staggered adoption (Lecture 4.6): compare the change in outcomes at sites that switched on with the change at sites not yet switched on. With one site and a long series, use interrupted time series (Lecture 4.7).
Why it matters
It is often the only impact evidence a health system can produce after the fact, and the design most real-world AI evaluations should use instead of before–after.
How to read it
Look for an event-study plot: flat effects before go-live, a change after. Check that the estimator is built for staggered timing (for example Callaway and Sant’Anna), since two-way fixed effects can mislead when effects vary by site or time (Lecture 4.6).
What a change means
If early adopters were the keenest sites, their improvement may be enthusiasm, not the tool. A pre-trend that slopes the right way before go-live is the warning sign.
library(did)
# sites: one row per site-month; site (integer id), month, go_live (first month on, 0 = never), sepsis_mort
att <- att_gt(yname = "sepsis_mort", tname = "month", idname = "site",
              gname = "go_live", control_group = "notyettreated", data = sites)
es <- aggte(att, type = "dynamic")   # effect by months since go-live
ggdid(es)                             # event-study plot: check the pre-period

att_gt estimates an effect for each group of sites that went live in the same month, using sites not yet live as controls. aggte(type = "dynamic") lines them up by time since go-live, which gives the event-study plot.

Example · TREWS sepsis alerts

A prospective study at five hospitals monitored 590,736 patients with the TREWS sepsis alert. Among 6,877 septic patients flagged before antibiotics, those whose alert a clinician confirmed within 3 hours had an adjusted mortality 3.3 percentage points lower (an 18.7% relative reduction) than those whose alert was not confirmed in time. That is an informative and large study. But confirming the alert was a clinician’s choice, and prompt attention tracks staffing, workload and how obvious the patient looked. It estimates an association with prompt response, not the effect of switching the system on.

Trap

Comparing patients whose alerts were acted on with patients whose alerts were ignored. Acting is a decision made after seeing the patient, so the comparison is confounded by everything that drove the decision. The clean contrasts are site against site, or period against period.

Check yourself

  1. A vendor shows an AI-ECG with an AUC of 0.92 on data from three hospitals and asks your health system to buy it. What do you ask for before deciding?

    Show answer

    Evidence further up the ladder: external validation in a population like yours, then ideally impact data. Ask what action follows a positive result, whether that action is available locally (echo capacity), and what share of flagged patients clinicians would have found anyway. Offer a silent deployment to check accuracy on your own data, then a randomised or stepped-wedge rollout to measure impact.

  2. A hospital switched on a sepsis alert in January. Sepsis mortality was 12% lower that year than the year before. The press release credits the alert. What else could explain it?

    Show answer

    A secular trend (sepsis mortality has been falling for years), co-interventions launched at the same time (a sepsis bundle campaign), and coding changes: an alert makes clinicians label more patients as septic, adding milder cases that dilute mortality. An interrupted time series with a long baseline, or a comparison with hospitals that had not yet switched on, would separate these.

  3. Why did EAGLE randomise care teams rather than patients, and what did that cost?

    Show answer

    Clinicians who see AI results for some patients change how they treat all patients, which would contaminate the control arm and shrink the apparent effect. The cost is a design effect: outcomes within a team are correlated, so more patients are needed for the same precision, and the analysis must account for clustering.

  4. A stepped-wedge trial compares all AI-exposed hospital-months with all unexposed hospital-months and finds a large benefit. Outcomes were improving across the region throughout. Which way is the estimate biased, and what fixes it?

    Show answer

    It exaggerates the benefit. Exposed months are mostly later months, so they inherit the improvement. Including period effects compares exposed and unexposed hospitals within the same period, which removes the trend.

  5. Model A has an AUC of 0.88 but alerts mostly on patients clinicians have already recognised. Model B has an AUC of 0.78 and flags patients clinicians usually miss, with a clear treatment available. Which would you take to an impact trial first?

    Show answer

    Probably B. Impact depends on new information that leads to an effective action, not on ranking performance. Model A’s true positives were going to be treated anyway, so its marginal benefit is small. AUC alone cannot choose between them.
