Lecture 5.14

AI as a medical device: FDA, EU and SFDA regulation; managing model updates

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

An AI model that diagnoses, predicts or monitors disease is a medical device, and its intended use decides how hard regulators look. In the US almost all AI devices reach the market through the 510(k) route, which asks for similarity to an existing device, not proof of patient benefit. Learning models create a new problem: every retrain is a change to a cleared device. The FDA’s answer is the predetermined change control plan (PCCP). The EU’s answer is still being phased in, and the SFDA has its own AI guidance. Project 5B uses the FDA’s public list of AI devices to measure how much of this rests on evidence of impact rather than retrospective accuracy.

1. When software is a medical device

Software is a medical device when its intended purpose is medical, and its risk class depends on how serious the condition is and how much the clinician relies on the output.

What it is
The International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) defines software as a medical device (SaMD) as software intended for one or more medical purposes that performs them without being part of a hardware device. An AI-ECG running in the cloud is SaMD; the same algorithm inside an ECG cart is software in a device. IMDRF’s 2014 framework grades risk on two axes: the state of the condition, and the significance of the information to the decision.
Why it matters
Intended use is set by the manufacturer’s claims and labelling, not by what the code could do. The same model labelled “for research” or “wellness” and labelled “to detect low ejection fraction” are different regulatory objects.
How to read it
In the table below, category IV is the highest risk. Most diagnostic AI sits in categories II–III.
State of the conditionTreat or diagnoseDrive clinical managementInform clinical management
CriticalIVIIIII
SeriousIIIIII
Non-seriousIIII
Trap

“It only supports the clinician, so it isn’t a device.” In the US, some clinical decision support escapes device regulation, and the FDA revised that guidance in January 2026. But one of the statutory conditions is that the software does not analyse a medical image or a physiological signal. An AI that reads ECG waveforms is a device however it is framed.

Analogy

A kitchen knife and a scalpel can be made of the same steel. What makes one a medical device is what the maker says it is for.

2. FDA pathways: 510(k), De Novo, PMA

Nearly all FDA-authorised AI devices were cleared because they resemble an earlier device, not because a trial showed they help patients.

PathwayForWhat must be shownShare of AI devices
510(k) (“cleared”)Mostly class II (moderate risk)Substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate: same intended use, and differences that raise no new safety questionsAbout 96–97%
De Novo (“granted”)Novel low-to-moderate risk, no predicateReasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness with special controls; creates a new device type that later 510(k)s can citeAbout 2–3%
PMA (“approved”)Class III (high risk)Reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness, usually with clinical dataAbout 1%
Why it matters
A 510(k) usually rests on bench and retrospective performance testing. Predicates chain: a 2026 device may be equivalent to a 2021 device that was equivalent to a non-AI device from 2015.
How to read it
The submission number tells you the route: K for 510(k), DEN for De Novo, P for PMA. The public decision summary states the predicate, the intended use and the performance testing.
What a change means
A De Novo grant for a new type of AI device opens the cheaper 510(k) route to every competitor that follows.
Example · Project 5B: the AI-ECG for low ejection fraction

Anumana announced 510(k) clearance of its ECG-AI LEF algorithm, derived from the Mayo Clinic work in Lecture 2.15, in October 2023, after a Breakthrough Device designation. The clinical validation reported for clearance was a retrospective multi-site study of about 16,000 patients (sensitivity 84.5%, specificity 83.6%). The EAGLE cluster-randomised trial exists, and it is rare evidence of impact, but clearance did not depend on it. Most cardiovascular AI devices have only the first kind of evidence.

Trap

Reading “FDA-cleared” as “FDA-approved” or “proven to improve outcomes”. Clearance means the FDA agreed the device is substantially equivalent to a predicate for a stated use. That is a statement about the device, not about what happens to patients when clinicians use it.

3. The FDA AI-enabled device list as a dataset

The FDA publishes a downloadable list of AI-enabled devices it has authorised; each row links to a decision summary, and those summaries are Project 5B’s raw data.

What it is
The AI-Enabled Medical Device List on fda.gov (Medical Devices › Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Medical Devices), downloadable as CSV or Excel. Columns: date of final decision, submission number (linked to the FDA database entry and its summary), device, company, lead review panel and primary product code. The FDA finds devices by searching summaries and classifications for AI-related terms.
Why it matters
It is the closest thing to a sampling frame for regulated medical AI. By late 2025 it held about 1,250 devices; roughly three-quarters are radiology and about one in ten cardiovascular. It is updated periodically.
How to read it
The FDA says the list is not comprehensive: devices whose summaries do not use AI terms can be missed, and recent authorisations appear only after their summaries are published. The FDA has said it will explore tagging devices that use foundation models or large language models; as of late 2026 that is not a column.
library(readr); library(dplyr); library(janitor); library(lubridate)
ai <- read_csv("ai-enabled-devices.csv") |> clean_names()   # downloaded from the FDA page
names(ai)                                                     # check headers: they can change
cv <- ai |>
  filter(panel_lead == "Cardiovascular") |>
  mutate(decided = mdy(date_of_final_decision),
         pathway = case_when(startsWith(submission_number, "DEN") ~ "De Novo",
                             startsWith(submission_number, "K")   ~ "510(k)",
                             startsWith(submission_number, "P")   ~ "PMA"))
count(cv, year = year(decided), pathway)

This reads the download, standardises the column names, keeps the cardiovascular panel, derives the pathway from the submission number and counts by year. The real work comes next: opening each decision summary and coding its evidence (Section 8).

Treating “not in the decision summary” as “not done”. Summaries are short and inconsistent; some companies publish validation studies separately. Project 5B should search for publications for each device, and report the two sources separately.

4. Locked versus adaptive models, and the PCCP

A PCCP lets a manufacturer get a set of future model changes authorised in advance, with the testing rules written down, so each retrain does not need a new submission.

What it is
A locked algorithm gives the same output for the same input every time; an adaptive one changes with new data. Nearly all authorised AI devices are locked. Section 515C of the US Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, added in 2022, lets the FDA authorise a PCCP. The FDA’s final guidance for AI-enabled devices was issued in December 2024 and reissued in August 2025. A PCCP has three parts: a description of modifications, a modification protocol (data, retraining, testing methods and pre-set acceptance criteria) and an impact assessment of benefits and risks.
Why it matters
Without a PCCP, a change that could significantly affect safety or effectiveness needs a new 510(k) or a PMA supplement. With one, changes specified in the plan and carried out by its protocol can ship without a new submission.
How to read it
The guidance names three kinds of acceptable change: performance changes from retraining on new data within the intended-use population; changes to inputs, such as new sources of the same signal type; and limited changes to use, such as a specific subpopulation within the original indication. Changes must keep the device within its intended use. The plan must say whether updates are automatic or manual, and global or local to a site.
What a change means
A change outside the plan, or a planned change that fails or departs from its protocol, falls back on the usual rules, which usually means a new submission. Labelling must say the device uses machine learning and has an authorised PCCP.
PCCP sandbox: can this update ship?

An AI-ECG device was cleared with a PCCP. For each proposed change, pick what the manufacturer must do, then read the feedback, which follows FDA’s own examples.

Trap

“A PCCP lets the model keep learning on its own.” The FDA says it will consider automatically implemented updates, with clear guardrails, but a PCCP is mostly a pre-agreed plan for manual, periodic retraining with fixed tests. It also does not cover new intended uses. The FDA’s broader draft guidance on lifecycle management of AI-enabled devices (January 2025) was still a draft as of late 2026.

From your ML background

A modification protocol is a pre-registered evaluation plan for your CI/CD pipeline: fixed data sources, a sequestered test set that retraining never touches, the metrics, and non-inferiority margins written down before you see results. The regulatory novelty is not the retraining; it is committing to the acceptance criteria in advance, as a trial pre-specifies its primary analysis (Lecture 5.8).

5. Good Machine Learning Practice

Ten short principles, agreed by the FDA, Health Canada and the UK’s MHRA, set out what regulators expect of how an AI device is built and tested.

What it is
The Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP) guiding principles were published in October 2021. The IMDRF adopted a nearly identical set as a final document (N88) in January 2025. The same three agencies later issued joint principles on PCCPs and on transparency.
Why it matters
They are not binding rules, but reviewers in many countries use them as the checklist behind their questions. Four of them bear directly on the kind of evidence you will appraise.
PrincipleWhat a reviewer, or Project 5B, checks
Datasets represent the intended patient populationAge, sex, ethnicity and disease-severity mix; sites and device makes
Training data are independent of test setsNo patient, site or time overlap; how the split was made
Focus on the performance of the human–AI teamReader studies with and without the AI, not AI versus clinician
Testing under clinically relevant conditionsProspective use in the intended workflow, not archived data only
Trap

Quoting “AI matched cardiologists” as evidence for the human–AI team principle. A standalone comparison says nothing about what happens when a busy clinician sees the AI’s output. Automation bias, over-reliance and alert fatigue only show up when the team is studied.

6. The EU: MDR Rule 11 and the AI Act

In the EU, Rule 11 of the Medical Device Regulation pushes most diagnostic software into class IIa or higher, and those AI devices are also “high-risk” under the AI Act, whose duties for them now start in August 2028.

What it is
Rule 11 (MDR Annex VIII): software that informs diagnostic or therapeutic decisions is class IIa, IIb if a wrong decision could cause serious deterioration or surgery, and III if it could cause death or irreversible deterioration. Monitoring software is IIa, or IIb for vital parameters where changes could mean immediate danger. Everything else is class I. The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) treats an AI system as high-risk under Article 6(1) when it is, or is a safety component of, a product that needs third-party (notified body) assessment under laws such as the MDR.
Why it matters
High-risk status adds AI-specific duties on top of the MDR: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency to users, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, and post-market monitoring. A single notified-body conformity assessment is meant to cover both laws.
How to read it
Under the AI Act, a change pre-determined by the provider and assessed at the initial conformity assessment is not a “substantial modification” (Article 43(4)), which is the EU’s nearest equivalent to a PCCP.

Rule 11 walkthrough: from intended purpose to class

Classify an AI-ECG that flags probable low ejection fraction to prompt an echo. Try “Serious harm” and then “Less serious” for a missed case. Then switch to a monitoring tool and to non-AI software, and watch the AI Act status change.

Trap

Assuming the EU rules are settled. As of late 2026, two proposals are still moving: the Commission’s December 2025 targeted revision of the MDR would rewrite Rule 11 so that most software defaults to class I, and a separate proposal would move the MDR into the AI Act’s lighter Section B. Neither is law. Write “as of” next to every EU date you cite.

7. Saudi Arabia: the SFDA’s AI guidance

The SFDA requires marketing authorisation for AI devices and asks for clinical evidence at every risk class, with an explicit demand for local validation of devices approved elsewhere.

What it is
SFDA guidance MDS-G010, Guidance on Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) technologies based Medical Devices (version 1.0, dated November 2022, published January 2023). It covers AI and ML that diagnose, manage or predict disease, and sets out what is needed for Medical Device Marketing Authorization (MDMA).
Why it matters
It follows the IMDRF clinical evaluation model of three steps: a valid clinical association, analytical (technical) validation and clinical validation, which apply to every risk class.
How to read it
It is more explicit than the FDA on some evidence points, and less developed on updates: it has no PCCP mechanism. Changes go through the general change-notification rules (the last row of the table below).
AreaWhat MDS-G010 asks for
Reference datasetsPrevalence that reflects the target population; several centres; demographics matching the target region; not publicly available, so the model cannot have trained on them
GeneralisabilityMultisite investigation, subgroup analyses, justified sample size, local validation of devices developed and approved in other jurisdictions
ImpactCompare clinicians with and without the device, not clinicians against the device; prefer prospective real-world studies
ChangesNotify the SFDA through its electronic system within 10 days of a significant change, 30 days for a non-significant one
In the Gulf

The local-validation requirement matters for Project 5B’s framing. An FDA-cleared AI-ECG validated in US hospitals still needs evidence that it performs in Saudi patients, whose age structure, ancestry and disease mix differ (Lecture 5.13, Section 4). The curriculum adds the SFDA device register to Project 5B only if its entries can be searched publicly; check that in week 65.

Trap

Quoting the guidance’s example cut-offs (metrics below 0.6 “unsuitable”, above 0.81 “admissible for clinical validation”) as SFDA thresholds. The document labels them a device-dependent example, “not a rule”.

8. What regulation means for evidence

Regulators mostly certify that a device measures what it claims to; whether using it improves care is a separate question, answered by the designs in Lecture 5.15.

What it is
A ladder of evidence for an AI device, which Project 5B codes for each device. Each rung answers a different question, as in Lecture 5.13’s diagram.
Why it matters
Health systems, HTA bodies and payers need the upper rungs. The 510(k) route rarely requires them, so they are often missing.
What a change means
A PCCP makes this harder to track. Version n+1 may differ from the version whose study you read, and the public record of what changed is thin. Post-deployment monitoring (Lecture 5.16) becomes part of the evidence, not an afterthought.
Evidence levelQuestionTypical design
1. Retrospective accuracyDoes it agree with the reference standard on archived data?Standalone performance on a held-out or external dataset
2. Prospective accuracyDoes it work on consecutive patients in the real workflow?Prospective diagnostic accuracy study (STARD)
3. Impact on careDoes using it change decisions, diagnoses or process?Cluster or stepped-wedge trial; DiD on rollouts (5.15)
4. Patient outcomesAre patients better off?Randomised trial with clinical end-points
Example · Coding one device

For the AI-ECG low-EF device: level 1 is met by the retrospective multi-site clearance study, and level 3 by EAGLE, which raised new low-EF diagnoses in primary care, with one caveat: EAGLE tested the Mayo Clinic research version, so record whether it matches the cleared product (see the trap below). Level 4, fewer heart failure admissions or deaths, has not been shown. Most cardiovascular devices on the list will stop at level 1. That gap is Project 5B’s finding.

Trap

Grading a device by its best study when the study tested a different version or intended use. Match the evidence to the cleared indication and, where possible, the cleared version. A trial of a research prototype in one health system is not automatically evidence for the commercial product.

Check yourself

  1. A start-up says its app, which uses AI to read smartwatch ECGs and tells users “possible AF, see a doctor”, is “just wellness” and needs no clearance. How do you respond?

    Show answer

    The claim to detect a possible arrhythmia is a medical purpose, and the software analyses a physiological signal, so it is a medical device whatever it is called. Its intended use and labelling determine the class, not the marketing language.

  2. A colleague says, “Over 95% of AI devices are cleared by 510(k), so they must be low risk and well tested.” What is wrong with each half?

    Show answer

    510(k) devices are mostly moderate risk (class II), not low. And the route tests substantial equivalence to a predicate, usually with bench and retrospective data. It does not require prospective or outcome evidence, which reviews of the list show is uncommon.

  3. Under its PCCP, a manufacturer retrains on new data and specificity improves, but it used a new labelling method not in the protocol. Can it ship the update without going back to the FDA?

    Show answer

    No. A planned change that is not carried out by the authorised protocol is a deviation, and the FDA’s guidance treats it like a change outside the plan: it likely needs a new submission. The protocol, not the result, is what was authorised.

  4. An EU hospital asks whether the AI Act’s high-risk duties already apply to the class IIa AI-ECG it bought. What do you tell it, as of late 2026?

    Show answer

    The device is high-risk under the AI Act because it needs notified-body assessment under the MDR. But since the Digital Omnibus on AI, those duties apply from 2 August 2028, not 2027. The MDR applies now. Pending proposals could still change both Rule 11 and how the AI Act applies to devices, so check again before relying on it.

  5. For Project 5B you find a cardiovascular AI device whose decision summary reports only an AUC on 800 retrospective cases from two US sites. How do you code it, and what would change your coding?

    Show answer

    Level 1, retrospective accuracy, with a small, two-site, single-country test set and no calibration or subgroup data. Search the literature: a published prospective accuracy study would move it to level 2, and a trial of its use on diagnoses or outcomes to level 3 or 4. Check that any study matches the cleared version and intended use.
