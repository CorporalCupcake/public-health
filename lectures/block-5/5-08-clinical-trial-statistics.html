Lecture 5.8

Statistics of clinical trials: randomisation, intention-to-treat, interim analyses (moved from Block 3)

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

A trial is the benchmark every real-world study in this block is measured against, so you need to read one the way a regulator does. Randomisation buys exchangeability only if allocation is concealed. The estimand says which effect the trial targets once patients stop, switch or die. Analysis populations (ITT, per-protocol) are not interchangeable. Looking at the data early inflates false positives unless the alpha is spent carefully, and trials that stop early for benefit overstate it. Lecture 5.9 uses all of this to judge when a target trial emulation has matched its trial.

1. Randomisation and allocation concealment

Randomisation makes the arms exchangeable on average; concealment stops anyone from undoing it by choosing who gets enrolled next.

What it is
Allocation concealment means nobody enrolling a patient can know or influence the next assignment (central web randomisation, not envelopes on the ward). It is different from blinding, which hides the assignment after allocation. Concealment is always possible; blinding is not.
Why it matters
If a clinician can foresee the next allocation, they can steer sicker patients away from the new drug, and confounding by indication (Lecture 2.16) returns inside the trial. Schulz and colleagues (1995) found odds ratios exaggerated by 41% in trials with inadequate concealment and 30% where it was unclear; later meta-epidemiological studies found smaller but consistent effects.
How to read it
Look in the methods for who generated the sequence, how it was concealed, and whether block sizes were varied. A baseline table with large imbalances in a big trial is a warning sign; small imbalances in a small trial are expected.
MethodWhat it doesWatch out for
SimpleA coin toss per patientChance imbalance in arm sizes and covariates in small trials
Permuted blockEqual numbers in each arm every 4, 6 or 8 patientsFixed small blocks in open-label trials let staff guess the last allocations
StratifiedSeparate blocks within strata (site, severity)Too many strata leave half-empty blocks; analyse with the strata as covariates
MinimisationAssigns each patient to the arm that best balances several factors so far, usually with a random elementNeeds central software; analysis should adjust for the minimisation factors
Trap

Significance tests in the baseline table. If randomisation was done properly, any difference is chance by definition, so a p-value answers a question nobody asked. What matters is whether an imbalance is large in a prognostic variable, and the fix is to pre-specify adjustment for strong prognostic factors (section 5), not to test.

Analogy

Dealing cards face down. Shuffling (randomisation) is useless if the dealer can see the next card and decide who gets it (no concealment).

2. Estimands and intercurrent events

“The effect of the drug” is ambiguous until you say what happens to patients who stop it, switch, or die before the outcome can be measured.

What it is
The ICH E9(R1) addendum (adopted November 2019) defines an estimand by five attributes: treatment, population, variable (endpoint), how intercurrent events are handled, and the summary measure. Intercurrent events happen after randomisation and change how the outcome is interpreted or whether it exists: stopping the drug, rescue medication, switching arm, death. It names five strategies for them.
Why it matters
The estimand drives the design, the data you must keep collecting, the analysis and the missing-data assumptions. Lecture 4.1 gave you estimands as causal contrasts; E9(R1) is the regulatory vocabulary for the same idea, and target trial emulation protocols use it too (Lecture 5.9).
How to read it
Find the intercurrent events the trial expected and the strategy for each. If the paper never says, assume the analysis chose for it, usually by how missing data were handled.
What a change means
Moving from treatment policy to hypothetical usually makes a drug look better (it imagines away stopping for side effects) and requires untestable modelling assumptions.
Trap

Treating “ITT analysis” and “treatment-policy estimand” as the same thing when outcomes after stopping were never collected. A treatment-policy estimand needs follow-up after discontinuation. If the trial stopped collecting data when patients stopped the drug, the “ITT” analysis is quietly imputing them, and the imputation model decides the answer.

In the Gulf

The SFDA has been an ICH Regulatory Member since June 2021, so ICH E9(R1) is the shared vocabulary for trials submitted in Saudi Arabia, as at the FDA and EMA. The SFDA’s 2026 real-world evidence framework also asks for well-defined estimands in observational studies, which is the bridge to Lecture 5.9.

3. ITT, per-protocol and as-treated

Intention-to-treat compares groups as randomised and keeps exchangeability; naive per-protocol and as-treated analyses compare groups formed after randomisation, so they bring back confounding.

What it is
ITT: everyone analysed in the arm they were randomised to. Naive per-protocol: drop or censor patients who deviated from their assigned treatment. As-treated: analyse by the treatment actually received. A proper per-protocol effect (the effect of following the protocol) is a valid estimand, but it must be estimated with adjustment for why people deviated, for example by censoring at deviation and inverse-probability weighting (Lectures 4.3 and 4.5).
Why it matters
Who adheres is not random. People who stop a drug are often sicker, frailer or having side effects. Dropping them compares a selected group in one arm with a differently selected group in the other.
How to read it
ITT answers “what happens if we offer this drug”. When adherence is poor, the ITT effect is smaller than the effect of taking the drug. A large gap between ITT and per-protocol results is a reason to ask about adherence, not proof that per-protocol is right.
Example · The Coronary Drug Project

In the clofibrate trial, five-year mortality in the placebo arm was 15.1% among good adherers and 28.3% among poor adherers. Placebo cannot save lives: adherence was a marker of a healthier person. Comparing adherers on the drug with everyone on placebo would have credited clofibrate with the healthy-adherer effect. The overall ITT comparison showed almost no difference (20.0% vs 20.9%).

Trap

Calling an analysis that excludes non-adherers “per-protocol” and treating it as the effect of the drug. Without adjustment for the reasons for non-adherence, it is an observational comparison inside a trial, with all the biases of Lectures 5.2 to 5.4.

From your ML background

This is the A/B-testing rule “analyse by assignment, not by exposure”. Users who actually saw the new feature differ from those assigned who never opened it. Analysing by exposure reintroduces selection; ITT is the assignment-level comparison that randomisation protects.

4. Non-inferiority trials

A non-inferiority trial asks whether a new treatment is not unacceptably worse than an established one, and everything rests on how “unacceptably worse” was chosen.

What it is
A pre-set margin: the largest loss of efficacy you would accept in exchange for other advantages (no monitoring, fewer bleeds, oral rather than injected). Non-inferiority is shown if the whole confidence interval lies on the good side of the margin. Regulators (FDA guidance, 2016) build the margin from the historical effect of the active control over placebo (M1), then keep a chosen fraction of it (M2).
Why it matters
There is no placebo arm, so the trial cannot itself show that the control worked. It relies on assay sensitivity: the assumption that the control would have beaten placebo in this trial, as it did historically (the constancy assumption).
How to read it
Read the upper CI limit against the margin, not the p-value against 1. A wide margin makes non-inferiority easy and meaningless.
What a change means
Sloppiness (poor adherence, crossover, mismeasured outcomes) pushes the arms together, which helps a non-inferiority claim. That is why ITT is not automatically conservative here, and why regulators want per-protocol analyses alongside it.
Example · ROCKET AF

Rivaroxaban was tested against warfarin in atrial fibrillation with a non-inferiority margin of 1.46 for the hazard ratio of stroke or systemic embolism. In the pre-specified on-treatment (per-protocol) population the hazard ratio was 0.79 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.96): non-inferior. By intention-to-treat it was 0.88 (0.74 to 1.03): still non-inferior, but not superior. Same trial, two populations, two different headlines. This is the kind of trial that Lecture 5.9’s benchmarking projects try to reproduce in claims data.

Trap

“Non-inferior, so equivalent.” Non-inferiority only rules out being worse by more than the margin. The drug could be somewhat worse (case D), and a chain of trials, each non-inferior to the last, can drift towards placebo (“biocreep”).

5. Sample size and covariate adjustment

Power comes from events, not patients, and it falls with the square of the effect; adjusting for strong prognostic covariates buys precision for free.

What it is
For a time-to-event trial with 1:1 allocation, the number of events needed is roughly (Schoenfeld):
\[ d \approx \frac{4\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{(\ln \mathrm{HR})^2} \]
How to read it
With two-sided 5% alpha and 90% power, a hazard ratio of 0.75 needs about 510 events; 0.80 needs about 840. A modest-looking change in the assumed effect changes the trial’s size by two-thirds. Patients are then recruited until that many events are likely.
Why it matters
Optimistic effect sizes are the commonest reason trials are underpowered. Event rates lower than planned (healthier recruits) have the same effect, which is why many trials extend follow-up rather than add patients.
What a change means
Adjusting the analysis for baseline covariates that predict the outcome (pre-specified, measured before randomisation) narrows the confidence interval without bias. For a continuous outcome the variance falls by roughly the share of outcome variance the covariates explain; covariates with R² = 0.3 save close to 30% of the sample. The FDA’s final guidance on adjusting for covariates (May 2023) endorses this for both superiority and non-inferiority trials.
Trap

Adjusting only for the covariates that happened to be imbalanced. Choose covariates for their prognostic strength, before unblinding. And for odds ratios and hazard ratios, adjusted and unadjusted estimates target different estimands (conditional vs marginal, because these measures are non-collapsible), so state which one you report.

From your ML background

This is CUPED in online experiments: regress out the pre-experiment version of the metric and the treatment-effect variance shrinks. Same logic, same condition: the covariate must be measured before assignment, or it can absorb part of the effect.

6. Interim analyses and alpha spending

Every look at accumulating data is another chance for noise to cross the line, so a trial that looks five times at p < 0.05 has a false-positive rate near 14%, not 5%.

What it is
A group-sequential design fixes in advance how many interim looks there will be and a stricter boundary at each, so that the overall type I error stays at 5%. Pocock uses the same boundary at every look (about z = 2.41 for five looks). O’Brien–Fleming makes early looks very strict (z ≈ 4.56 at the first of five) and the final look almost unchanged (z ≈ 2.04). The Lan–DeMets alpha-spending approach lets the timing and number of looks flex while spending alpha along a chosen curve.
Why it matters
Data monitoring committees must be able to stop for overwhelming benefit or clear harm. Without a pre-set boundary, “stopping when it looks good” is optional stopping, and the p-value means nothing.
How to read it
O’Brien–Fleming is the usual choice for confirmatory trials: early stopping only for dramatic effects, and almost no penalty at the end. Pocock stops early more readily but needs a stricter final test, so the trial needs more patients for the same power.
Peeking at a trial five times

Each line is one simulated trial’s z-statistic as data accumulate. Start with no true effect and naive testing at 1.96 every look; then switch to Pocock or O’Brien–Fleming boundaries, and to a real effect, and compare how often trials “win”.

library(rpact)
of <- getDesignGroupSequential(kMax = 5, alpha = 0.025, sided = 1, typeOfDesign = "OF")
pk <- getDesignGroupSequential(kMax = 5, alpha = 0.025, sided = 1, typeOfDesign = "P")
of$criticalValues        # 4.56 3.23 2.63 2.28 2.04
pk$criticalValues        # 2.41 at every look
# Lan-DeMets spending with O'Brien-Fleming shape and unequal looks
sp <- getDesignGroupSequential(kMax = 4, alpha = 0.025, sided = 1, typeOfDesign = "asOF",
                               informationRates = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.75, 1))
summary(sp)              # boundaries and cumulative alpha spent at each look

One-sided alpha of 2.5% is the regulatory equivalent of two-sided 5% for a benefit claim. The asOF design spends almost no alpha at the 30% look and keeps nearly all of it for the end; summary shows the boundary and cumulative alpha at each information fraction.

Trap

Reporting the final p-value from a group-sequential trial as if it were a fixed-size trial. Once stopping depends on the data, the naive p-value, point estimate and confidence interval are all biased; the trial should report stage-wise adjusted inference.

From your ML background

The same problem as checking an A/B dashboard daily and shipping when p dips below 0.05. Industry fixes include sequential tests and “always-valid” p-values; trials use alpha spending. Either way, the rule must exist before the data do.

7. Stopping early exaggerates effects

Trials that stop early for benefit stop on a random high, so their estimates are too large even when the stopping rule controlled the false-positive rate.

What it is
At an early look, an estimate crosses the boundary only if it happens to be extreme. Conditioning on crossing selects those lucky highs: the “winner’s curse”.
Why it matters
Bassler and colleagues (2010) matched 91 truncated trials with 424 similar trials that ran to completion: the relative risks in truncated trials were on average more extreme (ratio of relative risks 0.71), with the largest differences when the truncated trial had fewer than 500 events. Meta-analyses that include truncated trials inherit the bias.
How to read it
Treat an effect from a trial stopped early, on few events, as an upper bound. Strict early boundaries (O’Brien–Fleming) reduce, but do not remove, the overestimate.
Winner’s curse in trials stopped early

2,000 simulated trials with the same true hazard ratio, five looks. Each dot is one trial’s estimate when it stopped. Compare the trials that stopped early with the true value, under naive and O’Brien–Fleming stopping rules.

Trap

“The boundary was O’Brien–Fleming, so the estimate is unbiased.” The boundary protects the type I error, not the point estimate. Those are different properties.

Analogy

Judging a basketball player by the night they scored 40 points, chosen because it was the night they scored 40. Their next game will probably be closer to their average.

8. Adaptive and platform trials

Adaptive designs change pre-planned features as data accrue; platform trials test many treatments against a shared control under one master protocol, adding and dropping arms over time.

What it is
Pre-specified adaptations include sample-size re-estimation, dropping arms, enrichment of a subgroup and response-adaptive randomisation. A platform trial runs indefinitely: arms enter and leave, and the control arm is shared. ICH E20 on adaptive designs reached its public-consultation draft in June 2025; check whether it has been finalised.
Why it matters
They answer more questions faster with fewer patients on ineffective treatments. The price is complexity: type I error must be controlled across adaptations, and comparisons with a shared control should use concurrent controls only, because care changes over time.
How to read it
Ask whether every adaptation was pre-specified, who saw unblinded data, and whether the controls compared with each arm were randomised at the same time.
Example · RECOVERY

The UK RECOVERY platform trial randomised hospitalised COVID-19 patients to several treatments against usual care, with a simple primary outcome (28-day mortality) collected largely from routine NHS data. Dexamethasone reduced 28-day mortality in patients on invasive ventilation (rate ratio 0.64, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.81) and on oxygen (0.82, 0.72 to 0.94), but not in those on no respiratory support. Other arms, such as hydroxychloroquine, were stopped for lack of benefit. The subgroup gradient by respiratory support is a reminder that the estimand includes the population.

Trap

Comparing a new platform arm with every control patient ever enrolled. Non-concurrent controls were treated under older standards of care, which is the same problem as an external control arm (Lecture 5.10).

Check yourself

  1. An open-label trial uses permuted blocks of 4 at each site and sealed envelopes kept at the nurses’ station. What worries you, and what would you have advised?

    Show answer

    Both concealment and predictability. Envelopes can be opened or held to the light, and in open-label trials staff can deduce the last allocation in each block of 4. Central web-based randomisation with varied block sizes (or minimisation with a random element) would protect concealment.

  2. An anticoagulant trial reports a hazard ratio for stroke with patients censored when they stop the drug. Which estimand is that closest to, and what assumption is hidden in it?

    Show answer

    A while-on-treatment or hypothetical strategy, depending on interpretation. Censoring at stopping assumes those who stop have the same future stroke risk as those who continue (non-informative censoring). People who stop after a bleed or because of frailty probably do not, so without weighting for the reasons for stopping, the estimate is biased.

  3. In a non-inferiority trial, a colleague says “ITT is the conservative analysis, so we only need ITT.” Do you agree?

    Show answer

    No. Non-adherence and crossover make the arms more alike, which pushes the result towards “no difference” and so helps a non-inferiority claim. For non-inferiority, regulators want ITT and per-protocol analyses to agree.

  4. A trial with five planned looks stopped at the second look with HR 0.55 and 90 events, crossing an O’Brien–Fleming boundary. How do you use this result in a guideline?

    Show answer

    The benefit is probably real (the boundary was strict), but its size is probably overstated: stopping on few events selects a random high. Use the estimate cautiously, look for completed trials or longer follow-up, and in a meta-analysis consider the truncation.

  5. The planned HR was 0.75 but a reviewer thinks 0.80 is realistic. Without arithmetic, what happens to the required number of events, and to the trial?

    Show answer

    Events depend on the square of the log hazard ratio, so moving from 0.75 to 0.80 needs roughly two-thirds more events. The trial must recruit more patients, follow them longer, or accept lower power. Adjusting for strong prognostic covariates can recover some of the precision.
