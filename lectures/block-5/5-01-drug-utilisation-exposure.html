Lecture 5.1

Pharmacoepidemiology foundations: drug utilisation, defining exposure from prescriptions and dispensing

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

In a drug study, “exposed” is not a column in the data. You build it from records: orders, dispensings, administrations. The rules you choose (which record, how long a supply lasts, how big a gap counts as stopping, which time window matters) can move the answer as much as confounding does. This lecture shows how to count drug use, how to turn records into exposure, and how to make those rules explicit. In Project 5A you will build anticoagulant exposure, hour by hour, from MIMIC-IV administration records.

1. Drug utilisation: who uses what, how much

Before asking whether a drug helps or harms, count who uses it, at what dose and for how long; those counts are the denominator of every safety question and often a finding in themselves.

What it is
Drug utilisation research describes prescribing and use in a population. Two WHO tools make counts comparable. The ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical) code classifies a drug in five levels: apixaban is B01AF02 (B blood, B01 antithrombotic agents, B01AF direct factor Xa inhibitors). The DDD (defined daily dose) is the assumed average maintenance dose per day for the drug’s main indication in adults: 10 mg for apixaban, 7.5 mg for warfarin.
Why it matters
Utilisation numbers give the denominator for pharmacovigilance (Lecture 5.12), show channelling (who gets which drug, Lecture 5.2), and decide feasibility. Prevalence of use (the share using in a period) and incidence of use (the share starting) answer different questions: safety studies of early harms need starters.
How to read it
Population use is reported as DDDs per 1,000 inhabitants per day. A value of 10 means that, on an average day, about 1% of the population takes one standard dose, if real doses match the DDD.
What a change means
DDDs rising while the number of users is flat means higher doses or longer use, not more patients. Incidence of use falling while prevalence rises means people stay on the drug longer, or the users are ageing in place.
Trap

Reading DDDs as doses taken. The DDD is a unit of measurement, not a recommended dose. Apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily, the reduced dose in atrial fibrillation (AF) and the dose after hip or knee replacement, is 0.5 DDD a day. A switch towards reduced dosing halves the DDD count with no change in the number of people treated.

Example · The Project 5A feasibility count is a utilisation study

Before any protocol, count: of ICU patients with new AF on the ECG, how many start therapeutic anticoagulation, with which drug, and how many hours after onset? If almost nobody is treated, or almost everybody, there is no one to compare with. That is a positivity problem (Lecture 4.1), and it is the project’s go/no-go step.

2. Prescribed, dispensed, administered, taken

Each kind of record sits one step further along the path from a doctor’s intention to drug in the body; use the record closest to ingestion that your data have, and say what the gap is.

RecordTypical sourceWhat it showsWhat it misses
Prescription or orderEHR order entry; MIMIC-IV hosp.prescriptions, hosp.pharmacyA clinician intended the drugNever filled, stopped early, doses held
DispensingInsurance claims, pharmacy systemsThe patient collected it, with a days’ supplyWhether and when it was taken; cash or over-the-counter purchases
AdministrationHospital medication administration records; MIMIC-IV hosp.emar, ICU infusions in icu.inputeventsA nurse gave a stated dose at a stated timeAnything before admission or after discharge
In the bodyDrug levels, anti-Xa, INR; electronic pill bottlesThe drug reached the patientRarely recorded outside trials
 
-- Therapeutic heparin infusions charted in the ICU
SELECT ie.stay_id, ie.starttime, ie.endtime, ie.rate, ie.rateuom, ie.amount
FROM mimiciv_icu.inputevents ie
JOIN mimiciv_icu.d_items di ON di.itemid = ie.itemid
WHERE di.label ILIKE '%heparin%';
-- Scanned administrations: enoxaparin, apixaban, warfarin, SC heparin
SELECT e.hadm_id, e.charttime, e.medication, e.event_txt
FROM mimiciv_hosp.emar e
WHERE e.medication ILIKE ANY (ARRAY['%heparin%','%enoxaparin%','%apixaban%','%warfarin%'])
  AND e.event_txt = 'Administered';

The first query finds heparin infusions with their start, end and rate. The second finds scanned doses that were actually given (other values of event_txt include “Not Given” and “Delayed”). The label search is only a start: you still have to separate line flushes and prophylactic doses from therapeutic anticoagulation, using dose, rate and route from emar_detail and the infusion rows.

Trap

Reading “no record” as “not given”. The MIMIC-IV documentation notes that the eMAR system was introduced during 2011–2013, so eMAR data are not available for all patients. For earlier admissions, an empty emar means missing data, not an untreated patient. MIMIC-IV dates are shifted for privacy, so you cannot read the real year from a timestamp; patients.anchor_year_group (a three-year range such as 2008–2010) tells you roughly when care happened. Restrict to periods with eMAR, or use orders for earlier ones and say so.

In the Gulf

The Saudi Ministry of Health’s Wasfaty e-prescription service links prescriptions written in its hospitals and primary care centres to community pharmacies, where the patient collects the medicine. The ministry reported more than 20 million prescriptions dispensed through it by the end of 2021. A record of this kind has both the prescription and the dispensing, so primary non-adherence (never collected) becomes visible. It covers only the ministry’s prescriptions; private-sector purchases are outside it. Whether such data can be used for research is a separate question of access and governance.

Analogy

A restaurant has the order slip, the kitchen ticket and the plate set down at the table. None of them proves the meal was eaten, but the plate on the table is much closer than the order slip.

3. Persistence and adherence: PDC and MPR

Persistence asks how long people stay on a drug; adherence asks how fully they take it while on it; PDC and MPR both estimate adherence from refills, and they disagree exactly when refills overlap.

What it is
The ABC taxonomy (Vrijens and colleagues, 2012) splits adherence into three phases: initiation (first dose taken), implementation (how closely dosing matches the regimen) and discontinuation. Persistence is the time from initiation to discontinuation. The medication possession ratio (MPR) adds up days’ supply and divides by the days in a period. The proportion of days covered (PDC) counts each calendar day at most once.
Why it matters
Adherence is an outcome in its own right (does a policy improve it?), a description of real-world use, and a dangerous variable to adjust for, because adherers differ from non-adherers in ways that predict health (Lecture 5.2).
How to read it
PDC of at least 80% is the usual threshold for “adherent” in quality measures. MPR can exceed 100% when patients refill early; PDC cannot. Report which measure, which period and how overlaps were handled.
What a change means
A PDC moving from 60% to 80% means one more day in five covered. An MPR moving from 90% to 120% may mean nothing more than earlier refills.
\[ \text{PDC} = \frac{\text{days in the period with drug on hand}}{\text{days in the period}} \]
Trap

Treating refill-based adherence as pills swallowed. Both measures show possession, not ingestion. They also say nothing about people who never filled the first prescription, the most common form of non-adherence for some drugs. Refill data cannot see it; e-prescription data with dispensing linked can.

From your ML background

Adherence measured during follow-up is a post-treatment variable. Using it as a feature or as a subgroup (“among adherent patients the drug worked”) is conditioning on something the outcome process can affect, the same leak as using future information at prediction time. Lecture 5.2 shows the healthy adherer effect this produces.

4. Building exposure episodes

Dispensing data give you dots; exposure is the line you draw between them, and the grace period and stockpiling rules decide where that line breaks.

What it is
Each fill covers a days’ supply (quantity divided by daily dose). Consecutive fills join into a treatment episode unless the gap between them exceeds the grace period (a permissible gap, in days or as a percentage of the last supply). Stockpiling (carry-over) moves leftover supply from an early refill to the end of the current supply. The episode usually ends at the last day of supply plus the grace period.
Why it matters
These rules decide who is a “current user” on the day of an event, how long persistence is, and when people are censored in as-treated analyses (Lecture 5.2). They are analytic choices, so they belong in the protocol with a sensitivity analysis.
How to read it
For chronic drugs, grace periods of 30–90 days or 50–100% of the last supply are common. For an acute effect (bleeding on an anticoagulant) the grace period should be short, close to how long the drug acts. Show results for two or three values.
What a change means
A longer grace period gives fewer, longer episodes, so more events count as “exposed” and persistence looks better. If the hazard ratio moves a lot with the grace period, the effect depends on events that happen just after stopping, which is a finding to explain, not hide.
Build exposure episodes from a dispensing record

Patient A has gaps; patient B refills early, then stops. Change the grace period and switch stockpiling on and off. Watch the episodes, PDC and MPR, and move the event day to see how one event is labelled under different exposure definitions.

library(AdhereR)
ep <- compute.treatment.episodes(
  data = disp,                          # one row per dispensing
  ID.colname = "id", event.date.colname = "date",
  event.duration.colname = "days_supply", medication.class.colname = "drug",
  consider.dosage.change = FALSE,
  carryover.within.obs.window = TRUE,   # stockpiling on
  maximum.permissible.gap = 30,         # grace period
  maximum.permissible.gap.unit = "days",
  maximum.permissible.gap.append.to.episode = TRUE,   # episode ends at supply + grace
  followup.window.start = 0, followup.window.duration = 365,
  date.format = "%Y-%m-%d")

This returns one row per episode (episode.start, episode.end). Rerun with maximum.permissible.gap at 15 and 60, and with carry-over off, and keep all versions for the sensitivity table. CMA7() in the same package gives a PDC-like adherence measure that handles carry-over.

Trap

Defining users as people with at least two fills, with follow-up from the first. To be a “user” they had to survive and stay in the data until the second fill, so the time between fills is immortal (Lecture 5.3). Either start follow-up at the second fill, or count everyone from the first fill.

Example · How much the grace period moves the answer

Take an anticoagulant study of bleeding. With a 0-day grace period, a bleed four days after the last tablet runs out counts as “unexposed”. If patients often stop because of early warning signs (bruising, a falling haemoglobin), bleeds pile up just after stopping, and the drug looks safer than it is. A grace period of a few days, or attributing events to the drug for a fixed time after stopping, protects against this. Lecture 5.3 calls it reverse causation.

5. Exposure definitions change the answer

Match the exposure window to how the drug could cause the outcome: acute effects need current use, cumulative effects need dose over time, and the mismatch dilutes or distorts the estimate.

DefinitionExposed if…SuitsTypical problem
Ever useAny fill before the eventRarely rightDilutes acute effects; easily creates immortal time
Current / recent / pastSupply covers the event day / ended within e.g. 90 days / ended earlierAcute effects (bleeding, arrhythmia, falls)Result depends on the grace period and on why people stopped
Cumulative dose or durationTotal DDDs or years of use up to the eventSlow effects (cancer, fractures)Duration tracks survival and adherence; needs a lag (Lecture 5.3)
Time-varyingStatus updated over follow-upMost cohort analysesNeeds methods for time-varying confounding (Lecture 4.3)
Why it matters
Misclassifying exposure is information bias (Lecture 2.8). If the drug acts only while taken, counting past users as exposed adds people with no effect to the exposed group and pulls the estimate towards 1.
How to read it
A good paper states the definition, the biological reason for it, and how results change under the alternatives. Current-use estimates far from past-use estimates, with past use near 1, fit an acute effect that wears off.
What a change means
An odds ratio of 2.0 for current use and 1.2 for ever use is not a contradiction: the ever-use group is mostly people who were no longer taking the drug.
Trap

Trying several definitions and reporting the one that “works”. That is the forking-paths problem with a pharmacoepidemiology accent. Choose the primary definition from the mechanism before seeing outcomes, and report the others as pre-specified sensitivity analyses.

Analogy

Asking whether umbrellas keep people dry by comparing “ever owned an umbrella” with “never owned one”. The question is whether they were carrying it when it rained.

6. Exposure in Project 5A

In the ICU the clock runs in hours, the same drug serves two purposes, and every interruption needs a rule; write those rules down before you look at outcomes.

DecisionWhy it is hardA defensible default
What counts as anticoagulationAlmost every ICU patient gets prophylactic-dose heparin; flushes keep lines openTherapeutic dose only: heparin infusion, treatment-dose enoxaparin, oral anticoagulant; prophylaxis is the background
Time zeroAF onset from codes is days late or missingFirst ECG showing AF without AF before (MIMIC-IV-ECG), aligned with eligibility
Initiation windowTreatment rarely starts the minute AF appearsStarted within a set number of hours of onset, handled with a grace period in the target trial (Lecture 4.5)
InterruptionsInfusions are held for procedures and restartedPauses of a few hours are not stopping; a gap beyond a stated limit is
Example · Prophylaxis is not “no anticoagulation”

If your untreated group is defined as “no heparin in inputevents”, many of them are receiving prophylactic subcutaneous heparin recorded in emar. The real contrast is therapeutic versus prophylactic anticoagulation. That changes the estimand, the bleeding risk in the comparator arm and the wording of your conclusion. Lecture 5.2 takes up the comparator problem.

Trap

Starting the clock at ICU admission and classifying patients by whether they were ever anticoagulated. Treated patients must survive until treatment starts, which hands them free survival time. Lecture 2.16 introduced this; Lecture 5.3 measures it.

Check yourself

  1. National apixaban use measured in DDDs per 1,000 inhabitants per day fell by 15% in a year, while the number of patients dispensed apixaban rose. What could explain this, and what would you check?

    Show answer

    A shift to the reduced dose (2.5 mg twice daily is 0.5 DDD), shorter courses (more post-surgical prophylaxis, fewer long-term AF patients), or poorer persistence. Check the dose distribution, the indication mix and the number of days supplied per patient. DDDs measure volume, not people.

  2. A study reports mean MPR of 1.15 for a statin and concludes adherence is “excellent”. Is that reasonable?

    Show answer

    Not on its own. MPR above 1 means supply exceeded the days in the period, usually from early refills or overlapping fills. Patients can over-collect and still have long gaps. A PDC, which counts each day once, and the share of patients with PDC at least 80% would be more informative.

  3. You are studying hypoglycaemia on a sulfonylurea. Your colleague wants a 90-day grace period “to be safe”. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Hypoglycaemia is an acute effect that wears off within days of stopping, so a 90-day grace period counts many unexposed days as exposed and dilutes the association. Use a short grace period close to the drug’s duration of action as the primary analysis, and show longer ones as sensitivity analyses.

  4. In MIMIC-IV, a patient whose anchor_year_group is 2008–2010 has anticoagulant orders in prescriptions but nothing in emar. How should your exposure algorithm treat this?

    Show answer

    Not as unexposed. eMAR was introduced in 2011–2013, so administration data are probably missing for that admission. Either restrict the study to years with eMAR coverage, or use orders as the exposure source for earlier years with a sensitivity analysis. Mixing the two silently creates differential misclassification by calendar time.

  5. A paper finds that current users of a drug have a lower risk of a bleed than past users who stopped in the last 30 days. Before calling the drug protective, what explanation should you rule out?

    Show answer

    Reverse causation: patients stop drugs when early signs of the outcome appear, so events cluster just after stopping and are labelled “recent past use”. Check why patients stopped, extend the exposure window for a few days after the last supply, and look at an ITT-like analysis where stopping does not change the label.
