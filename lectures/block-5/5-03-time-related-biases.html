Lecture 5.3

Time-related biases: immortal time, time-window, protopathic bias, reverse causation

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

Drug exposure changes over time, and so does the risk of the outcome. Most of the dramatic “benefits” that observational drug studies have reported and later lost came from getting time wrong, not from unmeasured confounding. They come in a small family: immortal time, time-window bias, time-lag bias, protopathic bias and reverse causation. Each has a recognisable signature and a standard fix. Target trial emulation (Lecture 4.5) prevents the first three by design; the last two need extra work. Project 5A starts after this lecture, so every fix here belongs in its protocol.

1. One family: getting time wrong

Every time-related bias is a mismatch between when exposure is measured, when follow-up starts and when the outcome process begins; draw one patient’s timeline and most of them become visible.

BiasWhere it livesUsual directionStandard fix
Immortal timeCohorts with exposure defined after time zeroDrug looks protectiveTime-varying exposure, landmark, or a target trial with one time zero
Time-windowCase-control studies with unequal exposure windowsUsually protectiveRisk-set sampling; same window length for cases and controls
Time-lagComparing drugs used at different disease stagesEarlier drug looks betterCompare drugs at the same line of therapy; adjust for disease duration
ProtopathicDrug prescribed for symptoms of undiagnosed outcomeDrug looks harmfulLag window; exclude early follow-up; check time since starting
Reverse causationAs-treated analyses; drugs stopped or changed as the outcome developsEither wayInitial-treatment analysis; attribute events for a period after stopping
From your ML background

The first three are look-ahead leakage and mismatched sampling windows, the bugs that make a backtest look brilliant. The last two are label-dependent features: the drug record is partly a response to the outcome that is about to happen, like a fraud model trained on a “card blocked” flag.

2. Immortal time: misclassified and excluded

If treated patients must survive until treatment starts, that waiting time is immortal; crediting it to the treated (misclassified) or simply deleting it (excluded) both bias the result, and only correct allocation of person-time removes it.

What it is
You met immortal time in Lectures 2.2 and 2.16. Suissa distinguished two forms. Misclassified immortal time: the waiting time before the first dose is counted as treated. Excluded immortal time: the waiting time is dropped from the treated group, but untreated patients keep all their time, including those who died early before they could have been treated.
Why it matters
Both make treatment look protective, the misclassified form more strongly. The bias grows with the length and variability of the wait, so ICU and post-discharge studies, where treatment starts hours to weeks after time zero, are especially exposed.
How to read it
A useful test: does the estimate get stronger as the window for “becoming exposed” gets longer? In Suissa’s 2003 study of inhaled corticosteroids after a COPD admission, the time-fixed rate ratio fell from 0.98 to 0.51 as that window grew from 15 to 365 days; the time-dependent estimate stayed near 1.
What a change means
In the same study, the time-fixed rate ratio for corticosteroid use within 90 days was 0.69; analysed with time-dependent exposure, it was 1.00. A 31% “benefit” was entirely produced by the analysis.
Four ways to count the wait before anticoagulation

An ICU cohort followed for 28 days from new AF. Anticoagulation has no effect on death. Switch between the four analyses to see where each patient’s waiting time goes, then lengthen the typical wait and move the landmark.

library(survival)
# base: one row per patient; time zero = AF onset; t_ac = hours to first
# therapeutic dose (NA if never); t_end, died = end of follow-up and status
d <- tmerge(base, base, id = id, death = event(t_end, died))
d <- tmerge(d, base, id = id, ac = tdc(t_ac))      # 0 before first dose, 1 after
coxph(Surv(tstart, tstop, death) ~ ac + age + sofa0, data = d)

lm <- subset(base, t_end > 48)                       # landmark at 48 hours
lm$ac48 <- !is.na(lm$t_ac) & lm$t_ac <= 48
coxph(Surv(t_end - 48, died) ~ ac48 + age + sofa0, data = lm)

The first model splits each patient’s follow-up at the first dose, so the wait counts as untreated. The second keeps only patients alive at 48 hours, classifies them by treatment before then, and starts the clock at 48 hours. Neither handles confounding that changes over time (a falling platelet count, a new bleed); that needs the methods of Lecture 4.3 or clone-censor-weight (Lecture 4.5).

Example · Statins and progression of diabetes

Lévesque, Hanley, Kezouh and Suissa (BMJ, 2010) re-created a published analysis in which statin users were defined by at least a year of use, counted from the start of follow-up. The time-fixed hazard ratio for progression to insulin was 0.74. With statin exposure treated as time-dependent, it was 1.97. The apparent protection came entirely from the year every “user” had to survive to qualify.

Trap

Thinking the landmark analysis is bias-free. It removes immortal time, but it drops everyone who died before the landmark, classifies people who start just after the landmark as untreated, and its answer depends on where you put the landmark. Use it as a sensitivity analysis, or pick the landmark from the clinical question (for example, “treatment decided within 48 hours”) and state it in the protocol.

3. Time-window and time-lag bias

Cases and controls must have the same amount of time in which to become exposed, and drugs must be compared at the same stage of disease; otherwise the comparison measures time, not the drug.

What it is
Time-window bias (Suissa and colleagues, 2011) arises in case-control studies when exposure is measured over windows of different length: a case’s window runs from cohort entry to diagnosis, while a control’s runs to the end of follow-up. Controls have more time to start the drug. Time-lag bias arises when a drug used early in a disease is compared with one used later, when the disease is more advanced and outcomes worse.
Why it matters
Both can produce large spurious benefits. Suissa and Azoulay (2012) reviewed observational studies of metformin and cancer: 13 had immortal time bias, 9 had not considered time-window bias, and others compared first-line metformin with second- or third-line drugs. The studies that avoided these biases found no effect of metformin on cancer incidence.
How to read it
In a case-control paper, check that controls were sampled from the risk set at the case’s event time (incidence density sampling, Lecture 2.3) and that exposure is measured over the same window before that date. In a comparative cohort, check that both drugs are at the same line of therapy.
What a change means
The bias is roughly proportional to how unequal the windows are. Doubling the control window relative to the case window roughly doubles the chance a control looks exposed when exposure accrues steadily over time.
Example · Statins and lung cancer

A large case-control study of US veterans reported that statin use was associated with much lower lung cancer incidence. Suissa and colleagues (Epidemiology, 2011) reproduced its design in the UK General Practice Research Database and found an apparent reduction of about 45%. Analysed so that cases and controls had the same exposure windows, the rate ratio was 0.99 (95% CI 0.85–1.16).

Trap

Matching cases and controls on calendar date or time in the database and assuming the windows are now equal. Matching on entry date does not guarantee equal time with the disease or equal time to become exposed. Suissa and Azoulay made this point in reply to critics of the metformin review. Define the window from the matched index date backwards, with the same length for everyone.

Analogy

Asking who has ever visited Riyadh Season, among people who arrived in Riyadh last week and people who have lived there ten years. Long-term residents “visited more” because they had more time, not because they like it more.

4. Protopathic bias and lag windows

When a drug is prescribed for the first symptoms of a disease that has not yet been diagnosed, exposure piles up just before diagnosis and the drug looks like a cause; a lag window ignores that recent exposure.

What it is
Protopathic bias (Horwitz and Feinstein, 1980): exposure starts, stops or changes because of an early manifestation of the outcome before it is diagnosed. It is reverse causation driven by the prodrome, the period when disease causes symptoms but has no label yet. A lag window discards exposure in a set period before the event (or, in a cohort, the first months after starting the drug).
Why it matters
It creates harms that do not exist, usually for cancers and other diseases with a slow, symptomatic onset. Classic case: proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and gastric cancer. Early gastric cancer causes dyspepsia, dyspepsia is treated with PPIs, so PPI starts cluster in the months before diagnosis.
How to read it
The signature is an association that is strongest in the first months after starting and fades with longer use, the opposite of what a carcinogen would do. Look for results by time since initiation and with lag windows of, say, 6, 12 and 24 months.
What a change means
An odds ratio that falls from 2.4 with no lag to near 1 with a 12-month lag says the association lived in the recent exposure: protopathic bias, not a cause. If it stays raised with long lags and grows with duration, a real effect becomes more plausible.
A lag window removes a spurious association

The drug does not cause the disease. Some cases are prescribed it for early symptoms in the months before diagnosis. Increase the lag window and watch the odds ratio return to 1; then lengthen the prodrome and see how long a lag you need.

Trap

Choosing a very long lag “to be safe”. A lag discards real exposure too, which costs power and, if the drug has a genuine effect after a short induction time, hides it. Choose the lag from what is known about the prodrome and the biology, and show two or three values.

Example · An ECG version

Suppose you studied whether a rate-control drug “causes” a new diagnosis of heart failure. Breathlessness and a fast ventricular rate in undiagnosed heart failure often prompt rate control before the echo is done. Exposure clusters just before diagnosis. A lag of a few weeks, and excluding people with heart-failure symptoms at baseline, are the defences.

5. Reverse causation in drug studies

Clinicians stop, start and change drugs as patients deteriorate, so in as-treated analyses the outcome partly decides the exposure; the fix is to tie events to the exposure decision that came before the deterioration.

What it is
The outcome, or its early phase, changes exposure. Drugs are stopped when a bleed begins, when a patient becomes too unwell to swallow, or when care moves to comfort measures. In an as-treated analysis the event is then recorded as “off drug”.
Why it matters
It can make a drug look protective (deaths happen after it is stopped) or harmful (drugs started as the patient worsens). It also inflates the apparent harm of stopping a drug: a raised death rate after people stop a statin partly reflects people stopping statins because they were already dying.
How to read it
Be suspicious of large differences between current and recent-past users, with recent-past users at the highest risk. Ask why people stopped.
What a change means
Extending exposure for a period after stopping (attributing events within, say, 7 days to the drug) should barely change a real effect. If it changes the estimate a lot, events just after stopping were driving it.
Example · Project 5A

In the ICU, therapeutic heparin is stopped when haemoglobin falls, before a bleed is formally diagnosed, and all anticoagulation stops when goals of care change. An as-treated analysis will count those bleeds and deaths as happening off anticoagulation. That makes anticoagulation look safer and more protective than it is. An initial-treatment analysis, or a per-protocol analysis that keeps attributing events to treatment for a set number of hours after stopping, protects against this.

Trap

Treating “drug stopped” as random censoring. Stopping is often the first visible sign of the outcome. Censoring at that point removes the patients whose events were imminent, and inverse probability weights only help if the reasons for stopping (falling haemoglobin, a new procedure, a change in goals of care) are measured in the data.

6. What target trial emulation fixes, and what it does not

Aligning eligibility, treatment assignment and time zero, as in a trial, prevents the self-inflicted biases; it does not stop doctors responding to symptoms, and it does not remove confounding.

BiasPrevented by a well-specified target trial?Why, or what else is needed
Immortal timeYesEveryone starts follow-up at assignment; a grace period is handled by clone-censor-weight
Time-windowYesA cohort with one time zero has no unequal exposure windows
Time-lagLargelyEligibility defines the disease stage; both strategies are offered at the same point
ProtopathicNoExclude people with prodromal symptoms at baseline; add a lag or exclude early events
Reverse causationPartlyThe initial-treatment effect is protected; per-protocol effects need measured reasons for stopping
Confounding by indicationNoNeeds measured confounders, a good comparator (Lecture 5.2) and bias analysis
Why it matters
Hernán and colleagues (2016) showed that many famous discrepancies between observational studies and trials came from misaligned time zero, not unmeasured confounding. That is good news: design fixes it. But a target trial emulation that has been specified carefully can still be confounded, and reviewers will ask how you dealt with what design cannot reach.
How to read it
A good emulation paper has a protocol table with eligibility, strategies, assignment, time zero, follow-up and the causal contrast, and states explicitly how protopathic bias and reverse causation were handled. Lecture 4.5 built the Project 5A version.
Example · The Project 5A checklist from this lecture

Time zero is the first ECG showing new AF, which is also when eligibility is assessed. Treatment strategies have a grace period of a set number of hours, handled by clone-censor-weight, so there is no immortal time. Patients with active bleeding or a falling haemoglobin at time zero are excluded, because those are prodromes of the bleeding outcome. Bleeds within a set number of hours after stopping heparin are attributed to treatment in the per-protocol analysis. A quantitative bias analysis (Lecture 2.13) then covers the confounding that is left.

Check yourself

  1. A study of beta-blockers after ICU discharge defines users as patients who filled a prescription within 60 days and follows everyone from discharge. Authors respond to criticism by removing the first 60 days of follow-up for users only. Is the problem fixed?

    Show answer

    No. That converts misclassified immortal time into excluded immortal time. Non-users still contribute their early days, including early deaths of people who might have filled a prescription had they lived. Count each user as unexposed until their fill (time-varying exposure), or use a landmark at 60 days for everyone.

  2. A nested case-control study finds that ever-use of a drug in the whole period before the index date is associated with lower risk. Cases have on average 3 years of history in the database, controls 6. What do you suspect?

    Show answer

    Time-window bias. Controls had twice as long to become exposed, so “ever-use” is more common in them regardless of any drug effect. Measure exposure over the same fixed window before each index date (say the previous 2 years), with controls drawn from the risk set at the case’s event time.

  3. Users of an antidepressant have a hazard ratio of 3.0 for a dementia diagnosis in the first year after starting, 1.4 in years 1–3 and 1.0 after that. How do you interpret this pattern?

    Show answer

    The pattern fits protopathic bias: low mood, apathy and sleep problems in early, undiagnosed dementia prompt antidepressant prescriptions. A causal effect of a drug on a slow neurodegenerative disease would not be strongest in the first months. Report results with lag windows and by time since initiation, and say the early excess is probably not causal.

  4. A colleague says your Project 5A target trial emulation “cannot have time-related biases, because it emulates a trial”. What do you say?

    Show answer

    It prevents immortal time and time-window bias by aligning time zero, eligibility and assignment. It does not prevent protopathic bias (anticoagulation withheld or stopped because a bleed is starting) or reverse causation in per-protocol analyses, and it does nothing about confounding by indication. Those need baseline exclusions, lag or attribution windows, measured reasons for stopping and bias analysis.

  5. A cohort compares patients on first-line metformin with patients on insulin and finds lower cancer incidence with metformin. The authors adjusted for age, sex and BMI. What time-related bias remains, and how would you fix it?

    Show answer

    Time-lag bias: insulin is usually started years later in more advanced diabetes, so insulin users are at a different stage with different risks. Compare drugs used at the same line of therapy (for example two second-line options), or adjust for diabetes duration and line of therapy, with follow-up starting at each drug’s initiation.
