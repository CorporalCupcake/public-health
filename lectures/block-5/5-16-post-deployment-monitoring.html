Lecture 5.16

Post-deployment monitoring: drift, performance decay, feedback loops

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

A validated model is validated for the patients, practice and coding of the past. Once deployed, all three keep changing, and the model itself changes the care it was trained on. Monitoring is pharmacovigilance for algorithms (Lecture 5.12): watch the right signals, use control charts that tell real change from noise, know which kind of drift you are seeing, and have a named person who acts. The hardest case is the feedback loop, where the model’s success makes it look broken.

1. Why deployed models decay: three kinds of drift

A model learns a joint distribution of patients and outcomes; drift is any change in that distribution, and which part changes decides what breaks.

DriftWhat changesTypical causeWhat it usually breaks
Covariate shiftWho arrives: the mix of predictors, P(X)New referral pattern, new site, older populationDiscrimination moves with case mix; calibration holds only if the model is right for the new patients
Prior (label) shiftHow common the outcome is, P(Y)Epidemic, new screening test, new outcome definition or codingCalibration-in-the-large; ranking may survive
Concept driftWhat the predictors mean, P(Y | X)New treatment, practice change, new lab assayBoth calibration and discrimination
Why it matters
Each type needs a different response. Prior shift is often fixed by updating the intercept; concept drift needs new coefficients or a new model; covariate shift may need nothing, or a model that was never validated on these patients.
How to read it
Ask three questions when performance moves: did the inputs change, did the outcome rate change, or did the link between them change? Data errors (a broken feed, a renamed lab code) mimic all three, so rule them out first.
What a change means
An observed-to-expected ratio moving from 1.0 to 1.3 means 30% more events than the model predicts. If discrimination is unchanged, suspect prior shift or a coding change before concept drift.
Example · Sepsis alerts and COVID-19

Across 24 US hospitals in four health systems, daily alerts from the Epic Sepsis Model rose by 43% after the pandemic began, even though hospital census fell by 35%. Case mix had shifted abruptly towards patients with fever and organ dysfunction from a viral illness. At Michigan Medicine, nurses reported alert volumes that felt untenable, and the hospital’s clinical AI governance committee stopped using the tool. Finlayson and colleagues use the case to show how a change in what a fever means (concept drift) breaks a sepsis model.

Trap

“The AUC has held, so the model is fine.” Davis and colleagues followed models for hospital-acquired acute kidney injury across nine years of US Veterans Affairs admissions: discrimination held up, while calibration drifted into steady overprediction as AKI rates changed. A stable AUC with drifting calibration means the ranking is right and the numbers are wrong. Any decision made on a threshold is then wrong too.

From your ML background

Same taxonomy as the dataset-shift literature. Under pure label shift you can estimate the new outcome rate from the model’s own outputs on unlabelled data (black-box shift estimation) and correct the intercept. Under covariate shift, importance weighting reweights old labelled data to look like new patients. Concept drift has no shortcut: you need new labels.

2. Monitoring with and without labels

Labels (the true outcomes) arrive late, sometimes a year late, so monitoring runs in two speeds: fast signals that need no labels, and slow performance checks once they arrive.

SignalAvailableCatchesMisses
Inputs: missingness, distributions, new codesSame dayBroken feeds, covariate shiftConcept drift; tells you nothing about harm
Outputs: score distribution, alert rate, overridesSame daySudden shifts, alert-burden problemsWhether the alerts are right
Performance: calibration, discrimination, by subgroupWhen labels arrive (days to a year)All three drift typesAnything that happened since the last labels
Why it matters
A 1-year mortality model cannot be checked on last month’s patients. Fast signals are your early warning; labelled performance is the verdict.
How to read it
An input alarm is a reason to look, not proof of harm. A falling alert rate with stable inputs points at the model or its plumbing; a rising rate with shifting inputs points at the population.
What a change means
If the alert rate jumps from 5% to 9% of patients overnight, check for a data or software change first. Real populations rarely change that fast, except in a pandemic.
Trap

Treating a change in the labels as a change in the patients. A new outcome definition (moving to Sepsis-3 criteria, say) or a new coding version changes P(Y) on paper overnight. The model looks miscalibrated against labels that now mean something else. Check the label pipeline before the model, as you would check outcome validation in Lecture 5.7.

Analogy

A car’s dashboard warning lights and its arrival time. The lights (inputs and outputs) come on instantly but do not say whether you will be late. Only arriving tells you that, and by then you are already there.

3. Calibration drift and recalibration

Most drift shows first as miscalibration, and most miscalibration can be fixed with the smallest update that works: an intercept, then a slope, and only then a new model.

What it is
Calibration-in-the-large compares the observed event rate with the mean predicted risk. The calibration slope shows whether predictions are too extreme (slope below 1) or too timid (above 1). Recalibration refits these two numbers on recent data and leaves the model’s internal weights alone.
Why it matters
Updating two parameters needs a few hundred events; refitting a model needs thousands, and a new model is a new device, which may need a regulatory route (the PCCP in Lecture 5.14).
How to read it
Observed/expected near 1 and slope near 1: fine. O/E off but slope fine: update the intercept. Slope off: logistic recalibration. Discrimination falling: recalibration will not help; the model needs new information.
What a change means
An intercept update moving from 0 to +0.3 on the log-odds scale raises every predicted odds by about 35%. It shifts all risks up without changing who ranks above whom.
new$lp <- qlogis(new$p_hat)                         # model's linear predictor
itl   <- glm(y ~ offset(lp), family = binomial, data = new)   # intercept only
slope <- glm(y ~ lp, family = binomial, data = new)           # intercept and slope
coef(itl); coef(slope)            # near 0, and near (0, 1), mean no drift
new$p_recal <- predict(slope, type = "response")    # recalibrated risks
library(CalibrationCurves); val.prob.ci.2(new$p_recal, new$y)   # check the curve

The first model asks only “is the average risk off?”; the second also asks “are the risks too spread out or too squashed?”. Use recent patients only, and validate the update on the months after, not the months you fitted it on.

Trap

Retraining from scratch at the first sign of drift. A full refit on a small recent sample overfits, throws away years of information, and creates a new device to validate. In Davis’s kidney injury models, the drift tracked the changing AKI rate, which an intercept update addresses directly.

4. Telling change from noise: control charts

Monthly performance bounces around; a control chart accumulates small, persistent deviations and raises an alarm only when they add up to more than noise would.

What it is
Statistical process control, borrowed from manufacturing. A CUSUM (cumulative sum) chart adds up each month’s standardised excess of observed over expected events, minus an allowance, and resets at zero. An EWMA chart tracks an exponentially weighted moving average, which forgets old months gradually.
Why it matters
Testing each month at p < 0.05 gives a false alarm every 20 months and is slow to see a small shift. CUSUM sees small persistent shifts quickly while keeping false alarms rare.
How to read it
The allowance \(k\) is about half the shift you care about, in standard-deviation units (0.5 targets a 1-SD shift). The limit \(h\) sets the false-alarm rate: 4–5 is typical. The chart says when the change began: look back to where the sum started climbing.
What a change means
Lowering \(h\) from 5 to 3 catches drift sooner but trips more often on noise. Choose it by how costly a false alarm is compared with a missed drift.
\[ S_t = \max\!\left(0,\; S_{t-1} + z_t - k\right) \]

Here \(z_t\) is month \(t\)’s observed-minus-expected events, divided by its standard error. Alarm when \(S_t\) passes \(h\). Run a mirror-image chart for deficits.

Drift monitor: a deployed risk model, month by month

Inject one kind of drift at a time. Prevalence shift trips the CUSUM; covariate shift lowers the AUC but leaves calibration alone; concept drift does both. After an alarm, press Recalibrate and see which problems it fixes.

library(dplyr)
m <- scored |> group_by(month) |>
  summarise(z = (sum(y) - sum(p_hat)) / sqrt(sum(p_hat * (1 - p_hat))))
k <- 0.5; h <- 5
m$cusum <- Reduce(function(s, z) max(0, s + z - k), m$z, 0, accumulate = TRUE)[-1]
m$month[which(m$cusum > h)[1]]     # first alarm (NA if none)

Each month gives one standardised excess, z; Reduce(..., accumulate = TRUE) runs the CUSUM along the months. Repeat with -z for a chart that catches overprediction.

Example · Borrowed from surgery

CUSUM charts have long been used to monitor surgeons’ and units’ outcomes against risk-adjusted expectations. A model-monitoring chart is the same tool, with the deployed model as the risk adjustment and the observed events as the outcome.

Analogy

Your bathroom scale. One heavy morning means nothing. A running tally of how far above target you have been, which forgives small dips, will catch a real gain within weeks.

5. Feedback loops: when the model changes what it predicts

If flagged patients are treated and their events prevented, the data now say those patients were low risk; the model’s success hides its own signal.

What it is
A prediction that changes the outcome it predicts. In ML this is performative prediction. Lenert and colleagues put it bluntly: prognostic models will be victims of their own success.
Why it matters
Two things go wrong. Monitoring sees PPV fall and calls the model broken when it is working. Retraining on post-deployment data learns that high-risk features predict few events, so the next model stops flagging the people who most need it.
How to read it
If observed risk falls mainly among flagged patients, suspect the intervention, not decay. Compare flagged patients’ outcomes with what the model predicted for them without intervention, which needs a comparison group.
What a change means
An intervention that halves risk among flagged patients halves the observed PPV. A PPV drop from 40% to 20% can mean the model is doing exactly what it was meant to do.
Feedback loop: retraining on the model’s own success

Patients above the threshold get an intervention that prevents some events. Step through retraining rounds with naive retraining, then switch to retraining that records who was treated.

Example · A deterioration alert on the wards

An early-warning model sends flagged ward patients for a rapid-response review, and many are moved to intensive care early. A year later, the data show that patients with the highest scores rarely had a cardiac arrest on the ward. A model retrained naively learns that those scores are reassuring. Recording the intervention, or keeping a small randomised share of alerts unshown, preserves the information needed to tell the truth.

Trap

Fixing the feedback loop by retraining only on unflagged patients. They are a selected group (everyone below the old threshold), so the model loses the high-risk range entirely. Record treatment and model it, or keep a randomised hold-out: a causal problem needs a causal fix.

From your ML background

The same trap as a recommender trained on its own recommendations, or a fraud model trained on cases the old model flagged for review. The ML fixes carry over: log the action taken, keep an exploration slice (a small share of patients handled without the model), and use off-policy evaluation with known propensities.

6. Equity monitoring

Drift rarely hits everyone equally, so monitor calibration, alert rates and actions in each group the tool serves, not just overall.

What it is
Running the same monitoring by subgroup: sex, age, nationality, region, insurance or payer. Check calibration in each group (are risks right?), alert and action rates (who gets flagged, who gets treated), and outcomes.
Why it matters
A model can be well calibrated overall and badly miscalibrated in a minority group, whose share of the data is too small to move the overall numbers.
How to read it
Equal AUC across groups does not mean equal treatment: a group can be ranked well yet systematically under-predicted, so fewer of its members cross the alert threshold.
Example · The label is the bias

Obermeyer and colleagues studied a widely used US algorithm that predicted health-care costs as a stand-in for health needs. Because less was spent on Black patients with the same illness burden, Black patients at a given risk score were considerably sicker than White patients. The model predicted its label well; the label was wrong. Monitoring cost-calibration would never have found it; monitoring health outcomes by group did.

In the Gulf

In Saudi Arabia and the wider GCC, nationals and non-national residents differ sharply in age structure, sex ratio and access to care. A deployed model should report calibration and alert rates separately for nationals and non-nationals, and by region. Some groups will be small, so use longer windows or pooled control charts rather than reading monthly noise as bias.

Trap

Reading every subgroup difference in a small group as unfairness, or every non-significant one as fairness. Report the intervals; with 40 events a year in a group, a calibration difference of 30% can be noise, and a real one can hide.

7. Governance: who acts

A monitoring dashboard that nobody owns is decoration; write down before go-live who watches, what triggers action, and who may pause the tool.

What it is
A monitoring plan with a named owner, the metrics and their limits, the cadence, the actions allowed (recalibrate, retrain, pause, roll back to the last version) and who signs each one off. Every update is versioned, and clinicians are told what changed.
Why it matters
Regulators increasingly expect it. In the US, planned model updates go through a predetermined change control plan (Lecture 5.14). In Saudi Arabia, the SFDA’s guidance on AI/ML-enabled medical devices (MDS-G010, first issued January 2023) asks manufacturers for a post-market surveillance plan and continuous monitoring of safety, effectiveness and performance, because these devices can keep learning.
How to read it
A good plan answers: who gets the alarm, within what time they must respond, and what happens to clinical use while they diagnose.
Example · Pausing is an action

Michigan Medicine’s response to its COVID-era sepsis alerts (Section 1) was a governance decision: a committee with the authority to stop a tool used it. Rolling back to a previous version, or to no model, is a legitimate outcome of monitoring, and the plan should say so in advance.

Trap

Leaving monitoring to the vendor alone. The vendor sees its own metrics across many sites; your hospital’s case mix, coding and workflow are what drift. The deploying organisation needs its own local monitoring, as well as the manufacturer’s.

Check yourself

  1. Six months after go-live, a readmission model’s AUC is unchanged but it predicts 12% readmissions when 18% occur. What kind of drift is most likely, and what is the smallest fix?

    Show answer

    Prior shift (or a change in how readmissions are counted): the outcome rate moved while ranking held. Check the label pipeline first. If the change is real, update the intercept on recent data, then confirm calibration on the following months. No retraining needed.

  2. The alert rate of a deterioration model doubled overnight. Labels will not arrive for a month. What do you do first?

    Show answer

    Check the plumbing: input distributions and missingness, a new lab code or unit, a software release, a change of EHR feed. Populations rarely double their risk overnight. If a data fault is found, fix it and consider pausing the tool until outputs return to their usual range.

  3. A colleague proposes testing calibration every month and acting whenever p < 0.05. Why would you suggest a CUSUM instead?

    Show answer

    Monthly tests give a false alarm about once every 20 months and are poor at seeing small persistent drift, because each month is judged alone. A CUSUM accumulates evidence across months, so it catches small sustained shifts sooner while keeping false alarms rare, and it shows when the drift started.

  4. After a year, the PPV of a sepsis alert has fallen from 30% to 18%. The vendor proposes retraining on the past year’s data. What do you check first?

    Show answer

    Whether flagged patients were treated earlier because of the alert. If so, the PPV fell partly because the tool worked, and naive retraining will teach the next model that high-risk features matter less. Ask whether treatment was recorded, model it in any retraining, and look at unflagged patients’ calibration, where no intervention distorted outcomes.

  5. An ICU model is well calibrated overall. Among non-Saudi patients, observed mortality is 40% above predicted, but there were only 30 deaths. How do you report it?

    Show answer

    Report the O/E ratio with its confidence interval, and say it is imprecise but concerning. Keep monitoring that group on a longer window or a group-specific CUSUM, look for reasons (different case mix, later presentation, missing data patterns), and do not dismiss it because it is “not significant”.
