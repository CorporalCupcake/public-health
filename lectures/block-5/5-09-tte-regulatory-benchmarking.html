Lecture 5.9

Target trial emulation for regulatory decisions; benchmarking against trials

Block 5 · Pharmacoepidemiology, real-world evidence, regulatory science and AI evaluation
The point

Lecture 4.5 taught you how to write and emulate a target trial protocol. This lecture asks what it takes for a regulator to believe one. Regulators now name target trial emulation in their guidance, but the name buys nothing on its own: they judge the data source, the pre-specified protocol and the evidence that the method gets known answers right. Benchmarking projects such as RCT-DUPLICATE show that well-designed emulations often reproduce trial results, and that most failures come from questions that could not be emulated closely, not from bad statistics. For Project 5A there is no trial to benchmark against, so negative and positive controls have to do that job.

1. How regulators view target trial emulation

Regulators accept real-world evidence case by case, and they judge it on three things: whether the data are fit for the question, whether the design was fixed before the results were seen, and whether the analysis can be reproduced.

What it is
A set of guidances, mostly from 2023 to 2026, that move real-world evidence from “supportive at best” to “acceptable when fit for purpose”. Several now name target trial emulation explicitly as the design framework for causal questions from routine data.
Why it matters
The same emulation can be published in a journal and still fail as regulatory evidence, because the bar is not “plausible” but “decision-grade”. Knowing what reviewers ask lets you build the study to answer it.
How to read it
Look for three recurring demands: early engagement with the agency, a protocol and analysis plan submitted before the analysis, and the agency’s ability to access or re-run the data.

Trap

“We used target trial emulation, so this is regulatory grade.” TTE is a way to state the question and avoid self-inflicted biases such as immortal time (Lecture 5.3). It does nothing about unmeasured confounding, misclassified outcomes or a data source that cannot see the outcome. Reviewers read the protocol table to find the differences from the ideal trial, not to admire the format.

In the Gulf

The SFDA’s RWD/RWE framework (version 1.0, effective 20 July 2026) explicitly lists target trial emulation as a design for causal questions and asks for a well-defined estimand, which links directly to ICH E9(R1) in Lecture 5.8. For a Saudi sponsor, that means a 5A-style protocol table and pre-specified analysis plan are expected, not optional extras. National data sources and common data models (Lecture 5.6) will decide which questions are feasible locally.

2. Benchmarking against trials: RCT-DUPLICATE

The most direct test of a method is to aim it at questions whose answers are known from randomised trials, before the trial results are seen, and count how often it agrees.

What it is
RCT-DUPLICATE (Wang, Schneeweiss and colleagues, JAMA 2023) emulated 32 cardiovascular, diabetes and other drug trials in US claims databases (Optum, MarketScan, Medicare), each with a protocol registered before the analysis. Two trials were still running when the emulation was done, so their results were genuinely unknown.
Why it matters
It turned a debate (“can observational data replace trials?”) into numbers, and showed that the answer depends mainly on how closely the trial design can be emulated.
How to read it
Agreement was measured three ways: significance agreement (same conclusion about the null), estimate agreement (the real-world point estimate inside the trial’s 95% CI) and standardised difference agreement (the gap between estimates is within chance). Across all 32: correlation 0.82, significance agreement 75%, estimate agreement 66%, standardised difference agreement 75%.
What a change means
In the 16 trials judged, after the fact, to be closely emulable, correlation rose to 0.93 and agreement to 94%, 88% and 88%. In the 16 less closely emulated it fell to 0.53, with 56%, 50% and 69%. Both ongoing trials (CAROLINA and PRONOUNCE) were closely emulated and agreed on all three measures.
How often does an emulation “agree” with its trial?

Trap

Reading 66% estimate agreement as “observational studies are wrong a third of the time”. A trial’s 95% CI misses a second, independent estimate of the same quantity quite often by chance alone, especially when the trial is small. Agreement metrics have ceilings well below 100%, which the widget shows when emulation differences are set to zero.

From your ML background

Benchmarking is held-out validation for a causal pipeline: the trial result plays the role of the label. As in ML, the test is only honest if the protocol (the “model”) is frozen before the labels are seen. That is why RCT-DUPLICATE registered every emulation in advance.

3. What makes emulations diverge

Most disagreements trace to features of the trial that routine data cannot copy, so the gap is often a different question, not a wrong answer.

What it is
RCT-DUPLICATE listed the emulation differences for each trial. The top three: a placebo comparator replaced by an active drug thought unrelated to the outcome (there is no “placebo” in claims data); trial features such as run-in periods, loading doses or titration that practice does not reproduce; and outcomes measured differently (adjudicated in the trial, coded in claims).
Why it matters
Each difference changes the estimand. A run-in period removes early non-adherers and early adverse events before randomisation; a claims cohort keeps them. Trials of stopping maintenance therapy at randomisation have no clean real-world equivalent.
How to read it
For every row of your protocol table (Lecture 4.5), write the ideal trial element, your emulation of it and the direction of bias the difference could cause. Reviewers look for that column.
What a change means
Time-varying effects are a quiet source of divergence: in some trials the benefit appears after two or three years, while patients in routine data stay on the drug for months. An emulation can be correct and still show no effect because follow-up never reaches the window where the effect lives.
Example · Why a cardiovascular outcome trial is hard to copy

Many diabetes outcome trials compared a new drug with placebo on top of usual care. A claims emulation must compare it with another active drug (for example a DPP-4 inhibitor, assumed neutral for heart outcomes). If the comparator has its own small effect, the emulation estimates a different contrast. RCT-DUPLICATE flagged this substitution in 10 of its 32 trials.

Trap

Tuning the emulation after seeing that it disagrees with the trial (changing the comparator, the grace period, the outcome definition) until it agrees. That turns benchmarking into curve-fitting, and the “validated” pipeline then fails on the next question where no trial exists.

4. When an emulation should and should not agree

Disagreement has three sources: a different question, bias, and chance. Only bias is a failure of the method.

What it is
A decomposition to keep in mind whenever you compare an emulation with a trial: the gap between the two estimates is the sum of the difference in what they estimate (population, comparator, follow-up, estimand), the bias in the observational estimate, and sampling error in both.
Why it matters
An emulation in older, frailer patients from routine care should sometimes differ from a trial in selected volunteers; that difference is often the reason to do it. Agreement on a mismatched question can even hide two errors cancelling.
How to read it
Benchmark first on a question you can emulate closely, in the same data, with the same pipeline. If it agrees there, a later divergence on a new question is more credibly a real difference in population or practice.
Trap

Treating the trial as truth for your population. The trial is the truth for its estimand in its population. When the emulation deliberately targets a different population, disagreement is a finding to explain, not an error to fix.

Analogy

Calibrating a kitchen scale with a known 1 kg weight. If it reads 1 kg, you trust it with flour. If you weigh a different 1 kg bag and it reads 1.1, the scale might be off, or the bag might not be 1 kg. Benchmarking needs the known weight to be really the same thing.

5. Negative controls, calibration and pre-registration

When there is no trial to compare with, use questions whose answer you already know: outcomes the drug cannot affect should show no effect, and the spread of those null results measures your study’s systematic error.

What it is
A negative control outcome shares the confounding structure of the real outcome but cannot plausibly be caused by the treatment (Lipsitch and colleagues, 2010). A positive control is an effect known to exist. Empirical calibration (Schuemie and colleagues, 2014) fits the distribution of estimates across many negative controls and widens the p-value and confidence interval of the main result to account for that systematic error.
Why it matters
In observational data, standard 95% CIs cover only random error. If negative controls show hazard ratios of 1.3 on average, your main estimate probably carries similar bias.
How to read it
Negative controls centred near 1 with little spread are reassuring. A shifted or wide null distribution is a warning that confounding or measurement problems remain. A positive control that fails to appear suggests the design cannot detect real effects.
library(EmpiricalCalibration)
nc <- read.csv("negative_controls.csv")   # one row per negative control: logRr, seLogRr
null <- fitNull(logRr = nc$logRr, seLogRr = nc$seLogRr)
null                                         # mean and SD of the systematic error
calibrateP(null, logRr = log(0.85), seLogRr = 0.08)   # calibrated p for the main estimate
plotCalibrationEffect(nc$logRr, nc$seLogRr, log(0.85), 0.08, null)

Run the same pipeline on each negative control outcome, save its log hazard ratio and standard error, and fitNull estimates how far and how widely those estimates scatter from the null. calibrateP then gives a p-value for the main result that allows for that scatter; the plot shows the main estimate against the cloud of negative controls.

Trap

Choosing a negative control that is detected differently between arms. In the AF example, a catheter-associated urinary infection looks like a neutral outcome, but anticoagulation causes haematuria, haematuria prompts urine tests, and more testing finds more infections. The control then shows an “effect” that is really detection bias.

Pre-registration
Register the protocol and analysis plan before touching outcome data: ClinicalTrials.gov (used by RCT-DUPLICATE), the HMA-EMA RWD catalogue (which replaced the EU PAS Register in 2024) or OSF. Use the HARPER protocol template (ISPE/ISPOR, 2022) to structure it.
Reporting
Report with TARGET (Transparent Reporting of Observational Studies Emulating a Target Trial, 2025): 21 items, including the target trial protocol and how each of its elements was emulated in the data.

6. Project 5A in regulatory-grade form

No randomised trial has tested anticoagulation for new-onset AF in critically ill patients, so 5A cannot be benchmarked directly; it earns credibility through a frozen protocol, honest emulation differences, controls and bias analysis.

What it is
The same 5A question from Lecture 4.5, packaged as a regulator would want to read it: protocol table with an extra column for emulation differences and their likely direction of bias, pre-registration, a positive control (anticoagulation should increase major bleeding), negative control outcomes, and quantitative bias analysis for unmeasured confounding (Lecture 2.13).
Why it matters
Where trials are unlikely (the patients are too sick, the window too short), well-built emulations are the best available evidence. That is exactly the setting where regulators and guideline panels need to know how far to trust them.
How to read it
Work down the protocol elements and mark which ones MIMIC-IV can emulate closely and which carry high risk. The high-risk rows set your sensitivity analyses.

Trap

Claiming that 5A “emulates a trial” as if a trial result existed to be reproduced. Say instead that it emulates a hypothetical target trial, list how the emulation differs from it, and be explicit that agreement with nothing is not validation.

From your ML background

Negative controls are the epidemiological version of a label-permutation sanity check: run the full pipeline where the right answer is “nothing”, and see how often it reports something. A pipeline that finds signal in shuffled labels, or in outcomes the drug cannot touch, is leaking.

Check yourself

  1. An emulation of a placebo-controlled trial uses a different active drug as comparator and finds a hazard ratio of 0.95, while the trial found 0.80. Before calling the emulation wrong, what would you check?

    Show answer

    Whether the comparator has its own effect on the outcome (which shrinks the contrast), whether follow-up in routine data reaches the period where the trial’s effect appeared, whether outcomes were defined the same way, and the standardised difference, given both confidence intervals. Only after those does residual confounding become the main suspect.

  2. A team reports that their emulation reproduced a trial after they changed the grace period and the outcome definition. How convincing is that as validation of their pipeline?

    Show answer

    Weak. Changes made after seeing the disagreement fit the pipeline to one answer. Validation requires a protocol frozen and registered before results, as in RCT-DUPLICATE. The post hoc version is a hypothesis to test on a new benchmark.

  3. In 5A, ten negative control outcomes give hazard ratios centred around 1.25. What does that tell you, and what do you do?

    Show answer

    Systematic error pushes estimates upwards, probably because treated patients differ in ways the model does not capture or are monitored more closely. Investigate the design (time zero, confounders, detection), then report results calibrated against the negative-control distribution alongside the uncalibrated ones.

  4. Why do both RCT-DUPLICATE trials that were still running at the time count for more than the 30 completed ones?

    Show answer

    Because nobody knew the answer when the emulation was designed, so it could not have been tuned to agree, even unconsciously. They are the closest thing to a true prospective test.

  5. A regulator asks why your 5A positive control matters if bleeding is not the question of interest.

    Show answer

    It shows the design can detect an effect that is known to exist, with the same time zero, exposure definition and outcome capture. If anticoagulation did not appear to raise bleeding, something in the pipeline (exposure misclassification, misaligned time zero, poor outcome capture) would be hiding effects, including the one you care about.
