Lecture 1.2

Rose’s prevention paradox: population vs high-risk strategies

Master Block 1 · Foundations, burden of disease and health economics
The point

Most cases of a common disease come from the many people at modest risk, not the few at high risk. So you can prevent a disease in two ways: find and treat the high-risk tail (high-risk strategy), or move the whole population a little (population strategy). This lecture is the argument behind Project 1: a salt limit in bread is the textbook population strategy, and the model you build there puts a number on Rose’s idea.

1. The prevention paradox

A measure that prevents many cases across a population usually gives each person who takes part almost no benefit.

What it is
Rose’s observation that population benefit and individual benefit pull apart. When a small change is applied to millions of people, each one’s risk falls by a tiny amount. Multiplied across millions, those tiny amounts add up to many cases prevented.
Why it matters
It explains why the most effective prevention is often unpopular and hard to sell. People feel no gain, doctors see no patient helped, and politicians see a cost now for a benefit nobody can point to. You have to make the benefit visible with a model, which is Project 1’s job.
How to read it
Hold two numbers side by side: the absolute risk reduction for one person (how many percentage points their risk falls) and the cases prevented in the population. A paradox is in play when the first is tiny and the second is large.
What a change means
Covering more people raises the total in proportion while each person’s gain stays the same: doubling the population covered roughly doubles the cases prevented, and nobody feels any different. A larger shift (say, cutting more salt) raises both, but the individual gain usually stays too small to notice.
Trap

Reading “each person gains almost nothing” as “the measure is weak”. Judge a population measure by the total it prevents and what it costs, not by what one person gains. The reverse also holds: a treatment that helps each patient a lot can still prevent few cases overall if few people get it.

Example · seatbelts

A classic illustration of the paradox. A driver can wear a seatbelt every day for a lifetime and never be in a crash where it matters. Each driver’s benefit is close to zero. Across a whole country of drivers, seatbelt laws save many lives every year.

Analogy

A one-riyal rounding-up donation at a supermarket till. Nobody notices it in their own budget. Across a million shoppers, it funds a hospital wing.

2. Where the cases come from

A large number of people at small risk produces more cases than a small number of people at high risk.

Take a town of 100,000 adults, split by blood pressure (illustrative numbers):

What it is
The number of cases from a group is its size times its risk. A high-risk group has a big risk but is small. The middle of the distribution has a modest risk but is huge, so it usually wins.
Why it matters
A strategy that only treats the high-risk group can, at best, prevent the cases from that group. In the table that is under a third of strokes, even if treatment worked perfectly.
How to read it
Look at the share of cases, not the risk, in each group. If most cases come from people who look “normal”, the disease is a population problem and a high-risk strategy alone cannot solve it.
What a change means
The more the risk is spread across the whole range (a continuous risk factor like blood pressure, salt or cholesterol), the bigger the share from the middle. When risk is concentrated in a rare, sharply defined group (a single-gene disorder), the high-risk group holds most cases and targeting works well.
Trap

Thinking that a strong relative risk in the top group means most cases are there. Relative risk tells you how much riskier one person is. The share of cases also depends on how many people are in each group. A rare exposure with a big relative risk can cause fewer cases than a common one with a small relative risk.

Example · Down syndrome

A classic example used by Rose. The risk of a baby with Down syndrome rises steeply with the mother’s age. Yet in his day most affected babies were born to younger mothers, because younger mothers had far more babies. Screening only older mothers missed most cases.

From your ML background

A risk factor with a strong association is usually a weak classifier. Blood pressure predicts stroke clearly in a cohort, yet the score distributions of future cases and non-cases overlap heavily, so any cut-off misses most cases or flags most of the population. Wald and colleagues made this point formally: an association strong enough to matter for causation is rarely strong enough to work as a screening test. That overlap is why a high-risk strategy leaks cases.

3. Sick individuals and sick populations

“Why did this person get the disease?” and “why does this population have so much of it?” are different questions, and they can have different answers.

What it is
Rose separated causes of cases (why one person in a population is affected and another isn’t) from causes of incidence (why the rate is high in one population and low in another). The first is about position within a distribution. The second is about where the whole distribution sits.
Why it matters
A study within one population can only find factors that vary within it. If an exposure is high for nearly everyone, it barely varies, so it looks harmless, even if it is the main reason the disease is common. Comparing populations, or changing the exposure for everyone, is how you see it.
How to tell
Ask whether the comparison is between people in one setting or between settings. Within a setting you learn who is susceptible. Between settings you learn what makes the disease common.
Trap

Concluding that a factor “doesn’t matter” because a cohort study within one country found little effect. Rose’s thought experiment: if every person smoked 20 cigarettes a day, lung cancer would look like a genetic disease, because genes would be the only thing that differed between those who got it and those who didn’t. A within-population null can hide a population-wide cause.

Example · blood pressure

Rose compared the blood pressure distributions of Kenyan nomads and London civil servants. In London, “hypertension” was common and its causes looked individual. Among the nomads it was almost absent, because the whole distribution sat lower. The real question for London was not “which people are hypertensive?” but “why is the whole curve so high?” Salt intake is one of the leading answers.

Analogy

Exam results in two schools. Within one school, the top and bottom students differ in effort and ability. Between the schools, the gap may come from the teaching. Ranking students inside one school will never reveal that.

4. The whole distribution moves

Populations shift as a whole: when the average goes down, the number of people in the high-risk tail goes down with it.

What it is
Rose and Day used data from Intersalt, a study of 52 populations in 32 countries. Populations with a higher mean blood pressure, body weight, alcohol or salt intake had more people at the extreme, and the link was very close. The tail is not a separate group; it is the end of one curve.
Why it matters
It means you can shrink the high-risk group without identifying anyone. It also means the “normal” majority and the “sick” minority share a cause: whatever sets the population mean.
How to read it
Because the tail is thin, a small shift in the mean changes the count above a cut-off by a much larger percentage than the shift itself. A drop in average blood pressure of a few mmHg sounds trivial; the fall in the number of people above the treatment threshold is not.
What a change means
A population mean moving the wrong way, even slightly, signals a disproportionate rise in the number of people who will need treatment. A mean moving the right way does the opposite, at no screening cost.
Trap

Treating “normal” and “abnormal” as natural categories. For blood pressure, salt or cholesterol, risk rises smoothly across the range; the cut-off is a decision about whom to treat, not a line drawn by biology. People just under the cut-off are at nearly the same risk as those just over it, and there are many more of them.

Example · Saudi Arabia

The 2019 bread salt limit does not target anyone with hypertension. It lowers the salt in a staple that almost everyone eats, which pulls the population’s salt intake curve left, and with it the blood pressure curve. Project 1 models exactly this: a shift in mean intake, run through blood pressure to cardiovascular events.

From your ML background

It is the difference between changing the data-generating process and moving a decision threshold. The high-risk strategy works at the threshold on a fixed distribution. The population strategy changes the distribution, so every downstream count (prevalence above any cut-off) changes at once.

5. Two strategies compared

The high-risk strategy gives big benefits to a few people; the population strategy gives small benefits to everyone, and attacks the reason the disease is common.

High-risk strategyPopulation strategy
Acts onThe tail: people found by screeningThe whole distribution
Main strengthsFits the person; patient and doctor are motivated; good benefit-to-harm ratio for each person treatedLarge potential for the population; tackles the cause of high incidence; doesn’t ask anyone to behave differently from those around them
Main weaknessesMisses most cases; screening costs and must continue for ever; leaves the cause in place, so new high-risk people keep appearingSmall benefit to each person (the paradox); little motivation for public or doctors; any harm, however small, is applied to everyone
Salt exampleAdvise patients with hypertension to cut salt; prescribe antihypertensivesSalt limits in bread and processed food
Radical vs palliative
Rose called the population strategy radical: it goes at the root, the condition that makes the disease common. The high-risk strategy is palliative in his sense: it protects susceptible people from a cause that stays in place. Treating today’s hypertensive patients does nothing to stop next year’s.
Why it matters
The two are complements, not rivals. In practice you combine them: shift the curve to shrink the tail, then treat whoever is still in it. The policy question is the balance of effort and money between the two.
The safety rule
Because a population measure reaches everyone, even a rare harm adds up. Rose’s test: removing an unnatural excess (too much salt, tobacco smoke) can be presumed safe; adding something new to everyone (a drug for the whole population) must be proven safe first.
Trap

Assuming the population strategy is always better because it prevents more cases in theory. It depends on the size of the shift you can actually achieve, the cost, and whether the low end of the range is safe. For salt, a debate over whether very low intakes carry risk is exactly such a question; Lecture 1.9 covers it.

Example · cardiovascular prevention

Modern practice uses both. Risk calculators pick out people at high absolute risk for statins and blood pressure drugs (high-risk). Tobacco taxes, smoke-free laws and salt reduction move the whole population (population). Neither alone matches what the two achieve together.

Analogy

A leaky roof. You can keep putting buckets under the worst drips (high-risk), or fix the roof (population). Buckets help tonight. Only the roof stops new drips.

6. Making the population strategy work

Population measures that change the environment work better, and more fairly, than ones that rely on each person choosing to change.

What it is
There are two ways to shift a population. Agency-based measures ask people to act: campaigns, labels, advice. Structural measures change what people are exposed to by default: reformulating food, taxes, bans. Both are population strategies, but they behave differently.
Why it matters
Agency-based measures need time, money, literacy and attention, which better-off people have more of. They can lower the average while widening the gap between rich and poor, sometimes called the inequality paradox. Structural measures need nothing from the individual, so they tend to reach everyone equally or help the worse-off most.
How to tell
Ask: “Does this work if nobody notices it?” If yes (salt quietly lowered in bread), it is structural. If people must read, decide and act, it is agency-based.
What a change means
Moving a policy from voluntary to mandatory, or from labelling to reformulation, usually moves it towards the structural end: larger and more even effects, more industry resistance.
Trap

Assuming any population-wide measure is fair because it applies to everyone. Equal exposure to a campaign is not equal benefit. Always ask who is able to respond.

Example · UK salt reduction

From 2003 the UK set voluntary salt targets for processed food and ran public campaigns. Average salt intake, measured by 24-hour urine, fell by about 15% between the early 2000s and 2011. The programme’s evaluators attribute most of the fall to reformulation, which works whether or not shoppers pay attention.

Example · Saudi Arabia

Saudi salt policy shows both ends. The 2019 bread limit is structural: it changes a staple at source. The 2025 limits for other foods are voluntary, so their effect depends on how many manufacturers comply. That is why Project 1 runs every scenario at measured and at full compliance: the gap between them is the cost of relying on voluntary action.

Check yourself

  1. A health official says: “Our bread salt limit lowers each person’s stroke risk by a fraction of a percentage point. That’s negligible. Let’s spend the money on screening instead.” What is wrong with this argument?

    Show answer

    It judges a population measure by individual benefit, which is the prevention paradox in action. The right comparison is total strokes prevented and cost, for each option. A tiny risk reduction across millions can prevent more strokes than screening, which reaches fewer people and misses the many cases that arise below any cut-off. The two are also not exclusive: shifting the curve shrinks the group that screening then has to find.

  2. A cohort study within one Saudi city finds only a weak link between salt intake and blood pressure. Does this show that salt is not an important cause of high blood pressure in Saudi Arabia?

    Show answer

    No. If almost everyone eats a lot of salt, intake varies little within the city, so a within-population study has little contrast to work with. That answers “why this person and not that one”, not “why is the whole population’s blood pressure high”. Salt intake is also hard to measure well (Lecture 1.8), which weakens associations further. Evidence across populations and from trials that lower salt for everyone is what answers the population question.

  3. For which kind of disease would you expect the high-risk strategy to do most of the work, and why?

    Show answer

    One where risk is concentrated in a small, identifiable group that holds most of the cases, such as a single-gene disorder or a disease confined to a defined exposure (an occupational chemical). Then targeting reaches most cases and the cause of the disease is not spread through the whole population. For continuous risk factors like blood pressure, most cases come from the middle and targeting alone leaks.

  4. Someone proposes adding a low-dose blood pressure drug to the water supply. Using Rose’s ideas, why should this face a much higher bar than lowering salt in bread?

    Show answer

    Both are population strategies, so any harm reaches everyone, and most people gain little. Lowering salt removes an excess exposure, moving people towards a more natural level, so it can be presumed broadly safe. A drug adds something new to everyone, including people who gain nothing and may be harmed, so it needs direct proof of safety. Consent is also an issue: people can’t opt out of tap water as easily as they can choose a different food.

  5. A salt awareness campaign lowers average intake slightly, but a follow-up shows the fall happened almost entirely in high-income, highly educated groups. What happened, and what would you recommend?

    Show answer

    An agency-based measure worked best for those with the resources to respond, so the average improved while the gap widened: the inequality paradox. Recommend structural measures that need no action from the individual, such as mandatory reformulation of the foods that supply most salt. These shift everyone and tend to narrow, not widen, the gap.

  6. A report says the share of adults with hypertension rose “only slightly” while average systolic blood pressure rose by 2 mmHg. Why might the 2 mmHg deserve more attention than it gets?

    Show answer

    The mean is the lead indicator. Because the whole curve moves together, a small rise in the mean pushes many people towards and over the cut-off, and raises risk for everyone below it too, since risk rises smoothly across the range. A shift of a few mmHg across millions of people translates into a substantial number of extra strokes and heart attacks, most of them in people never labelled hypertensive.
