Lecture 1.8

Nutrition epidemiology: dietary assessment and measurement error

Block 1 · Foundations, burden of disease and health economics
The point

Diet is one of the hardest exposures to measure, and the errors are not harmless noise. This lecture shows what each method measures, why 24-hour urine is the reference for sodium, and how measurement error distorts both intake estimates and diet–disease associations. It is the reasoning behind Project 2’s rule to include only 24-hour urine studies, and behind how Project 1 treats intake distributions.

1. The target: usual intake

What matters for chronic disease is a person’s usual intake (their long-run average), but every method observes only a few days.

What it is
Each person’s intake swings from day to day around their own average. Usual intake is that average. One day’s measurement is the usual intake plus a large day-specific deviation.
Why it matters
Blood pressure and heart disease respond to years of exposure, not to Tuesday’s lunch. Any method that captures a single day mixes the signal (differences between people) with noise (differences between days).
How to read it
For sodium, day-to-day variation within a person is large compared with differences between people. A single day ranks people poorly, though the average of many people’s single days still estimates the population mean well.
What a change means
Adding repeat days per person shrinks the day-to-day part and sharpens the picture of each person’s usual intake. Adding more people only sharpens the population mean.
Trap

Reading the spread of single-day values as the spread of usual intake. Day-to-day noise widens the distribution, so the tails look fatter than they are. If most people eat well over 5 g, the share who appear to eat under 5 g is exaggerated. Correcting this needs repeat measures on at least some people.

Example · Gulf

Diet in the Gulf changes sharply during Ramadan, at weekends, and at gatherings with large shared meals. A survey run in one season, or only on weekdays, measures that period, not the year. Check when a study collected its data before you treat it as usual intake.

2. Self-report methods

The three self-report tools trade detail against burden, and all three measure sodium badly.

MethodWhat it capturesStrengthWeakness
Food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)How often a fixed list of foods was eaten over monthsCheap; aims at usual intake; ranks peoplePoor absolute amounts; list may miss local foods
24-hour recallEverything eaten yesterday, by interviewDetailed; little burden; good population meansOne day only; relies on memory
Food record (diary)Everything eaten, written down as it happens, over several daysNo reliance on memoryHeavy burden; people eat differently when recording
Why it matters
Most diet–disease cohorts use FFQs. Knowing what an FFQ can do (rank people) and can’t do (give accurate grams) tells you which conclusions from those cohorts to trust.
Why sodium is hard
Salt added in cooking and at the table is rarely reported, and the salt in a dish varies with the recipe and the bakery. Food composition tables seldom match local recipes. Self-report usually underestimates sodium.
How to judge one
Ask whether it was validated against a recovery biomarker (next section) in a similar population. Validation against another self-report method shares the same errors and proves little.
Trap

Assuming errors in self-report are random. They are not: people with obesity, and people who know they “should” eat less, tend to under-report more. That error is linked to the person, so it can bias associations in either direction.

Example

The US OPEN study compared FFQs and 24-hour recalls with recovery biomarkers: doubly labelled water for energy and urinary nitrogen for protein. Both self-report methods under-reported energy, the FFQ more so, and the FFQ’s errors were large enough to substantially weaken diet–disease associations.

3. Biomarkers and 24-hour urine

For sodium, collecting all urine for 24 hours is the reference method, because the body excretes most of what it eats through urine.

What it is
A recovery biomarker is one where a known, stable share of intake comes out of the body, so output tracks intake directly. In normal conditions about 90% of ingested sodium appears in 24-hour urine. Few nutrients have such a marker: energy (doubly labelled water), protein (urinary nitrogen), potassium and sodium (24-hour urine).
Why it matters
It is the only sodium measure free of the reporting biases above. It is why WHO and PAHO publish a protocol for population sodium surveys based on it, and why Project 2 includes only 24-hour urine studies.
How to read it
Values are reported as mmol or mg of sodium per day. To get salt, multiply grams of sodium by about 2.5. A single collection is a good estimate of a group’s mean but a noisy estimate of one person’s usual intake.
Two weaknesses
Day-to-day variation: one collection is one day, so several are needed per person to rank people. Incomplete collection: a missed void makes intake look lower. Studies check completeness with collection time, total volume, creatinine excretion relative to body size, or a tracer tablet (PABA, para-aminobenzoic acid) whose recovery confirms that all urine was collected.
Trap

Calling 24-hour urine “perfect”. It is the best available method, not error-free. A study that reports no completeness criteria, or excludes a large share of collections without saying why, may be biased low. In Project 2’s risk-of-bias assessment, completeness checks and response rate are the items to read first.

Example · Gulf

Urine misses sodium lost in sweat. In normal conditions that share is small, but heat and heavy outdoor work raise it. For outdoor workers in a Gulf summer, 24-hour urine may understate intake more than usual. This is worth noting as a limitation for any Gulf study measured in the hot season.

Analogy

Self-report is asking someone what they spent this month. 24-hour urine is reading their bank statement for one day. The statement is honest, but one day may be untypical, and a missing page makes the total look small.

4. Spot urine and estimating equations

A single urine sample plus a prediction equation is cheap, but it pulls estimates towards the middle and can create associations that aren’t real.

What it is
A spot urine is one sample taken at one moment. Equations (the best known are Kawasaki, Tanaka and INTERSALT) predict 24-hour sodium from the sample’s sodium and creatinine plus age, sex and body size.
Why it matters
Spot urine is far easier to collect, so large cohorts and many national surveys use it. Some of the most cited and most disputed salt findings rest on it (Lecture 1.9).
How to read the bias
Compared with measured 24-hour urine, spot equations overestimate intake in low-intake groups and underestimate it in high-intake groups. The population mean can come out close, but the distribution is squashed towards the middle and individual values are unreliable.
What it does to associations
The equations use age, body size and creatinine, which are themselves linked to mortality. So the “estimated sodium” carries information that has nothing to do with sodium. In the TOHP cohort, estimated sodium showed a J-shaped link with mortality while measured 24-hour sodium did not.
Trap

Assuming that “validated against 24-hour urine” means interchangeable. An equation can match 24-hour urine on the population mean and still fail badly at ranking individuals or at the tails, which is what you need for distributions (Project 1) and for risk curves (Project 3).

From your ML background

A spot equation is a regression prediction, and predictions regress to the mean: the predicted values have less spread than the true ones. Using a model’s prediction as a feature in a second model also carries over the first model’s inputs. Here those inputs (age, body size, creatinine) are confounders, so the second model learns their effect and labels it “sodium”.

5. Random vs systematic error

Random error adds noise around the truth; systematic error shifts the answer in one direction; they damage different things.

Random errorSystematic error
What it isScatter that averages out over many measurementsA consistent push in one direction
Sodium exampleDay-to-day variation in single 24-hour urinesMissed voids; under-reported table salt
Effect on a meanNone (just less precise)Biased; more data doesn’t help
Effect on a distributionToo wideShifted
Effect on an associationWeakened towards no effect (next section)Unpredictable; can go either way
FixRepeat measures; statistical correctionA better method
Why it matters
You need to know which kind dominates before you can say which way a result is wrong. A survey with random error gives an honest mean and an exaggerated spread. A survey with systematic error gives a wrong mean, however large it is.
How to tell
Ask whether the error would cancel if you repeated the measurement many times on the same person. If yes, it is random. If it would persist (the person always forgets the salt they add), it is systematic.
Trap

Thinking a large sample fixes measurement error. It fixes random error in a mean. It does nothing for systematic error, and nothing for the weakening of associations caused by random error in the exposure.

Analogy

A shaky hand on a dartboard scatters darts around the bullseye (random). A bent sight sends every dart to the same wrong spot (systematic). Throwing more darts helps the first, not the second.

6. Regression dilution

Random error in the exposure makes a real association look weaker than it is.

What it is
When a single noisy measurement stands in for usual exposure, some people look high or low just by chance. Their outcomes don’t follow, so the slope of outcome on exposure flattens towards zero. This is regression dilution (also called attenuation).
Why it matters
Risk curves from single baseline measurements understate the true effect of usual exposure. MacMahon and colleagues showed that correcting blood pressure–disease associations for this made them substantially steeper. Single-collection sodium cohorts face the same problem.
How to read it
The flattening depends on the share of total variation that is true between-person difference. If day-to-day noise is as large as the real differences between people, the observed slope is roughly halved. Studies estimate this share from repeat measurements on a subset and use it to correct the slope.
What a change means
More collections per person reduce the noise and bring the observed slope closer to the true one. A study with several 24-hour urines per person should show a steeper sodium–disease slope than one with a single collection, if the effect is real.
Trap

Treating dilution as a safe, conservative bias. It only flattens the slope when the error is purely random and in the exposure alone. Error linked to the outcome, or to confounders (as with spot equations), can bend the curve or invent a shape, such as a J-curve.

From your ML background

This is errors-in-variables. Noise in a feature shrinks its fitted coefficient; noise in the label does not (it only widens the uncertainty). The correction factor is the feature’s reliability: true-signal variance divided by total variance.

Example · Projects 1, 2 and 3

Project 2 includes only 24-hour urine studies so that systematic error is minimised and the pooled mean is trustworthy. Project 1 then uses that mean, but should treat the single-collection spread as wider than the usual-intake spread. Project 3 has about one urine sample per person in NHANES, so its sodium and potassium risk estimates will be diluted; say so, and correct if repeat data allow. Check whether the samples are spot or 24-hour: if spot, section 4 applies too.

Check yourself

  1. A national survey collects one 24-hour urine per person from 1,500 adults and reports that 85% exceed 5 g of salt a day. Which number do you trust more: the mean or the 85%?

    Show answer

    The mean. Day-to-day variation averages out across 1,500 people, so the mean is sound if collections were complete. But that same variation widens the distribution, so the share above any cut-off is distorted. If the mean is above 5 g, the true share above 5 g is probably higher than reported, not lower; if the mean is below, the reverse. Correcting it needs repeat collections on a subsample.

  2. A study estimates salt intake from an FFQ and reports it lower than 24-hour urine studies in the same country. Which is more likely to be right, and why?

    Show answer

    The 24-hour urine studies. FFQs miss salt added in cooking and at the table, use composition tables that may not match local recipes, and are designed to rank people rather than measure amounts. Self-report usually underestimates sodium.

  3. Why does Project 2 exclude spot-urine studies even though some match 24-hour urine on the population mean?

    Show answer

    Because agreement on average depends on the population and the equation, and the bias runs in opposite directions at low and high intakes. Mixing spot and 24-hour studies would add unknown bias to the pooled estimate and to differences between countries and sexes. Keeping to 24-hour urine gives one consistent, defensible measure.

  4. A 24-hour urine study excludes 30% of collections as incomplete, and the excluded group was mostly younger men. What could this do to the reported mean?

    Show answer

    It could bias it. If younger men eat more salt than average, losing them pushes the mean down. Exclusion for completeness improves measurement but can create selection bias. Check whether results were reported by sex and age, or weighted back to the population.

  5. Cohort A, with one 24-hour urine per person, finds a weak sodium–stroke association. Cohort B, with five per person, finds a stronger one. Does B contradict A?

    Show answer

    No. This is what regression dilution predicts. A’s single collection is noisier, so its slope is flattened towards zero. B measures usual intake better and recovers more of the true slope. Both are consistent with a real effect.

  6. A cohort using spot urine with the Kawasaki equation finds that low estimated sodium is linked to higher mortality. Give two reasons, other than a real harm of low salt, why this could happen.

    Show answer

    First, the equation uses creatinine, age and body size, which are linked to mortality themselves, so “estimated sodium” partly measures frailty. Second, people who are already ill often eat less, so low intake can be a result of disease rather than a cause (reverse causation). Section 4 and Lecture 1.9 cover both.
