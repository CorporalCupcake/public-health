Lecture 1.1

What public health is

Block 1 · Foundations, burden of disease and health economics
The point

Public health treats a population as its patient. This lecture gives you three lenses for placing any intervention: what job it does (core functions), when it acts on the course of a disease (levels of prevention), and which area of practice it belongs to (the three domains). You will use all three to frame every project, starting with Saudi salt policy.

1. The population is the patient

Medicine asks “why did this person get sick?” Public health asks “why does this population have so much of this disease, and what can society do about it?”

What it is
“The science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society.” Two phrases carry the meaning. Preventing: the work is mostly done before anyone is ill. Organised efforts of society: governments, laws, industry and communities act, not a single doctor.
Why it matters
The biggest levers on a population’s health sit outside the clinic: clean water, tobacco taxes, what goes into food. Most of your projects evaluate levers like these.
How to tell
Ask two questions. Who is the unit? Who acts? If the unit is a group and the actor is organised society, it is public health. If the unit is one patient and the actor is a clinician, it is clinical care.
How success shows
Success is measured as a rate across the population (deaths per 100,000, average salt intake), not as individual recoveries. The people it helps never find out, so success is invisible unless you measure it.
Trap

Equating public health with the government running hospitals. Hospitals are health care delivery. Public health is mostly prevention. Planning health services is one part of it (the third domain, below), not the whole.

Example · Saudi Arabia

The 2019 limit on salt in bread involves no patient and no doctor. One regulation changes the diet of millions. The person whose stroke never happens will never know the policy helped them. Project 1 makes that invisible benefit visible by modelling it.

Analogy

A lifeguard keeps pulling drowning people out of a river. Public health walks upstream to find out why they keep falling in.

2. Three core functions

Every public health activity does one of three jobs: find out what is wrong, decide what to do, and make sure it gets done.

FunctionPlain meaningSalt example
AssessmentMeasure the population’s health and what drives itA survey measuring salt intake from 24-hour urine
Policy developmentUse the evidence to set rules, plans and prioritiesSetting a maximum salt content for bread
AssuranceMake sure the policy is carried out and the services existInspecting bakeries and testing bread for compliance
Why it matters
It tells you where a failure sits. A policy that didn’t work may have failed at assessment (wrong problem), at policy (wrong lever) or at assurance (nobody enforced it). Each failure needs a different fix.
How to tell
Look at what the activity produces. Data means assessment. A decision or rule means policy. Delivery or enforcement means assurance.
How they connect
The three form a loop. Checking compliance (assurance) produces new data, which feeds the next assessment. The US expands the same three jobs into a finer list, the “10 Essential Public Health Services”.
Trap

Treating a published policy as the finish line. Carrying it out is the assurance job, and that is often where things break. This is why Project 1 models salt limits at both measured and full compliance, and why Project 9 asks why the gap exists.

From your ML background

It is the lifecycle of a model in production. Assessment is collecting data and framing the problem. Policy is the model and the decision rule. Assurance is deployment and monitoring. A model that never ships, or drifts without anyone noticing, fails at the last step, not the first.

3. Levels of prevention

Levels of prevention place an intervention on the timeline of a disease: before the risk exists, before the disease starts, before symptoms appear, or after the disease is established.

LevelActs whenAimExample
PrimordialBefore risk factors appearStop the risk factor arising at allLess salt in the food supply, so fewer people develop high blood pressure
PrimaryRisk present, no diseaseStop the disease startingVaccination; treating high blood pressure to prevent stroke
SecondaryDisease present, no symptoms yetCatch it early, while the outcome can still be changedScreening for diabetes or breast cancer
TertiaryDisease establishedLimit damage, disability and recurrenceCardiac rehabilitation after a heart attack
QuaternaryAny timeProtect people from too much medicineNot screening healthy people with tests that mostly find harmless abnormalities
Why it matters
The level decides who you reach and what evidence you need. Earlier levels reach many people at a low cost per person, but each person gains little. Later levels reach fewer people, and each gains a lot.
How to tell
Name the disease first. Then ask: when the intervention happens, does the person already have the disease? Do they know they have it?
What a shift means
Moving effort earlier widens reach and shrinks the benefit to each person. Moving it later concentrates the benefit in people who are already ill. This trade-off is the heart of Rose’s prevention paradox (Lecture 1.2).
Trap

Forgetting that the level depends on which disease you name. Treating high blood pressure is secondary prevention of hypertension, because it is already there. It is also primary prevention of stroke, which hasn’t happened yet. Always say “prevention of what”.

Example · Saudi Arabia

Salt limits in bread work at two levels at once. They are primordial for high blood pressure (fewer people develop it) and primary for stroke and heart attack (people with raised blood pressure are less likely to go on to have a cardiovascular event).

Analogy

A town and a flood. Primordial: don’t build on the floodplain. Primary: build levees. Secondary: early-warning sirens. Tertiary: repair and rebuild. Quaternary: don’t evacuate the town every time it drizzles.

4. Three domains

UK practice divides public health into three domains: protecting people from threats, improving how they live, and making health services work for the population.

DomainCoversExampleBlock
Health protectionInfectious disease, environmental hazards, emergenciesMeningococcal vaccination required of Hajj pilgrims; heat rules for outdoor workers6
Health improvementBehaviour, lifestyle and the conditions that shape them; chronic disease preventionSalt reduction; sugary-drink taxes; tobacco control7
Healthcare public healthPlanning, quality, fairness and value of health servicesDeciding where cardiac services go; health needs assessment8
Why it matters
Jobs, training and services are organised this way in the UK and in many other systems. Each domain has its own toolkit: outbreak and surveillance methods, behaviour-change theory, and health economics with service planning.
How to tell
Ask what the problem is. Something external acting on people (a pathogen, heat, a chemical) is protection. How people live, and what shapes that, is improvement. How the health system serves people is healthcare public health.
Trap

Treating the domains as separate jobs with clean borders. They are labels for organising work, and real problems cross them. Salt policy is health improvement, but you evaluate it with healthcare public health tools such as cost-effectiveness. Use the label to find the right methods, not to argue about which box something goes in.

Example · Hajj

Requiring meningococcal vaccination for pilgrims is health protection: a mass gathering raises the risk that an infection spreads, and the vaccine requirement guards against it. Planning clinics and emergency capacity along the pilgrimage route is healthcare public health. Promoting heat-safe behaviour among pilgrims is health improvement. One event can involve all three domains.

5. Putting the lenses together

The three lenses answer different questions, so any intervention gets one tag from each.

InterventionFunctionLevelDomain
Salt limit in breadPolicy developmentPrimordial (blood pressure); primary (stroke)Improvement
24-hour urine survey of salt intakeAssessmentNone (it measures, it doesn’t prevent)Improvement
Vaccine requirement for Hajj pilgrimsPolicy, enforced through assurancePrimaryProtection
Diabetes screening in primary careAssurance (a service is delivered)SecondaryHealthcare public health
Trap

Mixing up the questions. “Is it primary or secondary?” asks about timing. “Is it assessment or assurance?” asks about the job. “Protection or improvement?” asks about the area. A single intervention has an answer to each, and the answers don’t need to match.

Check yourself

  1. Suppose a law requires restaurants to display calories on menus, but a survey finds half of them don’t. Which core function failed, and what would you look at first?

    Show answer

    Assurance. The problem was identified (assessment) and a rule exists (policy). The breakdown is in carrying it out. Look first at enforcement and feasibility: are there inspections, are there penalties, do small restaurants know the rule, and can they afford to comply? The survey itself is assessment, which closes the loop.

  2. A 55-year-old with high cholesterol and no heart disease starts a statin. What level of prevention is this? Does the answer change depending on the disease you name?

    Show answer

    For heart attack and stroke it is primary prevention: the risk factor is present, but the disease isn’t. If you name “high cholesterol” as the condition, the statin is treatment of an existing condition. The level always depends on “prevention of what”.

  3. Compare a sugary-drink tax with a diabetes screening programme. Which acts earlier, and what does each trade off?

    Show answer

    The tax acts earlier: it is primordial or primary. It reaches everyone at low cost per person, but each person gains little, and the effect is hard to see without population data. Screening is secondary. It reaches only people who are tested and already have the disease, but each person found early can gain a lot. It costs more per person and brings false positives. Neither is better in general; they answer different parts of the problem.

  4. A private clinic offers healthy executives a yearly full-body scan “to catch everything early”. Which level of prevention should make you sceptical, and why?

    Show answer

    Quaternary. In healthy people, scans mostly find harmless abnormalities. These lead to more tests, procedures, anxiety and sometimes harm, with no proven benefit. “Catch it early” sounds like secondary prevention, but early detection only helps when catching it early changes the outcome.

  5. A colleague says: “Our salt policy is working. No patients have complained.” What is wrong with this reasoning, and what evidence would you want instead?

    Show answer

    Public health success is invisible to individuals, so the absence of complaints tells you nothing. You need population measures over time: salt intake (ideally from 24-hour urine), blood pressure levels, and eventually rates of stroke and heart disease. You also need evidence of compliance, because a policy can only work if it is carried out.

  6. Heat illness among outdoor workers rises in a hot summer. Which domain owns this, and which other domain has a stake?

    Show answer

    Mainly health protection: heat is an external environmental hazard. Healthcare public health has a stake in whether clinics and emergency services can cope in peak months. Health improvement has one in workers’ behaviour, such as drinking water and taking rest breaks. Project 6 sits mostly in protection.
