Lecture 1.10

Economic evaluation: costs, perspectives, discounting, ICERs and thresholds

Block 1 · Foundations, burden of disease and health economics
The point

An economic evaluation asks whether an intervention buys enough health for what it costs, compared with the next best option. You need five decisions to answer that: what to measure, whose costs count, how to value the future, how to compare options (the ICER) and what counts as worth it (the threshold). Project 1 makes every one of these decisions for Saudi salt policy, and a reviewer will check each against CHEERS 2022.

1. Four types of evaluation

All economic evaluations compare costs with consequences; they differ in how they measure the consequences.

TypeHealth measured asAnswersUse when
Cost-effectiveness (CEA)A natural unit: strokes averted, mmHg lowered, lives savedCost per stroke avertedOptions share one outcome
Cost-utility (CUA)QALYs gained or DALYs averted (Lecture 1.6)Cost per QALY or DALYComparing across diseases; the default for policy
Cost-benefit (CBA)Money, e.g. willingness to payDo benefits exceed costs?Comparing health with non-health spending (roads, schools)
Cost-minimisationAssumed equalWhich is cheapest?Only when outcomes are shown to be the same
Why it matters
The type decides what you can compare. Cost per stroke averted can only be compared with other stroke interventions. Cost per DALY averted can be compared with a vaccine, a cancer drug or a road safety law, which is what a ministry needs.
How to tell
Look at the denominator. A disease-specific unit means CEA. QALYs or DALYs mean CUA. Money on both sides means CBA. Many papers call a CUA a “CEA”; the label is loose, the denominator is not.
The best outcome
Cost-saving: the intervention improves health and costs less overall, because treatment costs avoided exceed what it costs to run. No threshold is needed; you adopt it. Population salt reduction is often found to be cost-saving in models.
Trap

Treating “cost-effective” as meaning “saves money”. Cost-effective means the health gained is worth the extra money spent, judged against a threshold. Most cost-effective interventions add cost. Only a cost-saving one pays for itself.

Example · Project 1

Project 1 is a cost-utility analysis. It reports DALYs averted (to match WHO-CHOICE and GBD) and net costs, so a Saudi decision-maker can set salt policy beside any other WHO “best buy”, such as tobacco taxes. Reporting only “strokes averted” would hide the heart attacks and kidney disease that salt also drives.

2. Perspective: whose costs count

The perspective is the point of view of the analysis, and it decides which costs go into the sum.

PerspectiveCountsSalt policy example
Health system (payer)Costs falling on the health budgetRunning the regulator’s salt programme; hospital and drug costs of strokes avoided
SocietalAll costs, whoever bears themAdds industry reformulation, patients’ time and travel, and productivity lost to illness and early death
Why it matters
The same policy can look different under each. Prevention that keeps working-age adults healthy gains a lot from the societal view, because productivity counts. A health ministry with a fixed budget cares about the health system view, because that is the money it controls.
How to read it
Find the stated perspective, then check the cost list matches it. The US Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness recommends reporting both; NICE uses the health system view (NHS and personal social services).
What a change means
Moving from health system to societal usually makes prevention look better (more savings counted) and can turn a cost-effective policy into a cost-saving one. If the conclusion flips, say so: the answer depends on who pays.
Trap

Mixing perspectives: counting productivity gains (societal) but leaving out industry’s reformulation costs (also societal). Every cost and benefit in the stated perspective goes in, or the result is biased towards the policy.

Analogy

A family buying solar panels. From the household’s view, the question is the electricity bill. From the town’s view, it also includes the grid, the installer’s jobs and cleaner air. Same panels, different sums.

3. Discounting

Discounting gives costs and health that arrive in the future less weight than those that arrive now.

What it does
Each year’s costs and health gains are shrunk by a fixed percentage per year into the future, then added up. At 3% a year, a QALY gained 25 years from now counts as about half a QALY today, and one gained 50 years from now as under a quarter.
Why it’s done
People prefer good things sooner, and money spent now could be invested and grow. Health is discounted too, usually at the same rate as costs; otherwise you could always improve a result by delaying the programme.
How to read it
Typical rates are 3% a year (WHO-CHOICE, US Second Panel) and 3.5% (NICE), for both costs and health. Results should state the rate and show 0% as a sensitivity analysis.
What a change means
Raising the rate hurts interventions whose benefits come late. Prevention pays up front and gains decades later, so a higher rate makes it look worse. Going from 3% to 0% can make a salt policy look much better.
What future health is worth today

Two programmes each gain 10 QALYs: treatment now, prevention 30 years from now. Move the discount rate and watch the later gains shrink.

Trap

Mixing discounted and undiscounted numbers. GBD reports DALYs without discounting; WHO-CHOICE and most economic evaluations discount at 3%. If you take DALYs straight from GBD and costs from a discounted model, the ratio is wrong. In Project 1, report undiscounted health for comparison with GBD, and discount both costs and DALYs for the economic results.

Example

A smoking cessation programme in teenagers costs money this year; the lung cancers it prevents happen 40 years later. Discounted at 3.5%, those future gains shrink to about a quarter of their face value. A drug that treats lung cancer now faces no such shrinkage. This is why prevention often looks worse in economic evaluation than in burden-of-disease studies.

Analogy

Would you take 100 riyals today or 100 riyals in ten years? Almost everyone takes it today. Discounting writes that preference into the arithmetic.

4. The ICER and the cost-effectiveness plane

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) is the extra cost of each extra unit of health you get by choosing one option over the next best.

\[ \text{ICER} = \frac{\text{Cost}_{\text{new}} - \text{Cost}_{\text{comparator}}}{\text{Health}_{\text{new}} - \text{Health}_{\text{comparator}}} \]
What it is
Extra cost divided by extra health, e.g. “SAR 20,000 per DALY averted”. Incremental is the key word: it is always against an alternative, never on its own.
Why it matters
It is the number that goes to the decision-maker and is compared with the threshold (next section). Lower is better: you buy health more cheaply.
How to read it
Place the result on the cost-effectiveness plane: extra health on the horizontal axis, extra cost on the vertical. The quadrant tells you more than the number.
What a change means
An ICER going from 10,000 to 40,000 per DALY means each unit of health now costs four times as much. Whether that matters depends only on where the threshold sits.
QuadrantNew option isDecision
South-eastMore health, less costDominant (cost-saving): adopt
North-westLess health, more costDominated: reject
North-eastMore health, more costTrade-off: compare the ICER with the threshold
South-westLess health, less costTrade-off: are the savings worth the health lost?
The cost-effectiveness plane

It starts at the NMB example in section 6: 100 DALYs averted for SAR 2 million more. Move the threshold to 15,000, then drag the point into each quadrant.

With several options, sort them by cost, drop any that are dominated, and also drop any that are extendedly dominated: options beaten by a mix of their neighbours, which shows up as an ICER higher than the next, more effective option’s. What remains is the efficiency frontier, where each step up costs more per unit of health than the last.

Trap

Two classic errors. First, comparing every option with “do nothing” (an average ratio) instead of with the next best option; this hides an expensive add-on behind a cheap first step. Second, ranking negative ICERs: −5,000 can mean dominant (south-east) or dominated (north-west). Never read the sign without the quadrant.

Example · Project 1 (illustrative)

Three options: no policy, the 2019 bread limit alone, and bread plus the 2025 voluntary limits. The ICER for “bread plus voluntary” must be calculated against “bread alone”, not against “no policy”. If the bread limit is cost-saving, it sits in the south-east quadrant, and the question becomes whether the extra step is still worth it.

From your ML background

The efficiency frontier is a Pareto front over two objectives, cost and health. Dominated options are off the front. The threshold then picks one point on it, the way a fixed trade-off weight picks one point on a precision–recall curve.

5. Thresholds: what counts as worth it

The threshold is the most a health system should pay for one unit of health, and it turns an ICER into a yes or no.

ApproachWhat it saysProblem
GDP per capita (WHO Commission on Macroeconomics and Health, 2001; long used by WHO-CHOICE)Below 1× GDP per capita per DALY averted: highly cost-effective. 1–3×: cost-effective.Not tied to any budget, so nearly everything passes
Opportunity cost (supply side)What the health system actually gives up: the health lost when money is moved from existing servicesHard to estimate; few countries have estimates
Set by an agencyA stated range, e.g. NICE’s £25,000–35,000 per QALY (raised from £20,000–30,000 in 2026)Partly a political choice
Why it matters
The same ICER is “worth it” under one threshold and not under another. Choosing the threshold is often the decision in disguise.
Why GDP thresholds were dropped
They ask what a life-year is worth to the economy, not what the budget can buy elsewhere. In a fixed budget, paying for a new programme means stopping something else, and the health lost there is the real cost. WHO’s own authors advised against relying on GDP thresholds alone as a decision rule (Bertram et al., 2016), and WHO has since moved away from recommending them.
How to read it
Opportunity-cost estimates are usually well below GDP per capita. For the English NHS, Claxton and colleagues estimated about £13,000 per QALY, below NICE’s own range. Country estimates by Woods and colleagues often fall well below 1× GDP per capita.
What a change means
Lowering the threshold from 3× to 0.5× GDP per capita turns many “cost-effective” interventions into poor value. Cost-saving ones pass under any threshold.
Trap

Reading “below the threshold” as “affordable”. The threshold judges value per unit of health, not total spending. A cheap-per-DALY programme for the whole population can still cost more than the budget allows. That is the job of a budget impact analysis, reported alongside.

Example · Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia is a high-income country, so a 1–3× GDP rule sets a very high bar that almost any programme clears. Project 1 should not lean on it. Instead, report the ICER, show results across a range of thresholds (Lecture 1.12 turns this into a curve), and note where opportunity-cost estimates would place the line.

From your ML background

The opportunity-cost threshold is a shadow price: the value of relaxing the budget constraint by one riyal, in units of health. It plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier in a budget-constrained optimisation.

6. Net monetary benefit

Net monetary benefit (NMB) turns health into money at the threshold, then subtracts the cost, giving one number where bigger is better.

What it is
\( \text{NMB} = \lambda \times \text{health} - \text{cost} \), where \( \lambda \) is the threshold. Each DALY averted is valued at the threshold price, then costs are taken away.
Why it matters
Ratios misbehave: when the health difference is near zero, the ICER jumps to huge or negative values, and you cannot average ratios. NMB is a plain difference, so it averages cleanly, works for any number of options, and is what probabilistic analysis and value of information use (Lecture 1.12).
How to read it
Choose the option with the highest NMB. Incremental NMB above zero means the new option is cost-effective at that threshold; it is the same decision as ICER below threshold, without the ratio.
What a change means
NMB depends on the threshold you chose. Raising \( \lambda \) gives health more weight, so the more effective option gains. This is why results are shown across a range of thresholds.
Trap

Reporting NMB without the threshold used. An NMB of SAR 50 million means nothing unless you know what each DALY was priced at.

Example (illustrative numbers)

Option A averts 100 DALYs for 2 million more than option B. At a threshold of 30,000 per DALY, the health is worth 3 million, so incremental NMB is +1 million: choose A. At 15,000 per DALY, it is −0.5 million: choose B. Same data, different threshold, different answer.

From your ML background

NMB is a scalarised objective: two goals collapsed into one with a fixed exchange rate, like adding a weighted penalty to a loss function. The decision rule is then argmax of expected NMB.

7. Reporting: CHEERS 2022

CHEERS 2022 (Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards) is the 28-item checklist journals expect any economic evaluation to follow.

What it covers
Each decision in this lecture must be stated: comparators, perspective, time horizon, discount rate, outcome measure, costing methods, currency and price year, and how uncertainty was handled. The 2022 update added items on a health economic analysis plan, engagement with patients and others affected, and distributional effects (who gains).
Why it matters
Reviewers check it item by item, and most journals require the completed checklist on submission. Fill it in while designing the model, not after.
How to tell a good report
You could rebuild the model from the paper and its appendix. Every input has a source, a value and a distribution.
Trap

Treating CHEERS as a quality score. It checks that you reported your choices, not that they were good. A model can tick all 28 items and still use the wrong comparator.

Example · Project 1 costs

WHO-CHOICE publishes country-specific unit costs for health service delivery, such as an inpatient bed-day or an outpatient visit. They cover the “hotel” costs of care, not drugs or tests, and come in a past price year. CHEERS requires you to state the price year, how you inflated to current prices, and how you converted currencies, so record each step as you go.

Check yourself

  1. A paper reports a salt reformulation policy at “SAR 4,000 per stroke averted” and calls it highly cost-effective. What is missing before you can agree?

    Show answer

    It is a CEA in a disease-specific unit, so there is no threshold to compare it with, and it ignores the heart and kidney disease salt also causes. You need a cost per DALY or QALY, the comparator, the perspective and the discount rate. Only then can you judge it against a threshold or against other uses of the money.

  2. An analysis finds a programme is cost-saving from the societal perspective but costs the health ministry money. Is that a contradiction?

    Show answer

    No. The societal view counts savings that fall outside the health budget, such as productivity and patients’ own costs. The ministry pays the bill but doesn’t receive those savings. Report both, and say plainly who pays and who gains, because that decides who will fund it.

  3. Why does raising the discount rate from 3% to 5% usually hurt salt reduction more than it hurts stroke thrombolysis?

    Show answer

    Salt reduction pays now and gains health over decades, so its gains are shrunk the most. Thrombolysis costs and benefits arrive at almost the same time, so discounting barely touches it. The higher rate shifts value from prevention towards treatment.

  4. Option C has an ICER of −8,000 per DALY against current practice. A colleague says, “Negative, so it saves money. Adopt it.” What do you check?

    Show answer

    The quadrant. A negative ICER arises both when C gains health and saves money (dominant, adopt) and when C loses health and costs more (dominated, reject). Look at the signs of the cost and health differences separately, not the ratio.

  5. Using the 1–3× GDP-per-capita rule, nearly every intervention in a Saudi model is cost-effective. Why should that worry you, and what would you report instead?

    Show answer

    A rule almost everything passes doesn’t help choose between options, and it isn’t based on what the budget gives up. Report ICERs and NMB across a range of thresholds, show where opportunity-cost estimates (usually well below GDP per capita) would put the line, and add a budget impact analysis.

  6. Why do probabilistic analyses and value-of-information methods work with net monetary benefit rather than the ICER?

    Show answer

    The ICER is a ratio: it explodes when the health difference is near zero, flips sign across quadrants, and cannot be averaged sensibly. NMB is a linear difference at a fixed threshold, so you can take means across thousands of simulations and pick the option with the highest expected value.
