Lecture 3.6

Survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier, Cox models, checking proportional hazards

Block 3 · Biostatistics core
The point

Survival analysis is regression for time until an event when some people’s times are only partly seen. You already know the likelihood. What decides whether a survival paper is right is everything around it: when the clock starts, what it counts (years on study or years of age), who is still being watched, and whether one hazard ratio can honestly summarise the whole follow-up. Project 3 is a survival analysis of NHANES participants linked to death records, so every choice here becomes a sentence in its methods section.

1. Censoring and the assumption behind it

A censored person tells you “no event up to here”; survival methods use that partial information, but only give the right answer if being censored has nothing to do with the risk of the event.

What it is
Right censoring: follow-up stops before the event, because the study ended (administrative censoring) or the person was lost. Interval censoring: the event happened between two visits, time unknown (e.g. diabetes found at a 3-yearly check). Left truncation (delayed entry) is different: people only enter the data if they survived to some point, covered in section 2.
Why it matters
Every survival method assumes non-informative censoring: at any time, people who are censored have the same future risk as those still followed who look like them on the covariates. If the sickest drop out, the survivors look healthier than the cohort really is, and the curves are too optimistic.
How to read it
Ask why each kind of censoring happens. Administrative censoring at a fixed date is usually harmless. Drop-out because of illness, moving into care, or withdrawing after a side effect is the worrying kind. The assumption cannot be tested from the data; it is argued from how the study works.
What a change means
Censoring that rises from 5% to 30% does not by itself bias anything, but it widens intervals and makes any informative part more damaging. Report how many were censored, when, and why.
Trap

Treating deaths from other causes as ordinary censoring and then reading 1 − KM as “the risk of cardiovascular death”. A person who died of cancer will never have a cardiovascular death, so they are not like the people still followed. That is a competing event, not censoring, and it is the whole subject of Lecture 3.7.

Example · Censoring in Project 3

NHANES participants are matched to the National Death Index, so nobody is “lost” in the usual sense: anyone not found dead is assumed alive at the end of follow-up, which is 31 December 2019 in the current public-use linked mortality files. Almost all censoring is administrative, which makes the non-informative assumption easy to defend. The weak points are elsewhere: deaths missed by the linkage, and deaths from other causes. NCHS also changed the follow-up time or cause of death to synthetic values for some records in the public-use files to protect identities; vital status itself was not changed.

Analogy

Counting how long light bulbs last when the shop closes at six. A bulb still on at closing time is not a failure and not useless: you know it lasted at least that long. But if staff quietly removed the flickering ones earlier, the bulbs left would make the brand look better than it is.

2. Choosing the clock: study time, age and delayed entry

The time scale decides who is compared with whom at each death; for cohorts recruited across a wide range of ages, such as NHANES, age is often the better clock.

What it is
On time on study, everyone starts at zero on the day of their exam; age goes in as a covariate. On age as the time scale, each person enters the analysis at their age at the exam and leaves at their age at death or censoring. Entering late is called delayed entry or left truncation: a person examined at 62 contributes nothing before 62, because we only see people who survived to be examined.
Why it matters
A Cox model compares each person who dies with everyone still at risk at that moment on the chosen clock. On study time, a death in year 5 is compared with people of any age who are in their fifth year. On the age scale, a death at 60 is compared only with others who are 60 and under observation. Age, the strongest risk factor for death, is then controlled exactly and without a functional form.
How to read it
Korn, Graubard and Midthune recommended age as the time scale for follow-up of a survey, using NHANES I follow-up as their example. Later simulation and empirical work found that time on study gives very similar hazard ratios if baseline age is modelled flexibly. Both appear in NHANES mortality papers. State which you used and, ideally, show the other as a sensitivity analysis.
What a change means
If the hazard ratio moves noticeably when you switch clocks, the age adjustment on study time was probably too crude (e.g. linear age when risk rises exponentially), or the exposure is strongly tied to age or birth cohort. Investigate before choosing the answer you prefer.
Trap

Using age as the time scale but starting everyone at birth. That counts years before the exam as “at risk” when the person could not have died in the data (they had to survive to be examined): immortal time again (Lecture 2.2). In R the fix is the counting-process form, Surv(age_at_exam, age_at_exit, event), which tells the model when each person entered.

Example · Delayed entry as a sensitivity analysis

Your exposure, the urinary sodium-to-potassium ratio, comes from a single spot urine sample, not a 24-hour collection (Lecture 2.14). People who are already ill eat less, so their urine may look “low sodium” shortly before they die. A standard check is to start follow-up two years after the exam (delayed entry at year 2, dropping people who died earlier). If the hazard ratio changes a lot, early deaths from existing illness were driving it.

From your ML background

Choosing the time scale is choosing the matching key in a matched design: each death becomes a case, matched to everyone at risk at the same value of the clock. Age as time scale is matching exactly on age, instead of putting age into the model as a feature and hoping its shape is right.

3. Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test

A Kaplan-Meier curve is a chain of conditional survival probabilities, recalculated at each death among those still being watched; read it together with the numbers at risk beneath it.

What it is
At each death, the curve drops by the share of the people still at risk who died. Censored people leave the risk set quietly, without a drop. The survival probability at any time is the product of all the “survived this step” fractions so far. The log-rank test compares observed with expected deaths in each group at every death time, pooled over follow-up.
Why it matters
It is the standard first picture of any time-to-event study, and it assumes nothing about the shape of the hazard. Most survival claims a reader remembers come from the curve, not the table.
How to read it
Read the numbers at risk first. Where they are small, each death makes a big step and the curve is unreliable: Pocock, Clayton and Altman suggested stopping the plot once only about 10–20% of patients remain in follow-up. Look at the vertical gap at a time that matters clinically, not at where the curves end. Curves that cross, or diverge only late, mean one hazard ratio will not summarise them well (section 7).
What a change means
A gap that widens steadily on the survival scale is what proportional hazards looks like. A gap that opens early and stays constant means the effect happened early and then stopped. The log-rank test has most power when hazards are proportional, and little when curves cross.
Build a Kaplan-Meier plot

Start with the small trial and press “New random sample” a few times: watch how far the tails wander and how wide the Cox interval is. Then try the big trial, and heavy drop-out (the tails thin out sooner). Under “No true effect”, see how often the curves still look different.

Trap

Reading the end of the tail. A curve that ends at 40% after a flat stretch with three people at risk is not evidence that 40% are cured; one more death would drop it sharply. KMunicate (Morris and colleagues, 2019) found readers preferred plots with confidence bands and a table of numbers at risk, censored and with events under the axis. Ask for those when you review, and draw them in Project 3.

library(survival)
km <- survfit(Surv(years, died) ~ nak_group, data = d)
summary(km, times = c(5, 10))            # survival at fixed times, with CIs
survdiff(Surv(years, died) ~ nak_group, data = d)   # log-rank test
library(survey)                          # Project 3: use the design (Lecture 3.4)
svykm(Surv(years, died) ~ nak_group, design = des)

survfit builds the curves; summary(..., times =) reads them at chosen times; survdiff runs the log-rank test. In NHANES, svykm gives curves that use the survey weights, so they describe the US population rather than the sample.

Analogy

A climbing route with stages. The chance of reaching the summit is the chance of passing stage 1, times the chance of passing stage 2 given you passed stage 1, and so on. Climbers who turn back for the weather (censored) are not failures; they just stop counting in the stages they never reached.

4. What a hazard ratio does and does not mean

A hazard is an event rate among people still event-free right now; a hazard ratio compares those rates, which is not the same as comparing risks.

What it is
The hazard at time \(t\) is the instantaneous rate of the event among those still at risk at \(t\), in events per person-time. The hazard ratio (HR) is the ratio of two hazards, for example per doubling of the sodium-to-potassium ratio, or treated versus control.
Why it matters
It is the default output of a Cox model and the number most papers lead with. But patients and policymakers care about risk (“what fraction will have died by 10 years?”) and time (“how much longer will I live?”), and the HR answers neither directly.
How to read it
HR 0.7: at any moment, among people still alive, the treated die at 70% of the control rate. When events are rare (under about 10–15% by the end) the HR is close to the risk ratio. When they are common it is not: the risk ratio is pulled towards 1 because everyone eventually dies.
What a change means
An HR moving from 1.2 to 2 takes the exposed from a 20% higher rate to double the rate, at every moment. What that means for absolute risk depends on the baseline hazard: an HR of 2 for a rare cause of death may add a fraction of a percentage point over 10 years; for a common one, several points.
Trap

“HR 0.5 means half as many people die.” Over long follow-up with a common outcome, an HR of 0.5 can go with a 10-year risk ratio of 0.6 or 0.7, and over a lifetime everyone dies in both groups. Report absolute risks or survival at fixed times alongside the HR (Lecture 2.17 made the same point for risk scores).

Example · The built-in selection bias of hazard ratios

Hernán (2010), “The hazards of hazard ratios”, points out that a period-specific HR compares the people who are still alive in each group, and after the first deaths those survivors are no longer comparable, even in a randomised trial. If treatment kills the most susceptible early, the treated survivors are a hardier group, and later HRs look protective even when the treatment does no good to anyone. He used the Women’s Health Initiative trial of oestrogen plus progestin, where the HR for coronary heart disease was highest in the first year and fell over follow-up. His advice: report survival curves and risks at fixed times, which keep the randomised comparison intact.

From your ML background

The hazard is the event density divided by the share still event-free: the same object as the per-step “probability of churn given not churned yet” in a discrete-time model. A discrete-time survival model is just logistic regression on person-period rows, and a Cox model is its continuous-time limit with the baseline left unspecified.

5. The Cox model and time-varying covariates

The Cox model estimates hazard ratios without ever estimating the baseline hazard, by comparing, at each death, the person who died with everyone still at risk.

\[ h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\, e^{\beta x} \]
What it is
The hazard for a person with covariates \(x\) is an unspecified baseline hazard \(h_0(t)\), which can have any shape over time, multiplied by \(e^{\beta x}\). So \(e^{\beta}\) is the HR per unit of \(x\), assumed the same at every time: the proportional hazards assumption.
Why it matters
Leaving the baseline free is why it is the workhorse: you need no assumption about how mortality changes with time or age. It handles delayed entry and covariates that change during follow-up, and the survey package fits it with survey weights and design-based standard errors.
How to read it
Report the HR per a meaningful unit (per doubling, per standard deviation, or by quartile), with its CI. A continuous exposure such as the sodium-to-potassium ratio should be checked for non-linearity with splines before you report a single per-unit HR. To get absolute risks, combine the HRs with the estimated baseline (survfit on the fitted model).
What a change means
Adding a confounder that moves the HR from 1.40 to 1.15 means much of the crude association ran through that variable. Ties in event times (NHANES follow-up is in whole months) are handled by the Efron method, the default in R.
library(survival); library(survey)
d$age_exit <- d$age_exam + d$PERMTH_EXM / 12      # months from exam to exit
d$cvd <- d$MORTSTAT == 1 & d$UCOD_LEADING == "001" # heart disease; see 3.7
des <- svydesign(ids = ~SDMVPSU, strata = ~SDMVSTRA, weights = ~wt_pooled,
                 nest = TRUE, data = d)
fit <- svycoxph(Surv(age_exam, age_exit, cvd) ~ log2(nak) + sex + race +
                  smoker + bmi + egfr, design = subset(des, eligible))
summary(fit)   # exp(coef) = HR per doubling of the Na/K ratio

This uses age as the time scale with delayed entry at the exam age. PERMTH_EXM is the follow-up in months from the exam in the linked mortality file. The design is subset, not the data, so the standard errors stay correct (Lecture 3.4). Treating other deaths as censored here gives a cause-specific HR, which Lecture 3.7 explains.

Trap

Coding a covariate measured during follow-up as if it were known at baseline (“ever used a statin”). People must survive to start the drug, so the exposed group gets guaranteed survival time: immortal time bias (Lectures 2.2 and 5.3). Enter it as time-varying. And be careful adjusting for time-varying covariates that are themselves affected by earlier exposure: that needs the g-methods of Block 4.

Gulf example

Saudi or Emirati registries with repeated HbA1c and blood pressure readings are natural for time-varying covariates; NHANES is not, because exposure and covariates are measured once, at the exam. That is a real limitation of Project 3 to state openly: one spot urine sample stands in for decades of diet, and its noise pulls the HR towards 1 (regression dilution).

6. Checking proportional hazards

Proportional hazards means the HR is flat over time; check it by plotting how the estimated effect changes with time, not just by reading a p-value.

What it is
Scaled Schoenfeld residuals show, at each death, how far the person who died differs on a covariate from what the model expected. Plotted against time and smoothed, they trace how the coefficient changes over time: a flat line means PH holds. cox.zph plots them and gives a test per covariate and overall (Grambsch and Therneau). Log-log plots (log of minus log survival against log time, one curve per group) should show roughly parallel curves under PH.
Why it matters
If the HR changes over time, the single Cox HR is a weighted average whose weights depend on when deaths happen and on censoring. It may still be a useful summary, or it may hide effects that run in opposite directions.
How to read it
Look at the size and shape of the drift, not the p-value. In a large cohort like NHANES, a trivial drift will be “significant”; in a small trial, a large one may not be. A smooth line moving from log HR 0.4 to 0.1 is a fading effect. A line crossing zero is a reversal, and needs one of the fixes in section 7.
What a change means
On the age time scale, non-proportionality means the HR changes with age, which is often real and interesting (many risk factors matter less at older ages). Report the age-specific HRs rather than hiding them.
fit0 <- coxph(Surv(age_exam, age_exit, cvd) ~ log2(nak) + sex + smoker + egfr,
              data = d, weights = wt_pooled)
zp <- cox.zph(fit0)
zp                 # test per covariate and a global test
plot(zp[1]); abline(h = coef(fit0)[1], lty = 2)   # beta(t) for Na/K vs the Cox average

cox.zph runs on a coxph fit; with survey data, use it as a diagnostic picture, not a design-based test. The dashed line is the single Cox estimate: compare the smoothed curve with it.

Trap

“The global PH test was not significant, so the assumption holds.” Absence of evidence again (Lecture 2.5). With few events the test has little power, and crossing hazards can average out in a global test. Always look at the plot.

7. When proportional hazards fails

When the HR changes over time, either model the change or switch to a summary that does not need proportional hazards, such as survival at fixed times or the restricted mean survival time.

What it is
Three standard fixes. Stratify on a covariate that violates PH but is not of interest (e.g. sex): each stratum gets its own baseline hazard and no HR for it. Time-varying coefficient: let the exposure’s HR differ by period of follow-up (split time with survSplit) or as a smooth function of time. Change the summary: the restricted mean survival time (RMST) is the average event-free time up to a chosen horizon \(\tau\), the area under the survival curve.
Why it matters
With crossing hazards, a single HR near 1 can sit on top of a large early harm and a large late benefit. RMST and landmark survival describe what actually happened, in units patients understand: months of life, or percentage alive at 5 years.
How to read it
An RMST difference of +3 months to 10 years means the treated lived, on average, 3 months longer within those 10 years. Choose \(\tau\) in advance, at a time where enough people are still at risk in both groups. Report the period-specific HRs if you split time.
What a change means
Moving \(\tau\) changes the RMST difference: a treatment with early harm and late benefit loses at short horizons and gains at long ones. That is information, not a flaw.
\[ \text{RMST}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} S(t)\, dt \]
One hazard ratio, two very different curves

The default is surgery-like: three times the hazard in the first 18 months, then less than half. The Cox HR is about 1, “no effect”. Read survival at 1 year and at 10 years, and the RMST. Then move τ from 10 down to 2, and compare with the proportional scenario.

Trap

Concluding “no effect” from an HR of 1.0 when the curves cross. The right reading is “the effect changes over time”: the treated do worse early and better later. Which matters more is a value judgement for patients, and RMST at a pre-specified horizon makes the trade explicit.

ds <- survSplit(Surv(years, cvd) ~ ., data = d, cut = 5, episode = "period")
coxph(Surv(tstart, years, cvd) ~ log2(nak):strata(period) + sex + smoker, data = ds)
library(survRM2)                       # RMST difference with a CI
rmst2(d$years, d$cvd, arm = d$high_nak, tau = 10)

The first two lines split follow-up at year 5 and fit a separate Na/K HR for years 0–5 and after 5 (a classic check for reverse causation from early illness). rmst2 compares RMST up to 10 years between two groups; arm must be coded 0/1.

Example · Why trials now report RMST

Uno and colleagues (2014) argued that RMST should be reported routinely beside the HR, because it does not depend on proportional hazards and gives an absolute, clinically readable difference. Delayed benefit, as with some cancer immunotherapies, is the common case where PH fails in trials; in cohorts, early follow-up dominated by people already ill is the common case.

From your ML background

A time-varying coefficient is an interaction between the feature and time; stratifying is giving each stratum its own intercept function. RMST is just the expectation of \(\min(T, \tau)\): a truncated mean, estimable without any model. Survival forests and DeepSurv-type models (Python: lifelines, scikit-survival) relax PH too, but give up the single interpretable HR.

Check yourself

  1. In a cohort of heart-failure patients, those who stop attending clinic are censored at their last visit. A colleague says censoring is only 12%, so it cannot matter. What do you say?

    Show answer

    The amount matters less than the reason. Patients who stop attending may be too ill to come, or may have died elsewhere; if so, censoring is informative and survival is overestimated. Check whether censored patients were sicker at their last visit, and try linkage to death records or a sensitivity analysis that assumes worse outcomes for those censored.

  2. You analyse NHANES on time on study with age as a linear covariate and get HR 1.35; on the age time scale you get HR 1.12. Which do you believe, and what do you check?

    Show answer

    Suspect the time-on-study model: mortality rises roughly exponentially with age, so linear age leaves residual confounding by age, and the exposure probably varies with age. Refit time on study with splines for baseline age (and perhaps birth cohort). If it then agrees with the age-scale result, report the age-scale model and show the other as a sensitivity analysis.

  3. A Kaplan-Meier plot shows the treated curve flattening at 55% from year 6 to year 9, while the control curve keeps falling. The table shows 8 treated patients at risk at year 7. What can you conclude about long-term benefit?

    Show answer

    Very little. With 8 at risk, one or two deaths would change the tail a lot, and the flat stretch may simply mean no deaths happened to occur among a handful of people. Compare survival at a time where numbers are still large, with confidence intervals, and do not describe a plateau as a cure.

  4. A trial reports HR 0.95 (95% CI 0.80–1.12) for a new surgical approach, and the curves cross at 2 years. The press release says “no difference in survival”. Is that fair?

    Show answer

    No. Crossing curves mean the effect changes over time, and one HR averages early harm with later benefit (or the reverse). Ask for survival at 1 and 5 years and the RMST at a pre-specified horizon. Patients may reasonably choose differently depending on whether they weigh early risk or long-term gain.

  5. In Project 3, the HR for the highest versus lowest Na/K quartile is 1.40 over the whole follow-up, but 1.90 in the first 2 years and 1.20 afterwards. How do you interpret and report this?

    Show answer

    The early excess is consistent with reverse causation: people already ill eat differently, which shows up in a single spot urine shortly before death. Report the period-specific HRs, use the result after excluding the first 2 years as the main or key sensitivity analysis, and avoid presenting 1.40 as a constant causal effect. Also note that the later HR is probably diluted towards 1 by the noise of one spot sample.
