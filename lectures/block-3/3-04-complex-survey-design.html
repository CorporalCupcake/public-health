Lecture 3.4

Complex survey design: weights, strata, clusters, design effects

Block 3 · Biostatistics core
The point

National health surveys such as NHANES, WHO STEPS and the Saudi Health Interview Survey are not simple random samples. Weights fix who was over- or under-sampled, so ignoring them biases estimates. Clusters and strata change how precise the estimates are, so ignoring them gives wrong standard errors, usually too small. Project 3 is a survey-weighted cohort: every number in it, from Table 1 to the hazard ratios, must come from a design object, with the right weight, subsetted the right way.

1. Why a survey is not a simple random sample

Three design features each break a different part of a naive analysis: weights affect the estimate, clusters and strata affect its standard error.

What it is
Most national surveys use a multistage design: divide the country into strata (regions, urban/rural), pick a few primary sampling units (PSUs, such as counties or census blocks) in each, then households within PSUs, then people within households. Some groups are deliberately sampled more (oversampling) so they can be studied on their own.
Why it matters
Visiting a few areas is far cheaper than scattering interviews across the country, and oversampling buys precision for small groups. The price is that people are no longer equally likely to be chosen, nor independent of each other.
How to read it
In a survey paper’s methods, look for the words “weighted”, “accounting for strata and clusters” and the software (R survey, Stata svy, SAS PROC SURVEY). Missing any of the three is a reviewer comment.

WeightsUnequal selection, non-responseBiased means, prevalences and sometimes associations

Example · Saudi Health Interview Survey 2013

The Saudi Ministry of Health and IHME ran a national multistage household survey covering all 13 regions: subregions and blocks chosen with probability proportional to size, households sampled within blocks, then one adult (15 or older) picked at random from the household. 10,735 people completed it. Its weights combined the household and within-household selection probabilities with an age–sex adjustment to the Saudi population, which is exactly the chain in the diagram.

Trap

“It’s a large national sample, so it’s representative.” A national survey is representative only when analysed with its weights and design. Unweighted NHANES over-represents the groups it deliberately oversampled.

2. Sampling weights

A person’s weight is the number of people in the population they stand for; it undoes unequal selection and non-response.

What it is
Three layers multiplied together. Design weight: 1 / probability of selection. Non-response adjustment: inflate the weights of respondents who resemble the non-respondents. Post-stratification (or calibration): scale weights so totals by age, sex and ethnicity match the census.
Why it matters
Oversampled groups are over-represented in the raw data. If they differ on the outcome, any unweighted mean or prevalence drifts towards them.
How to read it
Weights sum to the population size. A weight of 20,000 means this person represents 20,000 people. The more the weights vary, the more precision you lose: the weighting design effect is about 1 + (coefficient of variation of the weights)².
What a change means
If weighted and unweighted means differ a lot, the oversampled groups differ on the outcome, and the weights are doing real work. If they barely differ, weights cost precision but change little.
Oversampling shifts a naive mean; weights restore it

One group makes up a small share of the population but is oversampled in the survey and has higher blood pressure. Increase the oversampling and compare the unweighted and weighted means with the truth.

Example · NHANES

NHANES deliberately oversamples. In 2011–2014 the oversampled groups were Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic Asian people, low-income non-Hispanic White and other people, and adults aged 80 and over; from 2015 the low-income cut-off rose from 130% to 185% of the poverty level. Because these groups differ in blood pressure, diet and mortality, unweighted NHANES estimates describe the sample, not the United States.

Trap

Passing survey weights to lm(weights = ) or glm(weights = ). Those functions treat weights as precision or frequency weights: the point estimates match the survey ones, but the standard errors are wrong, sometimes wildly. Use svyglm on a design object.

Example · Kuwait STEPS 2014

Kuwait’s 2014 STEPS survey (public-use microdata, the Project 3 fallback) drew Kuwaiti citizens aged 18–69 at random from the civil registry within eight sex-by-age strata. Its weights are the inverse of each person’s selection probability, adjusted to the population’s age–sex structure. Stratified sampling from a registry has no clustering, so its design effect comes mostly from unequal weights. Read each survey’s own design before reusing code written for another.

3. Clusters, strata and the design effect

People in the same cluster resemble each other, so each extra person from the same cluster adds less new information; the design effect says how much less.

What it is
The design effect (Deff) is the variance of an estimate under the actual design divided by its variance under a simple random sample of the same size. For cluster sampling with \(m\) people per cluster and intra-cluster correlation \(\rho\) (how alike people in the same cluster are, 0 to 1): \[ \text{Deff} \approx 1 + (m - 1)\,\rho \]
Why it matters
The effective sample size is n / Deff. A survey of 6,000 people with Deff 3 carries the information of 2,000 independent ones. Standard errors grow by √Deff.
How to read it
Deff near 1: little clustering. Deff of 1.5–3 is common in household health surveys. Even a tiny ρ matters with big clusters: ρ = 0.05 and 21 people per cluster gives Deff = 2.
What a change means
Halving people per cluster (and doubling clusters) cuts Deff, which is why survey designers trade fieldwork cost against cluster size. Stratification works the other way, nudging Deff below what clustering alone would give.
Design effect: same n, less information

1,200 people measured for systolic blood pressure, either as a simple random sample or in clusters. Raise the intra-cluster correlation or the cluster size and watch the cluster-sample estimates spread out.

library(survey)
options(survey.lonely.psu = "adjust")      # strata left with one PSU
des <- svydesign(ids = ~SDMVPSU, strata = ~SDMVSTRA, weights = ~WTMEC2YR,
                 nest = TRUE, data = nhanes)  # the FULL file, everyone
svymean(~sbp, des, na.rm = TRUE, deff = TRUE)

SDMVSTRA and SDMVPSU are NHANES’s masked variance strata and PSUs (pseudo-strata and pseudo-PSUs that protect confidentiality). nest = TRUE says PSU numbers restart in each stratum. The last line gives the weighted mean, its design-based standard error and the design effect.

Trap

Quoting “the survey’s design effect” as one number. Deff belongs to each estimate. Things that cluster by place (ethnicity, water supply, a regional diet) have big design effects; things that vary within every neighbourhood (sex, many biomarkers) have small ones. Regression coefficients usually have smaller design effects than means.

Analogy

Asking ten members of one family what they eat tells you mostly about one kitchen. Ten strangers tell you about ten. Same number of interviews, very different information.

4. Variance estimation: linearisation and replicate weights

Two ways to get design-based standard errors: Taylor linearisation uses strata and PSUs directly; replicate weights re-estimate the statistic many times with PSUs dropped or reweighted.

What it is
Taylor linearisation approximates the statistic as a weighted sum and computes its variance from between-PSU variation within strata. It is the sandwich estimator of Lecture 3.3 applied to the design. Replicate weights (jackknife, balanced repeated replication, bootstrap) are columns of altered weights; the spread of the estimate across them is the variance.
Why it matters
Some data providers release replicate weights instead of strata and PSUs, to protect confidentiality. Replicates also handle awkward statistics (medians, percentiles, ratios) without new formulas.
How to read it
The two approaches usually agree closely. Use what the documentation recommends. The design degrees of freedom are roughly the number of PSUs minus the number of strata: for one two-year NHANES cycle that is about 15, not thousands, so intervals use t on few degrees of freedom.
rep_des <- as.svrepdesign(des, type = "JKn")   # jackknife replicates from PSUs
svymean(~sbp, rep_des, na.rm = TRUE)            # compare with linearisation
svyquantile(~sbp, des, quantiles = c(0.5, 0.9), na.rm = TRUE)

The first line builds jackknife replicate weights by dropping one PSU at a time; the second re-estimates the mean with them. When a file ships its own replicate weights, build the design with svrepdesign() using the replicate type and scale stated in its documentation.

Project 3 · the urine files

NHANES urinary sodium and potassium were measured on stored spot (casual) urine from special subsamples, not on 24-hour collections (the 2014 24-hour urine study was a separate one-year substudy). Each subsample file has its own weight and replicate weights: the 2010 file uses WTNA, with jackknife replicates recommended over linearisation; the 2013–2014 file uses WT1YUR. Their documentation also states that the NHANES III, 2010 and 2013–2014 urine electrolyte files are available only through NCHS Research Data Centers. Check access and the right weight in the week-33 feasibility count before designing around them.

Trap

Mixing a subsample weight with the full-sample replicate weights, or the other way round. NCHS notes this as a common processing error. The weight and its replicates come as a set.

5. Subpopulations: subset the design, not the data

To analyse adults, or women, or people with complete urine data, build the design on the full file and then subset the design object.

What it is
A domain (subpopulation) analysis keeps every PSU in the variance calculation and gives people outside the domain zero contribution. subset(design, ...) does this; filtering the data frame first does not.
Why it matters
The number of people in a domain who land in the sample is itself random, and that randomness belongs in the standard error. Filtering first also drops PSUs that happen to have no domain members, which leaves strata with one PSU and breaks the variance formula.
How to read it
Point estimates are identical either way; only the standard errors differ. If a paper says “we restricted the dataset to adults aged 20 and over and then applied survey weights”, ask how.
adults <- subset(des, RIDAGEYR >= 20 & !is.na(nak_ratio))
svyby(~sbp, ~RIAGENDR, adults, svymean, na.rm = TRUE)   # mean SBP by sex
svytable(~htn + RIAGENDR, adults)                       # weighted counts
Trap

filter(age >= 20) before svydesign(). It runs without complaint, often gives nearly right answers in big domains, and goes wrong in small ones, which are exactly the subgroup analyses reviewers scrutinise. Excluding people with missing values is also a domain restriction, and if many are missing it is a missing-data problem too (Lecture 3.5).

6. Choosing the weight and combining NHANES cycles

Use the weight for the variable measured on the fewest people, and rescale weights when you pool cycles.

What it is
NHANES releases several weights: interview (WTINT2YR), examination (WTMEC2YR), and subsample weights for fasting labs, dietary recalls or special urine tests. NCHS’s rule is the “least common denominator”: use the weight of the variable collected on the smallest number of people.
Why it matters
Each weight is built for the people who took part in that component. Using the exam weight on a subsample variable misrepresents who the subsample stands for.
How to read it
To pool two-year cycles from 2001–2002 on, divide each cycle’s two-year weight by the number of cycles. For analyses including 1999–2000, start from the four-year 1999–2002 weight (WTMEC4YR) and give it 2/n. The 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic file covers 3.2 years, so combined with 2015–2016 the shares are 2/5.2 and 3.2/5.2. NCHS advises against pooling the August 2021–August 2023 cycle with earlier ones.
pooled <- bind_rows(n1516, n17p) |>
  mutate(wt = if_else(cycle == "2015-2016",
                      WTMEC2YR * 2 / 5.2,      # two-year cycle
                      WTMECPRP * 3.2 / 5.2))   # 2017–Mar 2020 pre-pandemic
des <- svydesign(ids = ~SDMVPSU, strata = ~SDMVSTRA, weights = ~wt,
                 nest = TRUE, data = pooled)

This stacks two releases, gives each its share of the 5.2 years, and builds one design. Masked strata numbers differ across cycles, so the strata stay distinct.

Trap

Stacking cycles without rescaling. Means and their standard errors barely change, which hides the mistake, but population totals (“X million Americans”) double. And if the outcome changed over time, a pooled estimate describes an average period, not a year.

7. Regression with survey data

Fit models on the design object; if weighted and unweighted coefficients disagree, treat it as a warning about your model, not a nuisance.

What it is
svyglm and svycoxph fit GLMs and Cox models with design-based standard errors. Use quasibinomial() or quasipoisson() families, since weighted data are not counts.
Why it matters
For descriptive estimates, weighting is not optional. For associations it is debated: an unweighted model adjusted for the design variables can be more precise, but only if it is correctly specified. Weighting targets the population-average association even when it is not.
How to read it
If weighted and unweighted coefficients differ markedly, the effect probably varies across the oversampled groups; look for an interaction (DuMouchel and Duncan’s point). Use Wald tests (regTermTest) rather than AIC or ordinary likelihood-ratio tests, which do not apply to weighted pseudo-likelihoods.
fit <- svyglm(htn ~ nak_q + RIDAGEYR + RIAGENDR, design = adults,
              family = quasipoisson())
exp(cbind(PR = coef(fit), confint(fit)))         # prevalence ratios
cox <- svycoxph(Surv(fu_months, cvd_death) ~ nak_q + RIDAGEYR + RIAGENDR,
                design = adults)                 # Project 3 main model shape
regTermTest(cox, ~nak_q)                         # joint Wald test for quartiles

The first model gives prevalence ratios with design-based (already robust) intervals, the modified Poisson of Lecture 3.3 for free. The second is the shape of the Project 3 survival model (Lecture 3.6); the last line tests all quartile terms together.

Example · pooling Gulf STEPS surveys

The Project 3 fallback pools STEPS surveys from several GCC countries. Each survey keeps its own strata and PSUs: make stratum and PSU identifiers unique by country, and treat country as a stratum. Then decide what the weights should represent: each country’s population (a pooled “GCC adult” estimate dominated by Saudi Arabia) or each country equally. Say which, and why.

From your ML background

sample_weight in scikit-learn gives weighted point estimates but no design-based uncertainty. Clustering also matters for validation: if people from the same PSU fall in both training and test folds, performance is optimistic. Split folds by PSU (grouped cross-validation), the survey version of patient-level splitting.

Check yourself

  1. A student analyses NHANES with lm(), unweighted, and finds a prevalence of hypertension 4 percentage points away from the CDC’s published figure for the same cycle. What is the most likely reason?

    Show answer

    Oversampling. NHANES oversamples groups such as the oldest adults, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic and Asian adults and low-income adults, whose hypertension prevalence differs from the population average (some higher, some lower). Unweighted, the sample over-represents them, so the estimate drifts towards their mix. The weights restore each group to its population share. (The lm() standard error would also be wrong, but that doesn’t move the estimate.)

  2. Two surveys each interview 5,000 people. One samples 250 villages with 20 people each, the other 1,000 villages with 5 each. For an outcome with intra-cluster correlation 0.1, which gives the narrower confidence interval, and by roughly how much?

    Show answer

    The 1,000-village survey. Deff is about 1 + 19 × 0.1 = 2.9 versus 1 + 4 × 0.1 = 1.4, so effective samples are about 1,700 versus 3,600. The CI is about √(2.9/1.4) ≈ 1.4 times wider in the 250-village survey.

  3. A paper restricts NHANES to adults with diabetes (n = 900), builds the survey design on that file and reports subgroup estimates by ethnicity. What would you ask?

    Show answer

    Whether they subset the design rather than the data. Filtering first drops the domain-size randomness and may leave strata with a single PSU, so the standard errors are wrong, worst in the smallest ethnic groups. Ask for subset(design, diabetes == 1) and the design degrees of freedom.

  4. The weighted hazard ratio for the top Na/K quartile is 1.45; the unweighted one, adjusted for age, sex and ethnicity, is 1.15. Which do you believe?

    Show answer

    Neither yet. A gap that large suggests the association differs across groups that were sampled at different rates, for example by ethnicity or income. Fit the interaction and report group-specific estimates if it holds. The weighted estimate is the population-average one; the unweighted one depends on the sample’s mix.

  5. Your Project 3 analysis uses a urine variable from a subsample but the MEC examination weight, because “it is the main weight”. What is wrong?

    Show answer

    The least-common-denominator rule: the subsample weight is built so that the subsample represents the population. The exam weight does not account for the extra subsampling step, so estimates are biased if the subsample selection was unequal, and its replicate weights do not match.
