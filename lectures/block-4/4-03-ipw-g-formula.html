Lecture 4.3

Inverse probability weighting and the g-formula

Block 4 · Causal inference and policy evaluation
The point

Once an effect is identified (Lecture 4.1) and you have an adjustment set (Lecture 4.2), there are two classic ways to estimate it. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) models who gets treated and reweights people until treatment no longer depends on the confounders. The g-formula models the outcome and predicts what would happen to everyone under each strategy. Both give marginal effects, both extend to treatments that change over time, where ordinary regression fails, and they fail in different ways. Project 5A will use one or both, so the diagnostics here become its methods section.

1. IPW: building a pseudo-population

Weighting each person by the inverse of the probability of the treatment they actually got creates a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of the measured confounders, as if it had been randomised.

\[ W_i = \frac{1}{\Pr[A = A_i \mid L_i]} \]
What it is
A treated person whose covariates made treatment unlikely (probability 0.2) gets weight 5: they stand in for themselves and four similar people who went untreated in the real data. An untreated person with the same covariates gets weight 1/0.8 = 1.25. In the weighted data, every stratum of \(L\) contributes its full size to both arms, so a simple weighted comparison of outcomes estimates the ATE.
Why it matters
It separates the two modelling jobs: you model treatment (the propensity score) and then compare outcomes with almost no outcome model. It extends naturally to treatments that change over time (section 6), and it is the logic behind censoring weights for informative drop-out.
How to read it
Weights near 1–3 are comfortable. A handful of weights of 50 or more means a few people are carrying a large share of the analysis: the positivity warning from Lecture 4.1, in numbers.
What a change means
As treatment probabilities in a stratum move towards 0 or 1, the weights for the rare people in that stratum explode and the estimate becomes noisier, even though it stays unbiased in large samples.
Weight a population until severity no longer predicts treatment

Clinicians treat high-severity patients more often, and severity raises death. Compare the crude estimate with IPW and standardisation. Then push the high-severity treatment probability towards 95% and watch the weights for the few untreated high-severity patients grow.

Trap

Thinking the weighted analysis “has more patients”. Unstabilised weights sum to about twice the sample size, but there is no new information: standard errors must account for the weighting (robust or bootstrap), and the effective sample size is usually smaller than the real one.

Analogy

Survey weights (Lecture 3.4). A survey that under-samples young men gives each young man a larger weight so the sample looks like the population. IPW does the same, except the “population” it targets is everyone under treatment, and everyone under no treatment.

From your ML background

This is importance weighting under covariate shift. The treated arm is a sample from \(p(L \mid A=1)\); you want averages over \(p(L)\); the importance weight \(p(L)/p(L \mid A=1)\) is proportional to \(1/\Pr[A=1 \mid L]\). Everything you know about importance weighting carries over, including its failure mode: unbounded weights where the source has little support.

2. The propensity score model

The propensity model is judged by whether it balances the confounders, not by how well it predicts treatment.

What it is
A model for \(\Pr[A=1 \mid L]\), usually logistic regression with splines and the interactions clinicians would expect. The covariates are the adjustment set from your DAG. Adding pure predictors of the outcome usually helps precision; adding pure predictors of treatment (instruments) hurts (Brookhart and colleagues, 2006).
Why it matters
IPW is only as good as this model. A misspecified propensity model leaves residual imbalance, and with it residual confounding, in a way the outcome comparison cannot repair.
How to read it
Fit it, weight, then check balance on every covariate, including squares and interactions you think matter. If balance is poor, revise the model (add a spline, an interaction) and check again. The outcome is never looked at during this loop, which keeps the analysis honest.
What a change means
Switching from logistic regression to boosted trees or another ensemble can reduce bias when the true treatment model is both non-linear and non-additive (Lee, Lessler and Stuart, 2010). A flexible model that overfits can also push scores towards 0 and 1 and create extreme weights. Judge it on balance and weights, not on cross-validated accuracy.
From your ML background · Why AUC is the wrong target

A propensity model is a classifier, but its job is different. A very high AUC means treatment is nearly predictable from covariates, which is a positivity problem, not a success. The easiest way to raise AUC is to add instruments, which increases variance and amplifies residual bias (Lecture 4.2). Westreich and colleagues (2011) pointed out that a high c-statistic is neither necessary nor sufficient for controlling confounding, and that chasing it can reduce overlap. Target balance (standardised mean differences) and well-behaved weights; methods such as entropy balancing or CBPS optimise balance directly. Python users: DoWhy or econml wrap the same steps.

Trap

Putting post-treatment variables in the propensity model because they “improve the fit”. Anything measured after treatment starts (day-3 labs, transfusion) is a descendant of treatment: it predicts treatment well because treatment caused it. It belongs in neither the propensity model nor the adjustment set.

3. Stabilised weights and weight diagnostics

Before looking at any effect estimate, check three things: the weights are well-behaved, the effective sample size is reasonable, and the confounders are balanced.

What it is
Stabilised weights put the marginal probability of the treatment received in the numerator: \(\Pr[A=A_i] / \Pr[A=A_i \mid L_i]\). They have mean close to 1 and smaller spread. Truncation caps weights at a percentile (often the 1st and 99th). Balance is measured by the standardised mean difference (SMD): the difference in a covariate’s mean between arms, divided by its pooled standard deviation.
Why it matters
For a single time point, stabilising changes the variance of a saturated estimate little, but it makes problems visible: a mean stabilised weight far from 1 signals a misspecified model or positivity trouble (Cole and Hernán, 2008). For time-varying treatments and MSMs with few parameters, stabilisation genuinely improves precision.
How to read it
Report the mean, range and percentiles of the weights, and the effective sample size in each arm. An absolute SMD below 0.1 after weighting is the usual threshold for adequate balance (Austin, 2009). Show a Love plot: every covariate, SMD before and after.
What a change means
Truncating at the 99th percentile instead of not at all usually cuts the largest weights sharply and narrows the interval, at the cost of some residual confounding. If the estimate moves a lot as you tighten truncation, the result depends on a few people: report it as fragile.
Diagnose a propensity model

Clinicians here avoid anticoagulation at both very low and very high SOFA scores, a curved relationship. Fit the main-terms model and look at SOFA² in the balance plot. Add the squared term. Then make treatment more strongly driven by covariates and try truncating the weights.

library(WeightIt); library(cobalt); library(marginaleffects); library(splines)
W <- weightit(anticoag ~ ns(age, 3) + ns(sofa, 3) + bleed_risk + creat + sex,
              data = d, method = "glm", estimand = "ATE")
summary(W)                                        # weight range, effective sample size
bal.tab(W, stats = "m", thresholds = c(m = 0.1))  # SMDs before and after
love.plot(W, thresholds = c(m = 0.1))
fit <- glm_weightit(death ~ anticoag, data = d, weightit = W, family = binomial)
avg_comparisons(fit, variables = "anticoag")      # marginal risk difference, robust SE

weightit fits the propensity model (with natural splines from the splines package) and builds ATE weights. bal.tab and love.plot show balance against the 0.1 line. glm_weightit fits the weighted outcome model with standard errors that account for estimating the weights, and avg_comparisons turns it into a risk difference. trim(W, at = .99) truncates.

Trap

Using a significance test to check balance. A p-value for the difference in means depends on sample size: in a big EHR cohort a trivial imbalance is “significant”, and in a small one a large imbalance is not. Balance is a property of the sample, measured by the SMD.

4. The g-formula: simulate each world

The g-formula models the outcome, predicts every person’s outcome under “everyone treated” and again under “no one treated”, and compares the two averages.

What it is
Also called standardisation or g-computation. Fit an outcome model given treatment and confounders; set treatment to 1 for everyone and predict; set it to 0 and predict; average each set of predictions over the sample. This is the standardisation formula from Lecture 4.1, with a model filling in strata too small to estimate directly.
Why it matters
It gives a marginal effect on any scale (risk difference, risk ratio, survival at 90 days) from any outcome model, including one with interactions. It is usually more precise than IPW, because it borrows strength from the outcome model.
How to read it
The estimate is only as good as the outcome model, including where data are sparse: with poor overlap the model extrapolates silently, where IPW would at least show large weights. Use a bootstrap or a delta method for intervals.
What a change means
If IPW and the g-formula disagree, one of the two models is wrong, or overlap is poor. That disagreement is useful: it is the motivation for doubly robust estimators (Lecture 4.4).
om <- glm(death ~ anticoag * (ns(age, 3) + ns(sofa, 3) + bleed_risk + creat + sex),
          family = binomial, data = d)
avg_predictions(om, variables = "anticoag")    # standardised risk under 0 and under 1
avg_comparisons(om, variables = "anticoag")    # their difference: the ATE
avg_comparisons(om, variables = "anticoag", newdata = subset(anticoag == 1))  # ATT

The outcome model lets the effect differ by every confounder. avg_predictions predicts everyone’s risk with treatment set to each value and averages; avg_comparisons returns the difference. Restricting the averaging to the treated gives the ATT.

From your ML background

The g-formula with one model is the S-learner: a single model with treatment as a feature, queried twice. Fitting separate models for treated and untreated is the T-learner. Lecture 4.4 shows why flexible learners plugged into this formula need a correction to give valid confidence intervals.

5. Time-varying treatment: why regression fails

When a confounder is affected by earlier treatment, regression cannot both adjust for it and leave the earlier treatment’s effect intact; IPW and the g-formula can.

What it is
Treatment is decided again at each time (A0, A1, …). A covariate measured in between, L1, is caused by A0, and it influences both A1 and the outcome. The question is the effect of a strategy, such as “anticoagulate every day” versus “never”, not of a single dose.
Why it matters
Including L1 in a regression adjusts away the part of A0’s effect that runs through L1, and, if an unmeasured factor affects both L1 and the outcome, opens a collider path. Leaving L1 out leaves A1 confounded. There is no right set of regression covariates.
How to read it
Look for covariates that are updated over follow-up, drive treatment decisions and respond to treatment: CD4 count under antiretroviral therapy, blood pressure under antihypertensives, platelet count under heparin. If one exists, the analysis needs g-methods.
Example · Zidovudine and CD4 count

The classic case is HIV treatment: CD4 count predicts both starting zidovudine and death, and zidovudine raises CD4. Hernán, Brumback and Robins (2000) used it to introduce marginal structural models in epidemiology, fitted with inverse probability weights to the Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study. The same structure sits in Project 5A: platelet count and bleeding signs drive each day’s anticoagulation decision, and heparin can lower platelets.

Gulf example

Diabetes care in Saudi primary care and hospital registries has the same shape. Whether treatment is intensified at each visit depends on the latest HbA1c; earlier treatment lowered that HbA1c; and HbA1c predicts complications. A Cox model of “insulin use” adjusted for time-updated HbA1c answers no well-defined causal question. To ask “what if patients were intensified whenever HbA1c exceeded a threshold?”, use IPW for a marginal structural model or the g-formula.

6. Marginal structural models and the parametric g-formula

For time-varying treatment, IPW multiplies the weights over time to fit a marginal structural model, while the parametric g-formula simulates the whole history forward under each strategy; they rest on different models and fail differently.

What it is
A marginal structural model (MSM) is a model for the counterfactual outcome as a function of the treatment history, such as the risk of death by day 90 by number of days anticoagulated. It is fitted to data weighted by the product over time of each day’s stabilised weight, with that day’s confounders in the denominator. The parametric g-formula fits a model for every time-varying confounder and for the outcome, then simulates a cohort day by day with treatment set by the strategy, letting the confounders evolve as the models say.
Why it matters
Both answer strategy questions that regression cannot. The g-formula can also compare dynamic strategies (“anticoagulate while platelets stay above a threshold”) and gives absolute risk curves directly. IPW for MSMs needs only the treatment models, which are usually better understood than the biology of every covariate.
How to read it
For IPW, the cumulative weights are the diagnostic: their product grows over time, so check mean, spread and percentiles at each time, not just overall. For the g-formula, the check is whether the simulated “natural course” (no intervention) reproduces the observed risks and covariate trends.
What a change means
If the natural-course simulation misses the observed risk curve, the covariate or outcome models are wrong. If cumulative weights blow up after day 5, positivity is failing late in follow-up; restrict the horizon or consider grace periods (Lecture 4.5).

Wm <- weightitMSM(list(A0 ~ age + sofa0 + plt0,
                       A1 ~ age + sofa0 + plt0 + A0 + sofa1 + plt1),
                  data = wide, method = "glm", stabilize = TRUE)
summary(Wm)                                     # cumulative weights per time
fit <- glm_weightit(death ~ A0 + A1, data = wide, weightit = Wm,
                    family = binomial)
avg_predictions(fit, variables = list(A0 = 0:1, A1 = 0:1))  # risk under each history

With data in wide format (one row per patient, a column per day), weightitMSM fits one treatment model per decision point, each including the history up to then, and multiplies the stabilised weights. The outcome model is the MSM: risk of death as a function of treatment on day 0 and day 1. Comparing the predictions for (1, 1) and (0, 0) gives “always” versus “never”. For the parametric g-formula, gfoRmula::gformula takes long-format data, a model for each covariate, and a list of strategies.

Trap

Thinking that because IPW and the g-formula target the same estimand they share assumptions. They share the identifying assumptions (sequential exchangeability, positivity and consistency at every time), but their modelling assumptions are different: IPW bets on the treatment models, the g-formula bets on the covariate and outcome models. Running both and comparing is a cheap robustness check; doubly robust methods (Lecture 4.4) formalise it.

Check yourself

  1. Your propensity model for anticoagulation has an AUC of 0.91. A colleague says this is excellent. What do you check before agreeing?

    Show answer

    A high AUC means treatment is very predictable, which signals poor overlap. Check the score distributions by arm, the largest weights and the effective sample size. Check whether the model includes instruments (site, prescriber) or post-treatment variables that inflate the AUC. Then check balance with SMDs. The model is good if balance is good and weights are tame, whatever the AUC.

  2. After weighting, all SMDs are below 0.05, the largest stabilised weight is 140 and the effective sample size of the untreated arm is 9% of its real size. Is the analysis ready?

    Show answer

    No. Balance is achieved by leaning on a few untreated patients who look like the treated; the estimate will be unstable and driven by them. Investigate who they are, consider restricting the population to where both treatments were plausible, changing the estimand (ATT or overlap weights), or truncating, and report how the estimate changes.

  3. IPW gives a risk difference of −4 points and the g-formula gives −1 point, with the same covariates. What could explain this, and what do you do next?

    Show answer

    One of the models is misspecified, or overlap is poor so that IPW leans on extreme weights while the outcome model extrapolates. Check weights and balance, check the outcome model’s fit in each arm, and try flexible versions of both. A doubly robust estimator (Lecture 4.4) is the natural next step; report the disagreement rather than choosing the number you prefer.

  4. A cohort study fits a time-dependent Cox model for statin use, adjusted for time-updated LDL cholesterol, and reports a hazard ratio for death. Why is that hard to interpret, and what would you suggest?

    Show answer

    LDL drives statin starts and is lowered by earlier statin use: treatment–confounder feedback. Adjusting for time-updated LDL removes the part of the effect that works through LDL and can open collider paths, so the HR answers no strategy question. Suggest an MSM fitted with IPW, or the parametric g-formula, comparing strategies such as “start and continue” versus “never start”, or a target trial with treatment fixed at baseline (Lecture 4.5).

  5. Your g-formula’s natural-course simulation predicts a 90-day mortality of 18%, but the observed mortality is 26%. What does that tell you?

    Show answer

    The covariate or outcome models do not reproduce the data even without any intervention, so their predictions under intervention are not trustworthy. Revisit the models (functional forms, interactions, missing time-varying covariates, how death and censoring are handled) before reporting strategy comparisons.
