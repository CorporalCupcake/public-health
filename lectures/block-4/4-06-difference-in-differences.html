Lecture 4.6

Difference-in-differences, including staggered adoption and event studies

Block 4 · Causal inference and policy evaluation
The point

Difference-in-differences (DiD) estimates a policy’s effect by asking how much more the treated group changed than a comparison group did. It needs no randomisation and no measured confounders, but it trades them for one strong, untestable assumption: without the policy, the two groups would have moved in parallel. When units adopt the policy at different times, the familiar regression with unit and year fixed effects can give the wrong answer, even the wrong sign, and newer estimators fix that. Project 4 (GCC sugary-drink taxes, five adopters at three dates, one never-taxed neighbour, annual data) is a staggered DiD with very few units, so every section here becomes a choice in its protocol.

1. The 2×2 design and parallel trends

DiD subtracts the comparison group’s change from the treated group’s change; it is right only if the treated group would otherwise have changed by the same amount.

\[ \widehat{\text{DiD}} = (\bar Y_{\text{T,post}} - \bar Y_{\text{T,pre}}) - (\bar Y_{\text{C,post}} - \bar Y_{\text{C,pre}}) \]
What it is
Two groups, two periods. The first difference (before vs after) removes anything fixed about each group, such as one country always having a higher sugar supply than the other. The second difference (treated vs comparison) removes anything that hits both groups in the same year, such as a regional price shock or COVID-19. What is left is the effect, if nothing else changed differently in the treated group at the same time.
Why it matters
The identifying assumption is parallel trends: the treated group’s untreated potential outcome (Lecture 4.1) would have changed by the same amount as the comparison group’s. Levels can differ; changes cannot. The target is the ATT, the average effect on the treated units, not on everyone.
How to read it
A DiD of −4 kg per person per year means the taxed country’s supply fell 4 kg more than the comparison’s did over the same years. Ask first: what is the counterfactual line, and why should I believe it? The estimate is only as good as the answer.
What a change means
If the treated group was already drifting down 0.5 kg a year faster before the tax, a DiD averaging six post years against seven pre years absorbs about 0.5 × 6.5 ≈ 3 kg of that drift into the “effect”. Small differences in trend become large biases over long follow-up.
The 2×2 DiD explorer

Start with parallel trends: the estimate equals the truth. Then add a pre-trend gap and watch the bias grow. Finally press “Looks parallel, isn’t”: the pre-period is perfect, yet the estimate is wrong, because the trends part only after 2017.

Trap

“The pre-trends are parallel, so the assumption holds.” Parallel trends is about the post-period counterfactual, which is never observed. Parallel pre-trends are evidence that the groups move together, not proof that they kept doing so after the policy date, when a co-intervention or a shock to one group can break it. Also, parallel trends depends on the scale: if it holds in levels it cannot also hold in logs when the groups start at different levels. Choose the scale (kg or log kg) on substantive grounds before you look.

Example · The classic 2×2

Card and Krueger (1994) compared fast-food employment in New Jersey, which raised its minimum wage in 1992, with neighbouring eastern Pennsylvania, which did not, surveying the same restaurants before and after. Employment did not fall in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania. The design’s credibility rested on the argument that restaurants on either side of the border face the same economy.

Analogy

Two runners on parallel treadmills set to slightly different speeds. Give one new shoes; the shoe effect is how much further that runner pulls ahead than the gap that was already opening. If someone also nudged that runner’s treadmill speed the same day, you cannot tell shoes from speed.

2. Choosing the comparison group

The comparison group is the whole design: pick the units whose untreated path is most believable as the treated group’s, and defend it with context, not just a pre-trend plot.

What it is
Three options in a staggered rollout: never-treated units (Kuwait), not-yet-treated units (Oman and Qatar before 2019, as controls for the 2017 adopters), and external units outside the policy region (non-GCC countries). Conditional parallel trends adds covariates (GDP per person, population growth) so that trends need only be parallel among similar units.
Why it matters
Each option fails differently. Never-treated units may differ for a reason (why did Kuwait not tax?). Not-yet-treated units may change behaviour in anticipation of their own tax. External units share fewer shocks. And DiD needs no spillovers: the policy must not change the comparison group’s outcome.
How to read it
A good comparison shares the treated group’s shocks (prices, migration, economic cycle) and none of its policy. Check pre-trends on the outcome and on a few covariates, and say what would break the comparison.
What a change means
If the estimate moves from −4 to −1 when you swap Kuwait for not-yet-treated GCC states, one comparison is wrong or effects differ by comparison; report both and explain which you trust.
Gulf example · Spillovers in Project 4

Cross-border shopping (Saudis buying cheaper drinks in Kuwait or Bahrain before Bahrain’s tax) would push Kuwait’s supply up and inflate the effect. Re-exports matter for the secondary outcome: the UAE is a regional trade hub, so its Comtrade imports of sweetened drinks (HS 2202) include goods that leave again. Kuwait’s status must also be confirmed in week 46: a 2020 paper expected a tax in 2020, a 2025 report says there is still none.

Trap

Choosing the comparison by which one gives the best-looking pre-trend plot or the biggest effect. That is specification search, and pre-trends chosen this way are no longer evidence of anything (section 5). Pre-specify the comparison and the covariates, then show the alternatives as sensitivity analyses.

3. Two-way fixed effects and staggered adoption

With staggered adoption and effects that change over time, the standard regression with unit and year fixed effects uses already-treated units as controls and can badly misstate the average effect.

What it is
Two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regresses the outcome on a treated-now indicator plus a dummy for each unit and each year. In a 2×2 it equals DiD exactly. With staggered timing, Goodman-Bacon (2021) showed it is a weighted average of every possible 2×2 in the data, including forbidden comparisons: late adopters treated as “treated” and early adopters, already taxed, used as “controls”.
Why it matters
If the early adopters’ effect is still growing, their change after the late adoption date contains effect, not just trend, and subtracting it biases the late comparison. The weights also favour units treated in the middle of the panel, and some treated unit-years get negative weight (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020). With constant effects none of this matters; with dynamic effects it can flip the sign.
How to read it
Run the Goodman-Bacon decomposition (bacondecomp::bacon()): it lists each 2×2, its estimate and its weight. Much weight on “later vs earlier treated” with estimates unlike the clean ones is a red flag.
What a change means
A TWFE estimate of −2 beside clean estimates of −4 means the forbidden comparisons are pulling towards zero, the typical result when a tax’s effect builds as habits and product recipes change.
Staggered adoption: TWFE vs the truth

With a constant effect all three numbers agree. Let the effect grow and TWFE falls behind; switch off the never-taxed unit and press “Sign flip”. The lower panel shows why: long-exposed unit-years, where the effect is largest, get small or negative weight.

Trap

Believing TWFE is safe because “it controls for unit and time”. The fixed effects are fine; the problem is which comparisons the single coefficient averages and with what weights. Even with no noise and perfect parallel trends, as in the widget, TWFE can be wrong.

From your ML background

TWFE is OLS, and OLS weights observations by their leverage on the residualised regressor, not by policy relevance. After removing unit and year means, an early adopter in a late year looks “less treated than average”, so its residualised treatment can turn negative. It is the same reason a linear model fitted to heterogeneous data reports a slope no subgroup has.

4. Heterogeneity-robust estimators

The modern estimators share one idea: only ever compare treated units with units that are not treated at that moment, then average the clean comparisons with weights you choose.

EstimatorIdea in one lineR
Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021)Estimate a 2×2 for each adoption cohort and year (a group-time ATT) against never- or not-yet-treated units, then aggregatedid
Sun & Abraham (2021)An event-study regression with cohort × event-time interactions, so cohorts are never used as each other’s controlsfixest::sunab()
de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2020)Compare units that switch with units that do not, period by period; handles treatment turning on and offDIDmultiplegt
Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024)Fit unit and year effects on untreated observations only, impute each treated unit’s untreated outcome, average the differencesdidimputation
What it is
All four assume parallel trends (and no anticipation) and differ mainly in which controls they use and how they weight. With a never-treated group and balanced data they usually agree closely.
Why it matters
Reviewers now expect one of these whenever timing is staggered, plus TWFE for comparison. A large gap between them is itself a finding: effects differ across cohorts or over time.
How to read it
Report the aggregation you chose: a simple overall ATT, an event-study path (effect by years since adoption), or effects by cohort. In Project 4 the cohort view matters: the 2017 and 2019 adopters may have responded differently.
What a change means
Switching the control group from never-treated to not-yet-treated changes which units carry the counterfactual. Stable results across both are reassuring; diverging ones point to anticipation or a poor never-treated unit.
library(did)
cs <- att_gt(yname = "log_sugar", tname = "year", idname = "id",
             gname = "first_tax_year",       # 0 = never taxed (Kuwait)
             control_group = "notyettreated",
             data = p4)
aggte(cs, type = "simple")                   # one overall ATT
es <- aggte(cs, type = "dynamic")            # effect by years since tax
ggdid(es)                                    # event-study plot

att_gt estimates one 2×2 per cohort and year; idname must be a numeric country id. aggte averages them into an overall effect or an event-study path. With six countries, read its default standard errors with suspicion (section 6).

Trap

Thinking the new estimators rescue a bad comparison. They fix the weighting problem of TWFE, nothing else. If Kuwait’s trend would not have matched Saudi Arabia’s, Callaway–Sant’Anna is just as biased as TWFE.

From your ML background

The imputation estimator is “train on controls, predict the counterfactual”: fit a model of the untreated outcome on untreated cells only, predict treated cells, and average treated-minus-predicted. Synthetic control (Lecture 4.8) is the same move with a different model, and the T-learner in Lecture 4.4 is its individual-level cousin.

5. Event studies, pre-trends and anticipation

An event-study plot shows the effect for each year relative to adoption; the years before adoption are a falsification check and the years after are the effect’s path.

What it is
Coefficients for each event time (years since adoption). Leads (negative event times) estimate “effects” before the policy; lags (zero and positive) estimate effects after. One period, usually −1, is the reference and fixed at zero.
Why it matters
It shows whether the effect is immediate, builds, or fades, and whether treated and control units were already diverging. A single DiD number hides both.
How to read it
Leads should hover around zero with no drift. Lags trace the effect. Late lags rest on fewer cohorts (only the 2017 adopters reach five years after), so their intervals widen and their composition changes.
What a change means
Leads drifting steadily downward towards −1 suggest a pre-existing trend or anticipation, and the lags inherit it. A spike at −1 then a drop at 0 is the classic stockpiling signature.
Trap

“The leads are not significant, so there is no pre-trend.” With six countries, pre-trend tests have little power, and Roth (2022) showed that keeping only studies that pass them can make the remaining bias worse. Better: ask how large a violation would overturn the result. Rambachan and Roth’s HonestDiD reports the effect’s interval when post-period trends may differ by up to some multiple of the worst pre-period difference. Also, TWFE event studies with staggered timing contaminate each lead with other periods’ effects (Sun and Abraham 2021): use a robust estimator for the plot too.

Example · Anticipation

An excise tax is announced months before it starts, so retailers and households can stock up just before and buy less just after. Annual supply then rises in the last untaxed year and dips in the first taxed one, exaggerating the immediate effect. Fix it by making the reference period the last year before the announcement (shift the base back with anticipation = 1 in att_gt), and treat the first year’s drop with caution.

6. Inference with few clusters

Policy varies by country, so errors must be clustered by country; with six countries the usual cluster-robust standard errors are far too small, and even the fixes are fragile.

What it is
Outcomes within a country are correlated over years, so the effective sample is closer to the number of countries than to country-years. Cluster-robust standard errors handle this but need many clusters: they are known to under-cover with fewer than roughly 30–50 (Cameron and Miller 2015). The wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller 2008) resamples by flipping the signs of whole clusters’ residuals and does much better with few clusters.
Why it matters
Project 4 has six GCC countries, five treated and one never treated. A conventional clustered p-value of 0.01 could easily be 0.2 in truth. With very few treated or very few control clusters, MacKinnon and Webb (2017) showed even the wild bootstrap can over- or under-reject badly.
How to read it
With six clusters, sign flips allow only \(2^6 = 64\) distinct bootstrap samples, so the p-value is coarse; Webb’s six-point weights give more. Report the bootstrap p-value and its weight type, and treat it as one strand of evidence.
What a change means
Adding non-GCC comparison countries raises the number of clusters (better inference) but weakens parallel trends (worse identification). That trade is the real decision, and it is why Project 4 also uses synthetic control with permutation inference (Lecture 4.8).
library(fwildclusterboot)   # archived from CRAN in 2024: install from GitHub
fit <- lm(log_sugar ~ taxed + factor(country) + factor(year), data = p4)
boottest(fit, param = "taxed", clustid = "country",
         B = 9999, type = "webb")   # Webb weights for very few clusters

This bootstraps the TWFE coefficient by country, imposing the null hypothesis, which behaves best with few clusters. It does not fix TWFE’s weighting problem (section 3); use it to show how much the conventional standard error understates uncertainty, and pair it with permutation tests.

Trap

Clustering by country-year or not clustering at all because “there are 78 observations”. Thirteen years of six countries is not 78 independent pieces of information about the tax. The tax varies across six countries, and that is your sample size for the policy question.

Analogy

Tasting six pots of soup, a spoonful a day for thirteen days. You have 78 spoonfuls, but if you want to know whether a new spice works, you have six pots.

7. Project 4 as a staggered DiD

Write the design as a grid of countries by years before you write any code: it shows the cohorts, the clean comparisons, the forbidden ones and the awkward cells.

What it is
Units: six GCC countries (plus optional non-GCC countries). Time: years in FAOSTAT’s food balance sheets. Outcome: sugar and sweetener supply per person (log scale if you expect proportional effects). Cohorts from Curriculum.md: Saudi Arabia and the UAE in 2017, Bahrain late in 2017, Oman and Qatar in 2019; Kuwait never.
Why it matters
Annual data with mid-year adoption means partly treated years. Saudi Arabia’s tax started in June 2017 and the UAE’s in October 2017, so 2017 is part-treated for both, and Bahrain’s 2017 is almost wholly untaxed. The December 2019 widening to all sweetened drinks in Saudi Arabia and the UAE is a second dose that a simple on/off coding ignores.
How to read it
Pre-specify the coding: first full taxed year as adoption (2018 for the 2017 adopters), or drop part-treated years. Show the other as a sensitivity analysis. Treat 2020 as a common shock that year effects absorb only if COVID-19 hit all six similarly.
What a change means
If moving adoption from 2017 to 2018 changes the estimate a lot, the first-year effect (or anticipation) is doing the work; report the event-study path rather than a single number.
Gulf example · A defensible primary analysis

A reasonable protocol: Callaway–Sant’Anna with not-yet-treated and never-treated controls, adoption coded as the first full taxed year, log supply per person, an event-study plot with the year before the announcement as reference, wild cluster bootstrap and permutation p-values, and synthetic control for Saudi Arabia (the largest market) as a separate strand. Report TWFE only to show how it differs.

Trap

Using FAOSTAT years before 2010 to lengthen the pre-period. The food balance sheets were recompiled with a new method from 2010 onward, and the old-method series (to 2013) does not match it where they overlap. Splicing them puts a measurement break inside your pre-period (Lecture 4.7 returns to this).

Check yourself

  1. A colleague shows an event-study plot for a city’s soda tax: all leads are small and not significant, so they write “parallel trends is confirmed”. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Parallel trends concerns the post-period counterfactual, which is never observed; flat leads are supportive evidence, not confirmation. With few units the leads have little power to detect a modest pre-trend. Ask how large a trend violation would overturn the result (HonestDiD-style sensitivity), and whether anything else changed in the city when the tax began.

  2. In a staggered rollout, TWFE gives an effect of −0.5 and Callaway–Sant’Anna gives −3.0. Which do you report, and what does the gap tell you?

    Show answer

    Report the heterogeneity-robust estimate as primary, and TWFE alongside. The gap says effects differ across cohorts or grow over time, so TWFE’s forbidden comparisons (earlier adopters as controls) are pulling it towards zero. A Goodman-Bacon decomposition would show how much weight sits on those comparisons. Show the event-study path, since the effect clearly is not one number.

  3. Your DiD has six countries. The conventional cluster-robust p-value is 0.004; the wild cluster bootstrap p-value is 0.16. How do you write this up?

    Show answer

    Lead with the bootstrap result and explain why: conventional clustered standard errors are badly too small with six clusters. Say the data are compatible with no effect at conventional thresholds, give the point estimate and the event-study pattern, and add permutation and synthetic-control evidence. Do not report 0.004 as the main inference.

  4. For Project 4, should Oman and Qatar serve as controls for Saudi Arabia in 2018? In 2020?

    Show answer

    In 2018, yes, as not-yet-treated units, provided they were not already changing behaviour in anticipation of their own 2019 tax. In 2020, no: they are taxed by then, so they are no longer valid untreated comparisons. Clean estimators make exactly this switch automatically.

  5. Supply rose sharply in Saudi Arabia in 2016 and fell in 2017. Your DiD uses 2016 as the reference year. What worries you?

    Show answer

    Anticipation (stockpiling before an announced tax) would make 2016 an unusually high baseline, inflating the estimated drop. Check when the tax was announced, use an earlier reference year (the last year before announcement), and compare with a version that averages several pre-years. Also check whether 2016 is a data artefact, such as a revision in the food balance sheets.
