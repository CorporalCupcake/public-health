Lecture 4.7

Interrupted time series

Block 4 · Causal inference and policy evaluation
The point

Interrupted time series (ITS) uses one population’s own past as its control: fit the trend before a policy, carry it forward, and read the effect as the gap between what happened and that projection. It is the standard design for national policies with no untreated comparison. Lecture 3.9 gave you the machinery (segmented regression, season, autocorrelation); this lecture is about the judgement around it: which impact shape to expect before you look, how honest your intervals are, what else could have bent the line, and why Project 4’s seven annual pre-tax points make ITS a supporting strand rather than the main analysis.

1. The counterfactual is a projected trend

ITS assumes that, without the policy, the pre-intervention trend (and season) would have continued; the effect is the distance between the observed series and that projection.

What it is
Segmented regression (Lecture 3.9) fits a pre-period line, then lets the level and the slope change at the intervention. The level change is the immediate step; the slope change is how much faster or slower the series moves afterwards. The extended pre-period line is the counterfactual.
Why it matters
Every causal claim from ITS rests on that projection. Its assumption is the time-series cousin of parallel trends (Lecture 4.6): nothing except the policy changed the series’ path at that moment.
How to read it
Report the effect at a stated horizon, combining both terms, with its interval, in absolute and relative form (“3.1 kg per person lower, 7% below the projection, five years after”). Do not report the level and slope coefficients as two separate effects.
What a change means
A level change of −2 with a slope change of +0.5 per year means an initial drop that erodes: by year 4 the series is back on its old path. The two coefficients only make sense together.
\[ \text{effect } k \text{ periods after} = \beta_2 + \beta_3\, k \]

Here \(\beta_2\) is the level change and \(\beta_3\) the slope change, as in Lecture 3.9. The interval for this sum needs the covariance of the two estimates; in R, marginaleffects or a linear combination with multcomp::glht gives it.

Trap

Projecting the pre-trend far into the future. A linear trend fitted over seven years and carried forward ten says more about the straight-line assumption than about the policy: no trend in sugar supply goes on forever. Keep the post-period short relative to the pre-period, or report effects only at horizons the data can support.

Analogy

Dead reckoning on a ship: from speed and heading you can say where the ship should be in an hour. If it turns up somewhere else, something pushed it, but the further ahead you project, the less you can tell a push from a small error in the heading.

2. Choose the impact model before you look

Decide what shape the effect should take, and when it should start, from how the policy works, then fit that model; picking the shape that fits best is a hidden multiple-testing problem.

What it is
The impact model is the hypothesised shape of the effect: a step, a change in slope, both, a temporary dip, a delayed start, or a gradual phase-in to a new level. Lopez Bernal, Cummins and Gasparrini (2017) make choosing it a priori, from theory and earlier evidence, an explicit step of their tutorial, and discourage selecting it from the outcome data.
Why it matters
Each shape answers a different question and has different power. With six candidate shapes and two or three possible start dates, one of them will fit a noisy series “significantly” by chance.
How to read it
A good protocol states the shape, the start date, any transition period (excluded or modelled as a ramp), and the mechanism behind them. Other shapes become sensitivity analyses, labelled as such.
What a change means
Moving the start by one period, or switching from a step to a ramp, should change the estimate little if the effect is real and well timed. Large swings mean the data cannot tell the shapes apart.
Gulf example · Writing the impact model for Project 4

An excise tax raises shelf prices quickly, so the core hypothesis is a level change, with a possible slope change as habits and recipes adjust. Annual data cannot see a ramp within a year, so the practical choice is about the first year: Saudi Arabia’s tax began in June 2017, so 2017 is a transition year. Pre-specify either excluding it or coding it as part-exposed (about 0.55 of a year), and pre-specify how the December 2019 widening enters (a second level change from 2020, or a sensitivity analysis).

Trap

Looking at the plot, seeing the drop start a year early, and moving the intervention date to match. That turns an anticipation effect (or noise) into the policy effect and invalidates the p-value. If anticipation is plausible (an announced tax), say so in advance and model an announcement date as well.

3. Honest intervals: season and autocorrelation in ITS

In ITS, autocorrelation makes the level and slope changes look more certain than they are, and the usual fixes need more points than most policy series have.

What it is
The tools are those of Lecture 3.9: Fourier or month terms for season, and for autocorrelation either Newey–West standard errors, generalised least squares with AR(1) errors (Prais–Winsten), or regression with ARIMA errors. What is ITS-specific is where they bite.
Why it matters
Positive autocorrelation lets a series wander, and a wander that happens to start near the intervention looks like a level or slope change. The slope change is hit hardest because it is estimated from the trend of a short segment. With an uneven split of seasons (say the post-period starts every year in a low month), season alone creates a level change.
How to read it
Check residual ACF and PACF plots after fitting season. With many points, report autocorrelation-aware intervals and say how. With few points, say plainly that autocorrelation cannot be estimated well, so even the corrected intervals are optimistic.
What a change means
If the interval for the level change roughly doubles when you switch from OLS to Newey–West or GLS, the naive result was overstated; a result that is only significant with naive errors is not a finding.
ITS builder

Start with the monthly preset and compare the naive and Newey–West intervals. Then try “No effect, strong memory” and draw new series: watch fake steps appear. Finally choose the annual preset: with 12 points, both intervals are unstable and the real coverage of the naive one drops.

library(nlme)
d$post <- as.numeric(d$time >= t0)          # 0 before, 1 after
d$time_after <- d$post * (d$time - t0)      # periods since the policy
m <- gls(sugar ~ time + post + time_after,
         correlation = corAR1(form = ~ time), data = d)
summary(m)          # post = level change, time_after = slope change
intervals(m)        # CIs, plus the estimated AR(1) coefficient

This fits segmented regression with AR(1) errors by generalised least squares, so the autocorrelation is modelled rather than ignored. With monthly data, add season terms as in Lecture 3.9. The estimated AR coefficient from a short series is itself very uncertain: look at its interval.

Trap

“The Durbin–Watson test found no autocorrelation.” With 12 annual points the test has almost no power, so a non-significant result is no reassurance. Absence of evidence again (Lecture 2.5). Assume some autocorrelation, and show how results change across plausible values.

From your ML background

The naive interval assumes each point is a fresh draw. With φ = 0.5, ten consecutive points carry the information of roughly three independent ones: it is the same reason you cannot shuffle a time series into random cross-validation folds. Newey–West is a kernel estimate of the long-run variance, and like any kernel estimate it needs data well beyond the bandwidth to work.

4. Adding a control: comparison series and controlled ITS

A comparison series that the policy should not affect, but that shares the same shocks, removes much of what a single series cannot rule out.

What it is
Two kinds of control. A control population: the same outcome in an untaxed place (Kuwait). A control outcome: an outcome in the same place that the policy should not touch but that shares the same shocks (a food group outside the tax). Controlled ITS fits both series together and estimates how much more the treated series’ level and slope changed than the control’s.
Why it matters
A single series cannot separate the policy from anything else that happened at the same time. A control that moves in the same way at the policy date flags a shared shock; one that does not makes the policy explanation stronger.
How to read it
Unlike DiD, controlled ITS lets the two series have different pre-trends and compares the changes in level and slope. That is more flexible, but it needs enough pre-points to estimate two trends, and it still assumes the gap between the series would have continued on its own path.
What a change means
If Saudi supply drops by 3 kg in 2018 and Kuwait’s drops by 2 kg, the controlled estimate is about −1 kg, not −3: two-thirds of the apparent effect was shared.
d2 <- rbind(transform(saudi, taxed = 1), transform(kuwait, taxed = 0))
m2 <- lm(sugar ~ (time + post + time_after) * taxed, data = d2)
coef(m2)[c("post:taxed", "time_after:taxed")]   # extra level / slope change

Each term is allowed to differ between the series; the two interactions are the controlled level and slope changes. For Kuwait, post and time_after use Saudi Arabia’s dates. The standard errors still need the autocorrelation treatment from section 3.

Example · Why controls matter

Lopez Bernal, Cummins and Gasparrini (2018) set out the main control options for ITS (control populations, control outcomes, and multiple-baseline designs where the policy starts at different times in different places) and the bias each one protects against. Their key point: a control is only useful if it shares the threats you are worried about, such as the same economic shock or the same data-system change.

Trap

Choosing a control outcome that the policy can reach indirectly. If people switch from taxed drinks to sweets, a “sugar from other foods” control rises because of the tax, and the controlled estimate overstates the effect on total sugar. Name the substitution routes before choosing.

5. Threats that bend the line

The main threat to ITS is anything else that changed the series at about the same time; list the candidates for your policy date before you look at the result.

ThreatWhat it doesProject 4 candidate
History and co-interventionsAnother event or policy starts near the same date and moves the outcomeVAT of 5% introduced in Saudi Arabia and the UAE in January 2018; Saudi VAT raised to 15% in July 2020; COVID-19 in 2020
Change in measurementThe way the outcome is counted changes, creating a stepFAOSTAT food balance sheets: new method from 2010; population denominators revised between releases
Regression to the meanPolicies follow unusually bad years; the next year looks better anywayA tax adopted after a spike in imports would show a fall regardless
Change in the populationWho is counted changes, so per-person values shiftLarge swings in expatriate populations change both consumption patterns and denominators
What it is
These are the classic threats to quasi-experiments (Shadish, Cook and Campbell). ITS rules out slow, steady confounders, because they are part of the pre-trend. It does not rule out anything that changes abruptly near the policy date.
Why it matters
Reviewers will ask, “what else happened in 2017–2018?” A methods section that names the co-interventions and shows how each was handled answers before they ask.
How to read it
For each threat, ask whether its timing matches the policy and whether it would also move the control series. If it matches and the control does not share it, it is a live alternative explanation.
What a change means
A co-intervention one year after the policy is separable with monthly data and nearly inseparable with annual data. Coarser time means more threats coincide.
Trap

Treating 2018 as clean post-tax evidence for Saudi Arabia. VAT arrived in January 2018, on top of the excise tax, and raised drink prices again. A level change from 2018 measures excise plus VAT plus anything else that year. Kuwait, without VAT, cannot separate them either; a non-sugar food group under the same VAT can help.

Analogy

You start a new diet the same week you start a new job with a long commute. Your weight drops. The scale cannot tell you which change did it; only something that changed with one but not the other can.

6. Annual data and few points: Project 4

With seven annual points before the tax and a handful after, ITS can detect only large effects, cannot estimate autocorrelation, and is exposed to every co-intervention in the same year; use it as a transparent descriptive strand.

What it is
FAOSTAT’s food balance sheets are annual, and the series compiled with the current method starts in 2010. For Saudi Arabia that gives 2010–2016 as clean pre-tax years (seven points), a part-exposed 2017, and post years that also contain VAT, the 2019 widening and COVID-19.
Why it matters
Power depends on the number of points, their split before and after, noise, effect size and autocorrelation. Cochrane EPOC’s minimum of three points each side is an inclusion floor for reviews, not a guarantee of power. Lopez Bernal and colleagues give no fixed minimum and recommend simulation-based power calculations in advance (Zhang and colleagues 2011).
How to read it
Before fitting, simulate: with your expected noise and plausible autocorrelation, how large a level change would you detect 80% of the time? If the answer is larger than any plausible tax effect, say so and present ITS as description.
What a change means
Going from 7 to 14 pre-points (by splicing the old FAOSTAT series) seems to double the information, but it imports a measurement break into the pre-period, which can bias the trend more than the extra points help.
sim_p <- function(n_pre = 7, n_post = 5, level = -2, sd = 1.5, phi = 0.3) {
  t <- seq(-n_pre, n_post - 1); post <- as.numeric(t >= 0)
  e <- arima.sim(list(ar = phi), n = length(t), sd = sd * sqrt(1 - phi^2))
  y <- 50 + 0.2 * t + level * post + e
  summary(lm(y ~ t + post + I(post * t)))$coef["post", 4]
}
mean(replicate(2000, sim_p()) < 0.05)   # share of runs that detect the drop

The function simulates one annual series with a pre-trend, a level drop and AR(1) noise, fits segmented regression, and returns the p-value for the level change. The last line estimates power. Plug in noise estimated from the pre-period of your real series, and try a few values of phi.

Gulf example · More points from other sources

UN Comtrade can report trade monthly for some countries; check whether monthly HS 2202 imports exist for each GCC country. Monthly data would allow seasonality, Ramadan terms (Lecture 3.9) and a better-timed start, at the cost of trade-data noise and re-exports. Keep supply as the primary outcome, since imports miss Saudi Arabia’s large domestic bottling.

Trap

Reporting “no significant effect” from a seven-point ITS as evidence the tax did not work. The design may simply have had no power. Report the estimate with its interval, the minimum detectable effect from your simulation, and let the DiD (Lecture 4.6) and synthetic control (Lecture 4.8) strands carry the inference.

Check yourself

  1. An ITS of monthly admissions reports a significant level change of −8% and a slope change of +0.6% per month. A press release says “admissions fell 8%”. What is the right summary?

    Show answer

    The drop erodes: +0.6% a month cancels −8% in about 13 months. The effect should be reported at stated horizons (for example −8% at month 0, about −1% at month 12, nothing after), with intervals for each combined estimate. “Fell 8%” describes only the first month.

  2. After seeing the data, an analyst moves the intervention date six months earlier because “the change clearly started then”. What is wrong, and what would be acceptable?

    Show answer

    Choosing the break from the outcome data finds the biggest apparent change, so the p-value and estimate are biased. If anticipation (an announcement) was plausible, that should have been pre-specified with its own date. Acceptable: keep the pre-specified date and report the alternative as a labelled post hoc sensitivity analysis.

  3. A 7-point pre-period ITS for Saudi Arabia shows a level change of −2.5 kg (95% CI −4.0 to −1.0) with ordinary least squares. How confident are you?

    Show answer

    Not very. With so few points, any autocorrelation makes the naive interval too narrow, and it cannot be estimated reliably. The 2018 post-period also includes the new VAT. I would want a simulation-based sense of power and coverage, a control series (Kuwait, and a non-taxed food group), and agreement with the DiD and synthetic control strands before treating −2.5 as the tax’s effect.

  4. Controlled ITS with Kuwait gives a smaller effect than the single-series ITS for Saudi Arabia. Which do you believe, and why might they differ?

    Show answer

    The controlled version, if Kuwait shares the region’s shocks and was not affected by the tax. The difference is the part of Saudi Arabia’s change that Kuwait also showed: a common shock such as prices, migration or COVID-19. Check for spillovers (cross-border buying would raise Kuwait’s supply and inflate, not shrink, the controlled effect) and for Kuwait-specific events.
