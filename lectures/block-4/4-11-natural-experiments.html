Lecture 4.11

Natural experiments in public health policy

Block 4 · Causal inference and policy evaluation
The point

Most policies are never randomised, but many are rolled out in ways that create an as-if random contrast: a date, a border, a threshold, a staggered start. A natural-experiment evaluation finds that contrast, picks the design that matches it (the DiD, ITS, synthetic control, RD and IV of Lectures 4.6–4.9), and then attacks its own result with the threats that design is weakest against. This lecture ties the block together into one decision, and turns it on the policies that matter for you: Project 4’s sugary-drink taxes, Saudi salt limits and menu calorie labelling.

1. What makes an experiment “natural”

A natural experiment is an event the researcher did not control that splits people into exposed and unexposed groups in a way unrelated to their outcomes; “natural” describes who assigned the exposure, not how good the evidence is.

What it is
Exposure is set by a law, a start date, a boundary, an eligibility rule or a supply shock rather than by individual choice. The question is always the same: what process decided who was exposed, and when? If that process is unrelated to the outcome’s other causes, the comparison behaves like a trial.
Why it matters
Population policies (taxes, bans, labelling rules) cannot be randomised, and their effects per person are small but spread over millions. The UK Medical Research Council’s guidance (Craig and colleagues, 2012) argues natural experiments are worth evaluating when the population affected is large, the likely effect is important, and there is real uncertainty about its size.
How to read it
A strong study describes the assignment process in detail, shows why it is unrelated to outcomes, and tests that claim. A weak one says “we used a natural experiment” and then runs a before–after comparison.
What a change means
Moving from “admissions fell after the ban” to “admissions fell 2.4% more than the pre-ban trend predicted” is the move from description to a counterfactual design. Most of the drop in a raw before–after number is often trend.

The MRC guidance (2012) set out when and how to use natural experiments. The same team’s 2017 review (Craig, Katikireddi, Leyland and Popham) set out the design menu used in this lecture, from regression adjustment and propensity scores to DiD, ITS, synthetic control, IV and RD. A full MRC/NIHR framework updated the guidance in 2025 (Craig and colleagues). It adds a broader definition, a design-by-design assessment of likely biases, and a role for qualitative, mixed-methods and economic evaluation.

Trap

Reading “natural experiment” as a quality label. The phrase only says nobody planned the exposure. A policy targeted at the places with the worst trends (which is common: governments act where problems are growing) is the opposite of as-if random.

Analogy

A road closed by a landslide is a natural experiment on traffic. It tells you something only if the closure had nothing to do with traffic levels. A road closed because it was congested tells you nothing about what closing roads does.

2. Match the design to the source of variation

Each design exploits one kind of variation, so the choice is made by how the policy was assigned and what data exist, not by which method you like.

What it is
A cut-off gives RD. Something that nudges uptake gives IV. Unexposed comparison units give DiD, or synthetic control when there are only one or a few exposed units and a long pre-period. A single exposed series with a long history gives ITS.
Why it matters
Every design swaps the untestable “no unmeasured confounding” for a different untestable assumption. Choosing the design means choosing which assumption you can best defend for this policy.
How to read it
In a paper, check that the design fits the assignment. An ITS of a policy that started on different dates in different regions throws away its best variation; a DiD with one treated country and clustered standard errors overstates its precision.

Design chooser

Pick an example or answer the questions for your own policy. You get the designs that fit, the assumption each rests on, the main threat for this policy, and the lecture to revisit.

Trap

Choosing the design first and the policy second, for example running synthetic control on everything because it is fashionable. Synthetic control on a policy with hundreds of exposed regions, or ITS on a policy with a perfectly good unexposed comparison group, wastes the variation that would have made the result credible.

3. Two classic evaluations and what they teach

The best-known public health natural experiments show that the design and data source change the size of the answer, and that even strong evaluations get revised.

Smoke-free laws
After Scotland’s smoke-free law (March 2006), Pell and colleagues (2008) found admissions for acute coronary syndrome fell 17% in nine hospitals, against 4% in England, which had no law yet. When England’s law came in (July 2007), Sims and colleagues (2010) ran an ITS on national hospital data with five years of pre-law trend. They found a 2.4% fall in emergency heart-attack admissions, about 1,200 fewer in the first year.
Why the gap
A before–after comparison in selected hospitals versus a national series that models the existing downward trend and seasonality. The authors also pointed to lower second-hand smoke exposure in England before its law. Both found a benefit; the national ITS is the more conservative design.
UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy
Announced in March 2016, in force from April 2018, charged on drinks with at least 5 g of sugar per 100 mL, with a higher rate from 8 g. A controlled ITS (Scarborough and colleagues, 2020) showed manufacturers cut sugar to get under the bands. ITS studies then linked the levy to an 8% relative fall in obesity in girls aged 10–11 (none in boys or younger children) and a 12% fall in hospital admissions for children’s tooth extractions for decay (Rogers and colleagues).
Example · A correction worth knowing

A 2021 BMJ paper reported that the levy cut sugar bought in soft drinks by 10% per household. The authors later found an error in their analysis. The paper was retracted and a corrected version in BMJ Open (2023) puts the fall at about 3% (8 g per household per week). The authors did the right thing, but the 10% figure still circulates. Check for corrections before citing, and publish your code so errors are found early.

Trap

Reading the SDIL obesity result as settled. It comes from a single-country ITS: there is no unexposed comparison, so any other change around 2018 that affected girls’ weight would be absorbed into the estimate. The authors say this themselves. The finding that only girls benefited is a pattern to explain, not a design check.

4. GCC policies: opportunities and data

The Gulf has run several ambitious food policies since 2017, but the binding constraint on evaluating them is outcome data, not methods.

Why it matters
Earlier evaluations of the GCC drink taxes mostly compared year-on-year growth in proprietary industry sales data, with no counterfactual. Saudi Arabia’s 50% tax on carbonated drinks was described as among the largest sugary-drink taxes anywhere, so a credible estimate would matter well beyond the region.
How to read it
For calorie labelling, the published evidence is thin: one early study compared intake a few months before and after enforcement and found no significant change, and a 2023 audit found 43% of “healthy food” restaurants on a delivery app were not complying. Short before–after windows and partial compliance both bias towards finding nothing.
GCC angle · Project 4’s co-intervention

Saudi Arabia and the UAE introduced 5% VAT in January 2018, months after their drink taxes, and Saudi Arabia raised VAT to 15% in July 2020. Any price-sensitive outcome moves with these. That is a reason to look at drinks relative to other foods (VAT hit both, the excise tax only drinks) and to check whether the estimated effect jumps at VAT dates rather than at tax dates.

From your ML background

Where official series don’t exist, ML can build them: scraping menu and price data from delivery apps, classifying products by sugar band from ingredient text, or extracting calorie labels from menu images. This creates outcome and compliance series that a later ITS or DiD can use. Start collecting before the next policy, because no one can scrape the past.

Trap

Running an ITS on modelled estimates, such as GBD sodium intake, to evaluate the salt limit. Modelled series are smoothed and partly driven by covariates, and most do not include any data from after the policy. Any “effect” found is the model’s assumptions, not evidence.

5. Timing: announcements, anticipation, pre-registration

A policy starts affecting behaviour when people learn about it, not when it legally starts, so the “intervention date” is a choice you must justify and fix before seeing the results.

What it is
Anticipation: firms reformulate, consumers stockpile or retailers change prices between announcement and start. Placing the break at the start date then either misses the effect or counts it as part of the pre-trend, biasing towards zero.
Why it matters
For the SDIL, an analysis of sales data found most of the fall in sugar from reformulation happened in the two years between announcement and start (Dickson and colleagues, 2023). An ITS with the break at April 2018 would see little.
How to read it
Good studies define the announcement and start dates in advance, model both (or exclude the window between them), and show event-study leads (Lecture 4.6). A dip in the leads is anticipation, or a sign the comparison is wrong.
d$t     <- seq_len(nrow(d))                      # monthly time index
d$ann   <- d$month >= as.Date("2016-03-01")      # after announcement
d$start <- d$month >= as.Date("2018-04-01")      # after levy starts
t_a <- min(d$t[d$ann]); t_s <- min(d$t[d$start])
fit <- glm(sugar ~ t + ann + I((t - t_a) * ann) + start + I((t - t_s) * start) +
             factor(cal_month), family = quasipoisson, data = d)
summary(fit)   # level and slope changes at each date

A segmented ITS (Lecture 4.7) with two breaks: ann and start give level changes, the I(...) terms give slope changes after each date, and calendar-month dummies absorb seasonality. Fix both dates in your protocol before fitting.

Pre-registration
Write down, before looking at post-policy outcomes, the dates, outcomes, comparison or donor units, and the main model, and post it (for example on OSF). Public data already exist, so it isn’t blinding; it limits the choices you could make after seeing results (the “garden of forking paths” of Lecture 3.1). For Project 4, register before downloading post-2017 outcomes.
Trap

Picking the break date that gives the biggest effect, or moving it “because the effect seems to start earlier”. Data-chosen breaks find effects in noise. If you suspect anticipation, say so in the protocol and test the announcement date as a pre-specified alternative.

6. Spillovers, co-interventions and triangulation

The three threats that sink most policy evaluations are effects leaking into the comparison group, other changes at the same time, and trusting a single design; triangulation across designs is the main defence.

Spillovers
The policy changes the comparison units too: shoppers cross a border to buy untaxed drinks, firms reformulate for a whole region, a national media debate reaches untaxed neighbours. Spillovers break the “no interference” part of consistency (Lecture 4.1) and usually bias towards zero, or double-count if comparison sales rise.
Co-interventions
Policies come in packages. A single-series ITS cannot separate a tax from a simultaneous VAT rise or campaign; a comparison unit exposed to the other change but not the policy can. Negative-control outcomes help: an outcome the policy can’t affect but the co-intervention or confounding would (for example, untaxed food sales).
Triangulation
Run designs whose biases point in different directions or come from different sources: DiD against Kuwait, synthetic control from non-GCC donors, ITS within each country, and different outcomes (supply, imports). If they agree, no single bias is likely to explain them all (Lawlor, Tilling and Davey Smith, 2016).
Reviewers askA convincing answer
How was exposure assigned, and could that relate to outcomes?A documented assignment process; balance or pre-trend evidence
Does the design fit the variation?The choice argued from the decision tree, not from habit

From your ML background

ITS and synthetic control are forecasting problems: predict the counterfactual series, then compare. Bayesian structural time-series (Google’s CausalImpact) does this with a flexible model. Treat it like any forecaster: backtest by pretending the policy started earlier (a placebo in time) and check the error. A model that can’t predict the pre-period can’t be trusted after it.

Trap

Calling it triangulation when three analyses share the same weakness, for example three ITS models on the same national series with different smoothers. Triangulation needs designs with different key assumptions, so a single failure can’t produce agreement.

Check yourself

  1. A city taxes sugary drinks; its neighbour doesn’t. Sales fall 20% in the taxed city and rise 5% in the neighbour. A report calls the tax effect a 25% fall. What is wrong?

    Show answer

    The rise in the neighbour is probably cross-border shopping, a spillover caused by the tax. Using the neighbour as the comparison counts the diverted sales twice. Some of the 20% fall is also diversion, not reduced drinking. Use a comparison area too far away for shoppers to cross, and look at combined sales across both cities.

  2. A national policy starts in the same month as a large media campaign on the same topic. You have six years of monthly data before. What can an ITS tell you?

    Show answer

    Only the combined effect of the policy and campaign. A single series can’t separate two changes on the same date. To isolate the policy, you need a comparison unit that saw the campaign but not the policy, or an outcome the campaign would affect but the policy couldn’t (or the reverse). Otherwise report it honestly as a package effect.

  3. For the same eligibility rule, an RD and a DiD give different estimates. Does one have to be wrong?

    Show answer

    No. They estimate different things: the RD gives the effect for people near the cut-off; the DiD gives the average effect in the exposed group, which includes people far from it. Effects that differ by distance from the cut-off would make both right. Check which population each describes before calling it a contradiction.

  4. A reviewer asks why you pre-registered Project 4 when FAOSTAT data were already public. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Registration fixed the tax dates, outcomes, donor pool and model before I looked at post-tax data. With few countries and many reasonable choices, results can be tuned by those choices. The registration shows which analysis was planned and which were added later. It isn’t about blinding the data; it’s about limiting choices made after seeing results.

  5. A colleague wants to evaluate the Saudi bread salt limit with an ITS on yearly GBD estimates of sodium intake. Your view?

    Show answer

    It won’t work. GBD intake estimates are modelled, smoothed and partly driven by covariates, and probably contain no Saudi measurements from after 2019. Any break would come from the model, not from behaviour. Better options: predict the impact with a simulation model (Project 1), pool measured intake (Project 2), and push for a 24-hour urine survey that could serve as a baseline before the next limit.
